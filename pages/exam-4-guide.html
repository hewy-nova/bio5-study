The big picture

This unit is the body's delivery and maintenance systems. Blood is the delivery fluid. The heart and vessels move it. The lymphatic and immune systems guard it. The lungs load oxygen into it and unload CO₂. The kidneys clean it and set its water, salt and pH. Reproduction and development make the next body. Most exam questions test one of two things: the order things happen in (a pathway, a sequence of steps) or which structure or hormone does which job.

1. Blood (Ch. 11, Lab 12)

What it is. Blood is a connective tissue: cells floating in a liquid matrix.

  • ★ 55% plasma (water, proteins, nutrients, wastes, salts) and 45% formed elements (the cells).
  • All the formed elements come from stem cells in red bone marrow.

★ What each cell type does:

CellJob
Red blood cellsCarry O₂ on hemoglobin; no nucleus; live about 120 days
White blood cellsDefense. Neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages eat invaders; eosinophils fight parasites; basophils release histamine; lymphocytes (B and T) make the targeted response
PlateletsCell fragments that plug leaks and start clotting

★ Blood types. Your type is the antigens on your red cells (A, B, Rh). Your plasma carries antibodies against the antigens you lack. A transfusion is safe only if the recipient's antibodies don't attack the donor's cells. Type O has no A or B antigens; type AB has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies.

★ How a clot forms. Three stages:

  1. The vessel constricts.
  2. Platelets stick and form a plug.
  3. A fibrin clot forms:
    • The platelets and the damaged tissue make prothrombin activator.
    • It turns prothrombin into thrombin.
    • Thrombin turns fibrinogen into fibrin strands, which trap cells into a clot.

The liver needs vitamin K to make clotting factors.

★ Anemia and leukemia. Anemia means too little oxygen-carrying capacity.

TypeCauseTreatment
Iron-deficiencyToo little iron, so too little hemoglobinIron
PerniciousNo B₁₂ absorbed (missing intrinsic factor)B₁₂ shots
Hemolytic, including sickle-cellRed cells burstDepends on the cause

Leukemia is cancer of the white cells: huge numbers of them, but they don't work.

2. Heart and circulation (Ch. 12, Lab 13)

The heart is two pumps side by side.

  • The right side sends blood to the lungs (the pulmonary circuit).
  • The left side sends it to the body (the systemic circuit).

Blood's path through the heart: body → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → lungs → left atrium → ★ mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.

  • ★ The mitral valve has three names: left AV, bicuspid, mitral.

★ The first branches off the aorta are the left and right coronary arteries. They feed the heart muscle itself, which is easy to forget.

Vessels. Blood runs arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins.

  • Arteries are thick and elastic to take the pressure.
  • Capillaries are one cell thick for exchange.
  • Veins have valves and return blood to the heart.

Blood pressure is written systolic over diastolic: the pressure while the ventricles contract over the pressure while they relax.

3. Lymphatic and immune systems (Ch. 12–13, Lab 12)

★ The lymphatic system does three jobs:

  • Returns fluid that leaks out of capillaries back to the blood.
  • Carries absorbed fats.
  • Filters lymph through the lymph nodes.

★ The spleen is the largest collection of lymphatic tissue, and it cleans the blood (old red cells, platelets, microbes). The thymus is where T cells mature.

Three lines of defense:

  1. Barriers, such as skin and mucus.
  2. Nonspecific defenses: phagocytes, inflammation, fever, interferons, complement.
  3. The adaptive response: targeted and remembered.

★ The 8 steps of the adaptive response:

  1. Threat: a foreign cell or molecule gets in.
  2. Detection: a macrophage eats it.
  3. Alert: the macrophage displays the antigen and activates a helper T cell.
  4. Alarm: the helper T cell activates both B cells and cytotoxic T cells.
  5. Building specific defenses: B cells become plasma cells, which make antibodies, and T cells become cytotoxic T cells.
  6. Defense: antibodies take out free antigens, and cytotoxic T cells burst infected cells.
  7. Continued surveillance: memory cells stay behind.
  8. Withdrawal: suppressor T cells shut the response down.

★ Know the table of adaptive-response cells:

CellJob
Helper TThe "on" switch: activates B and cytotoxic T cells
Cytotoxic TKills infected cells and cancer cells
Suppressor TThe "off" switch: ends the response
B cell → plasma cellMakes antibodies
Memory cellsA fast response the next time

★ Autoimmune disorders. The immune system attacks "self."

  • Organ-specific, usually from T cells: Hashimoto's (thyroid) and type 1 diabetes (pancreas).
  • Body-wide, usually from B-cell antibodies: lupus.
  • Molecular mimicry: antibodies against strep can also hit the heart valves, causing rheumatic fever.

Active vs. passive immunity. Active immunity is your own memory cells and lasts. Passive immunity is borrowed antibodies, which work right away but fade in weeks.

4. Respiratory system (Ch. 14, Lab 14)

Air route: nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli, where gas exchange happens.

Breathing.

  • The diaphragm contracts, the chest gets bigger, pressure in the lungs drops and air rushes in.
  • A quiet exhale is passive: the lungs recoil like a stretched rubber band.

★ Spirometry (learn the chart; skip the numbers):

TermMeaning
Tidal volume (TV)A normal breath
Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)Extra you can breathe in beyond a normal breath
Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)Extra you can force out beyond a normal breath
Vital capacity (VC)IRV + TV + ERV
Residual volume (RV)Air that never leaves the lungs
Total lung capacity (TLC)VC + RV

★ Respiratory and circulatory systems working together.

  • Gases diffuse from high to low concentration across the alveolus–capillary wall.
  • O₂ moves into the blood; CO₂ moves out of it.

★ How gases ride in the blood (these percentages will be tested):

GasHow it travels
O₂About 98.5% on hemoglobin, 1.5% dissolved in plasma
CO₂About 70% as bicarbonate, about 20–23% on hemoglobin, 7–10% dissolved

★ Breathing control ("a special question").

  • The medulla sets the rhythm and senses CO₂ (as acid).
  • The aortic and carotid bodies sense O₂, but only when it's dangerously low.
  • The medulla does not sense oxygen.

5. Urinary system (Ch. 16, Lab 15)

What it's for. The kidneys clean the blood and control its volume, pressure, salt and pH.

  • They also make erythropoietin, which tells the bone marrow to make red blood cells, and activate vitamin D.
  • The lungs, skin and gut eliminate some wastes too, but the muscles don't.

★ Urine's path: kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra. Inside the kidney: nephrons → collecting duct → minor calyx → major calyx → renal pelvis → ureter, which exits at the renal hilum.

★ The nephron, in order: Bowman's (glomerular) capsule → proximal convoluted tubule → descending loop of Henle → ascending loop → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct, which several nephrons share.

★ Three processes:

ProcessDirectionWhat happens
FiltrationGlomerulus → capsuleBlood pressure pushes water and small solutes out. Cells and proteins stay in the blood.
ReabsorptionFiltrate → bloodUseful things go back: all the glucose, most of the sodium and water. About 99% of filtrate is returned, mostly in the proximal tubule.
SecretionBlood → filtrateWastes, excess ions and drugs are added to the urine.

Hormones that set the dial:

  • ADH makes the collecting duct hold on to water. Alcohol blocks ADH.
  • Aldosterone makes the kidneys reabsorb more sodium.
  • ANH (ANP) lowers blood volume and pressure.

Urination. A stretched bladder triggers a reflex: the internal sphincter (smooth muscle, involuntary) relaxes. You control the external sphincter (skeletal muscle).

6. Reproduction and development (Ch. 17–18, Lab 16)

The gonads (testes and ovaries) make both the gametes and the sex hormones.

★ The hormones:

HormoneWhere it comes fromWhat it does
GnRHHypothalamusTells the pituitary to release FSH and LH
FSHAnterior pituitaryGrows follicles; in males, supports sperm production
LHAnterior pituitaryIts surge triggers ovulation and forms the corpus luteum; in males, makes the interstitial (Leydig) cells release testosterone
EstrogenFollicleBuilds the uterine lining; high estrogen sets off the LH surge
ProgesteroneCorpus luteumKeeps the lining ready for an embryo
hCGThe blastocyst (the early embryo)Keeps the corpus luteum alive so the lining isn't shed; it's what pregnancy tests detect

Testosterone and inhibin act as brakes: high testosterone shuts off GnRH, and inhibin lowers FSH.

The cycles run in parallel:

Ovarian cycleUterine cycle
Follicular phase: a follicle grows (driven by FSH)Menstrual (days 0–5), then proliferative: the lining rebuilds (days 5–14)
Ovulation (about day 14, from the LH surge)
Luteal phase: the corpus luteum makes progesteroneSecretory: the lining thickens and secretes (days 14–28)

Sperm path: seminiferous tubules (where sperm are made) → epididymis (where they mature) → vas deferens → urethra.

★ The prenatal periods (fertilization to birth, about 38 weeks):

PeriodWhenWhat happens
Pre-embryonicWeeks 1–2Fertilization in the oviduct → zygote → cleavage → morula (a solid ball) → blastocyst (hollow) → implantation in the endometrium (about day 6)
EmbryonicWeeks 3–8Gastrulation forms the 3 germ layers; every organ forms; this is when most birth defects start
FetalWeek 9 to birthGrowth

Infancy comes after birth, in the postnatal period.

Extra credit #54 (2 pts)

Describe one way one system helps regulate something else in the body, naming the chemicals and what each does. It comes from the board work for Labs 14 and 15.

A ready answer: low blood pressure → the kidney releases renin → angiotensin II forms → aldosterone is released → the kidneys reabsorb more sodium, water follows → blood volume and pressure rise. ADH and aquaporins, or erythropoietin and red-blood-cell production, also work.
