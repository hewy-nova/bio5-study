Did you know

  • Estimates of the total number of species on Earth range from 5 to 30 million, and only 1.7 to 2 million have been formally identified.
  • Two planets about the size of Earth — Kepler-438b and Kepler-442b — orbit a distant star and may have conditions that could sustain life.

The chapter as one chain

  • Life can only be characterised, not defined (1.1). The chapter refuses a one-line definition and replaces it with seven traits every living thing shares.
  • Those shared traits imply common ancestry (1.2). Because all organisms build from the same molecules and cells, the diversity around us has to be explained — and evolution is the explanation, which classification records.
  • Life is organised in nested levels (1.3). Atom through biosphere; the human individual sits in the middle, so the book can move between molecules and ecosystems without changing subject.
  • Studying those levels requires a method (1.4). Observation → question → hypothesis → prediction → controlled experiment → conclusion, worked all the way through on oatmeal and cholesterol.
  • The method has human-specific forms (1.4). Clinical trials — three phases, placebo, double-blind, informed consent — and epidemiological studies extend it to people, where direct manipulation is limited.
  • Most people consume results rather than produce them (1.5). So the chapter ends with critical thinking and information literacy: the skills needed to judge a claim you did not test yourself.

1.1Basic Characteristics of All Living Things

pp. 2–4
Big ideaNo single definition of life satisfies all biologists, so life is instead characterised by a list of seven traits that living things share.

Why life cannot be defined

  • Opening frame: the Amazon rain forest. A place teeming with life; its biodiversity is why scientists search it for useful compounds, including medicinal plants — the Environmental Issue essay below.
  • Every candidate definition fails against a counterexample.
    • "Alive if it reproduces" — a page with a wet ink spot can fall on another page and reproduce itself almost exactly.
    • "Alive if it grows" — crystals grow and are not alive.
    • So biologists list the traits associated with life instead.

The seven characteristics of life

  • Living things contain nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates and lipids. The same hundred-odd elements are in everything on Earth, but living things combine certain of them into molecules found in all organisms. DNA matters especially because it can copy itself, which is what enables reproduction. (Ch. 2.)
  • Living things are composed of cells. Cells are the smallest units of life. Some organisms are unicellular; humans are multicellular (trillions of cells). All cells come from pre-existing cells; cell division makes reproduction, growth and repair possible. (Ch. 3, Ch. 19.)
  • Living things grow and reproduce. Bacteria make virtually exact copies; humans combine genetic material with another individual. Many organisms have life stages — embryo → fetus → child → adolescent → adult. (Ch. 17, Ch. 18.)
  • Living things use energy and raw materials. Metabolism is all the chemical reactions occurring within the cells of living things; through it organisms extract energy from nutrients and transform it to do work.
  • Living things respond to their environment. A boxer weaves and ducks; a chameleon takes aim at prey. Responding requires detecting a stimulus and having a way to react — sensory organs detect, the nervous system processes, the skeletal and muscular systems execute. (Ch. 5, 6, 8, 9.)
  • Living things maintain homeostasis. Homeostasis is the relatively constant, self-correcting internal environment of an organism. Life exists only within certain limits and living things behave so as to stay within them: shivering when too cold, sweating when too hot. Behavioural responses supplement physiological ones. (Ch. 4.)
  • Populations of living things have adaptive traits and evolve. Members of a reproducing population with beneficial genetic traits survive and reproduce better than those without — natural selection. The result is that organisms have adaptive traits that help them survive and reproduce in their natural environment. (Ch. 22.)
    • Rain forest examples: thin topsoil with nutrients near the surface → shallow root systems; tall trees develop buttress-like supports; vines climb over roots and trees to reach light.
    • Epiphytes — plants rooted on the surfaces of other plants, living high in the canopy for sun rather than growing in the ground.
    • Rain forest animals have adaptations too — flight, climbing — that let them reach plants for food.

1.2Evolution: A Unifying Theme in Biology

pp. 5
Big ideaEvolution explains both the unity and the diversity of life, and classification schemes are built to display evolutionary relationships.
  • Why evolution unifies biology. It explains the unity and diversity of the at least 10 million species on Earth. Organisms are unified because all species descended from the first cells; diversity arose as organisms adapted to different environments.
  • Classification reflects evolutionary relationships. Organisms with the greatest similarity are grouped together, and groups are subdivided again to show successively closer relationships.
  • The three-domain scheme.
    • Bacteria and Archaea consist of the various kinds of prokaryotes — very small, single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus or other internal compartments.
    • Eukarya contains all other organisms, including humans. Eukaryotic cells contain a nucleus and complex internal compartments called organelles.
    • Domain Eukarya is subdivided into four kingdoms: protists, fungi, plants, animals.
  • Where humans sit.
    • Vertebrates — a subdivision of the animal kingdom; animals with a nerve cord protected by a backbone.
    • Mammals — two characteristics make us mammals: hair, and feeding our young milk from mammary glands.
    • Primates — humans, lemurs, monkeys and apes share a suite of features including forward-looking eyes and a particularly well-developed brain.
    • Opposable thumbs — a thumb that can touch the tips of the other four fingers — are shared by humans, monkeys and apes.
    • Smaller details such as tooth structure and skeletal characteristics divide the primates into smaller subgroupings.

1.3Levels of Biological Organization

pp. 6
Big ideaLife is organised in nested levels from atom to biosphere, with the individual organism in the middle; the same biology can be studied at any level.
  • Levels within the individual. Cells, the smallest unit of life, are themselves composed of molecules. Tissues are groups of similar cells that perform specific functions. Organs may consist of different types of tissue working together. An organ system is two or more organs working together; humans have 11 (Ch. 4).
  • Levels beyond the individual.
    • Population — individuals of the same species (individuals that can interbreed) living in a distinct geographic area. Yellow-bellied marmots in an alpine meadow; four-eyed butterfly fish in a coral reef.
    • Community — all living species that can potentially interact in a particular geographic area.
    • Ecosystem — all the living organisms in a community along with their physical environment. Its size varies with the interest of the person studying it — the whole Earth, a forest, a single rotting log — but is viewed as relatively self-contained.
    • Biosphere — the part of Earth where life is found; all of Earth's organisms and their habitats. It extends only about 11 km (7 mi) above sea level and the same distance below, to the deepest trenches. If Earth were the size of a basketball, the biosphere would have the depth of about one coat of paint.

1.4Scientific Method

pp. 7–10
Big ideaScience is a way of learning about the natural world by applying rules of logic to how information is gathered and conclusions are drawn — not a single fixed set of steps.

The process

  • There is no single "the scientific method." It often begins with an observation that raises a question; a testable explanation is formulated; the explanation leads to a prediction; if the prediction holds when tested, the results support the explanation; if not, an alternative explanation is generated and tested.
  • Step 1 — Make careful observations, and ask a question. Questions should be reasonable and consistent with existing knowledge.
  • Step 2 — Develop a testable hypothesis.
    • A hypothesis is an educated guess about the answer, stated as a clearly defined statement, not a question.
    • It must be possible to test it and to prove it false. A hypothesis can be shown to be false but can never be proved true.
    • You can collect data supporting a hypothesis, but you must also rule out alternative hypotheses.
    • The hypothesis leads to one or more predictions. Different hypotheses can lead to identical predictions; then other predictions are needed to reject one.
  • Step 3 — Make a prediction and test it with a controlled experiment.
    • In a controlled experiment the subjects are randomly divided into a control group and an experimental group.
    • Both are treated the same except for one factor — the independent variable — whose effect the experiment is designed to reveal.
    • Confounding variables are additional variables not controlled for that may have affected the outcome; when present, we cannot say which variable caused the effect.
  • Step 4 — Draw a conclusion based on the results. A conclusion is an interpretation of the data, often summarised in a graph.
    • Reading a graph. The horizontal x-axis shows the independent variable; the vertical y-axis shows the dependent variable — the variable changed by the independent variable. Always read the axis labels and notice the scale. A bar graph is used when each treatment is a discrete category; a line graph shows change over time.
    • Statistical significance is a measure of the possibility that results were due to chance. A probability of less than 5% (p < 0.05) is generally acceptable; the lower, the more confidence.
  • Step 5 — Make new predictions, and test them. Repeatability: experiments must be repeated and yield similar results; other scientists following the same procedure should obtain a similar outcome.
  • From hypothesis to theory. A theory is a well-supported and wide-ranging explanation of some aspect of the physical universe, formed when related hypotheses have been confirmed repeatedly and fit together. It cannot be tested by a single experiment; it emerges from many observations, hypotheses and experiments and leads to further predictions. Examples: the cell theory (all cells come from pre-existing cells) and the theory of evolution by natural selection.
  • Inductive and deductive reasoning. Inductive — facts accumulate through observation until the weight of evidence allows a general statement; used to develop a hypothesis. Deductive — begins with a general statement leading logically to one or more conclusions, usually an "if–then" series; used in the oatmeal prediction.

The worked example — does oatmeal lower blood cholesterol?

  • The claim. A TV advertisement: a daily bowl of oatmeal lowers blood cholesterol. Lowering it is desirable because elevated cholesterol is related to atherosclerosis — fatty deposits clog blood vessels — which raises the risk of heart attack or stroke.
  • The observations. The soluble fibre β-glucan in oatmeal binds bile in the intestines and prevents its reabsorption; bile is high in cholesterol; bile bound to fibre leaves in the faeces; the liver then removes cholesterol from the blood to make new bile.
  • Hypothesis: β-glucan in oatmeal lowers blood cholesterol levels.
  • Prediction: if oatmeal consumption lowers blood cholesterol, then a person will lower their cholesterol by eating a bowl of oatmeal a day for 6 weeks.
  • What was measured. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol, the "bad" carrier that promotes atherosclerosis.
  • Design. Adults with similar LDL — between 4.5 and 5.5 mmol/L — divided randomly into two groups. Experimental group: a 1 oz packet of oatmeal per day for 6 weeks. Control group: a 1 oz packet of farina, a wheat cereal lacking β-glucan. LDL measured again at 6 weeks.
  • Result. LDL declined more in the experimental group than in the control group, and the differences were statistically significant — so we might conclude that eating oatmeal lowers blood LDL.
  • The follow-up. Two further predictions: more oatmeal → a larger effect; stopping oatmeal → LDL rises again. Four groups (farina, 1 oz, 2 oz, 3 oz per day), LDL measured 6 weeks after starting and 6 weeks after stopping. The effect increases with dose and slowly reverses once cereal stops.

Clinical trials and epidemiology

  • Animals first. Before testing on humans a drug is usually tested on laboratory rodents. Rats and mice are mammals, so some of their physiology generalises to ours; they are inexpensive, short-lived and reproduce quickly; animal research also shows how the body handles the drug, which helps set dosage. Most medical advances — vaccinations, chemotherapy, surgical techniques, transplants — began with animal studies, under strict rules on animal care.
  • Clinical trials are studies on humans; all phases use volunteers.
    • Phase I — safety, on fewer than 100 healthy people: can it be given safely, what is the effective dosage range, what are the side effects.
    • Phase II — a few hundred people with the target disease, to see whether it works for its intended purpose.
    • Phase III — thousands of participants; the new drug is compared with alternative treatments.
    • The FDA approves only drugs that have passed all three phases.
  • Placebo — an innocuous, non-drug substance made to look like the drug being tested, given to the control group. Participants are randomly assigned and do not know which they receive.
  • Double-blind — neither the researchers nor the participants know who is receiving treatment and who the placebo. Participants' expectations could affect their response; researchers' expectations could affect their interpretation of the data.
  • Informed consent — legally required; the document lists all possible harmful effects and must be signed before participating. Participants must be mentally capable of understanding the treatment and risks.
  • Epidemiological studies look at patterns within large populations. To investigate air pollution and asthma, researchers would look for a correlation between the variable of interest and its suspected effects — predicting, say, that hospital admissions for asthma rise with air pollution.
    • Cell phones and brain cancer. Based on a 2011 review, the WHO called cell phone use "possibly carcinogenic to humans", resting largely on a 13-country study of nearly 13,000 users over 10 years: all users vs never-users showed no difference in brain tumour rates, but the heaviest users showed a slight increase. A newer study of more than 350,000 users over 10 years found no dose–response relationship — tumour incidence did not rise with length of use and was not greater in the most-exposed brain regions. Nearly all studies to date have failed to show a link; research continues because cancers take years to develop.

1.5Critical Thinking to Evaluate Scientific Claims

pp. 11
Big ideaMost people evaluate scientific claims rather than test them, and the key skill is asking a specific set of questions about the claim and its source.
  • Why this matters. Few of us perform controlled experiments, but all of us must evaluate claims met as advertisements, news stories and anecdotes. The key is to ask questions; the list is not exhaustive.
  • Is the information consistent with other sources? Gather information from a variety of sources; do not passively accept a report as true.
  • How reliable is the source? Does the person or group have scientific expertise? Is there reason to think the claim is biased — who stands to gain if you accept it? The FDA is probably a more reliable source on a drug's effectiveness than the company marketing it. If a claim is controversial, listen to both sides and note who argues on each.
  • Was the information obtained through proper scientific procedures? Controlled experiments beat anecdotal evidence, which cannot be verified. A friend says his muscles got larger with special exercise equipment — you cannot be sure unless measurements were taken before and after, and even then there is no guarantee it will build yours.
  • Were the results interpreted correctly? A headline "Fish Oils Increase Longevity" refers to rats fed a high-fish-oil diet living longer. Two reasons the supplement claim does not follow: the study was on rats, not humans (rats are more resistant to heart disease), and the variable was dietary fish oil, not capsules — supplements may not have the same effect, and could raise body fish oil to unhealthy levels.
  • Are there other possible explanations? Suppose people who eat fish three times a week live longer. That is a correlation, and a correlation does not prove that one factor caused the other — both may be caused by a third factor: people who eat fish may exercise more, have less stressful jobs, or live where there is less pollution.
  • Information literacy — recognising what you need to know, locating relevant information, evaluating it, applying it and communicating it. Because so much arrives through technology, information technology literacy is also essential.
  • Why it matters civically. Genetically modified food, stem cell research, pollution taxes — even if you are not a lawmaker you are a voter. Scientists can provide facts but not simple answers.

Stop And Think

1Water and methane have been found on Mars, and something must be producing the methane. If samples of Martian water or soil were brought back, what characteristics of life could scientists look for? (p. 4)

Work the seven characteristics as a checklist. Look for the molecules of life — nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids — and above all a self-copying information molecule such as DNA. Look for cells or their remains. Look for evidence of metabolism — energy being extracted from nutrients, or byproducts (the methane itself) that would not persist without something producing them. In a live sample, look for growth and reproduction over time, a response to a stimulus, and internal conditions held steady against the surroundings. For fossil material the accessible criteria are the molecules, the cell structures and the metabolic byproducts.

2If a new organism were discovered in the rain forest, what characteristic would you look for to decide whether it was a mammal? (p. 5)

Hair, or mammary glands producing milk — the two characteristics the section names as definitive. Hair is the one you could check on a single specimen without waiting to observe it rearing offspring. Do not use forward-looking eyes or a well-developed brain: those are primate traits, and a backbone would only establish a vertebrate.

3Why is randomly dividing the volunteers into groups a better design than letting them choose? (p. 7)

Because self-selection introduces confounding variables. People who choose the oatmeal group are likely to differ from those who choose farina in ways that also affect cholesterol — they may already eat better, exercise more, or be more motivated about their health. Any LDL difference at the end could then be due to those differences rather than to the β-glucan, with no way to tell which. Random assignment distributes such differences evenly, so the cereal is the only systematic difference between the groups.

Boxed essays

Environmental Issue

Medicinal Plants and the Shrinking Rain Forest (p. 2)

  • Willow bark relieved pain and fever long before scientists isolated salicylic acid from it and developed aspirin. Digitalis, a heart medication, came from purple foxglove in a folk remedy. Broccoli contains the anticancer chemical sulforaphane.
  • More than 25% of US prescription medicines contain chemicals that came from plants; 70% of newly developed drugs are from natural sources. Many plant compounds still cannot be synthesised.
  • Most medically useful plants are in the tropics, where forests are being cut. Madagascar, home of the rosy periwinkle (source of two anticancer drugs — Figure 1.A), has lost 90% of its vegetation.
  • 155,000 of the known 250,000 plant species are from tropical rain forests, and fewer than 2% of known plant species have been tested for medicinal value.
  • Questions posed: should indigenous people be compensated for plants found in their locality if extracts become drugs? What steps might preserve rain-forest biodiversity?
What Would YOU Do?

FDA approval and critically ill patients (p. 9)

  • The FDA balances patients' desire for access against protection from unsafe or ineffective treatments. Approval usually takes more than 8 years.
  • Questions posed: should the FDA bypass steps for critically ill patients who cannot wait? What criteria decide the degree of illness warranting an unapproved drug? Who is responsible if early access causes serious side effects or death? The book supplies no answer.
Consider This Case

Maribel's rain-forest asthma drug (p. 11)

  • A drug company asks Maribel to test a rain-forest plant that a native says relieves difficulty breathing; she suspects it might treat asthma, isolates a component, and animal tests show it is effective.
  • Phase I should give the drug to fewer than 100 healthy volunteers; the variable is the drug and its dose, the control is a placebo group, and a positive result means only that it is safe at a known dose range with known side effects — nothing yet about asthma.
  • Phase II should give it to a few hundred people with asthma, randomly assigned to drug or placebo, ideally double-blind; the variable is the drug, the control the placebo group, and a positive result means it relieves asthma better than a placebo.
  • Phase III should enrol thousands of asthma patients and compare the new drug with existing asthma treatments; a positive result means it works at least as well as, or better than, current treatment — which is what FDA approval needs.

2.1The Nature of Atoms

pp. 23–24
Big ideaAtoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons, and the number of electrons in the outermost shell determines how an atom will behave chemically.

Matter, atoms and shells

  • Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass. Mass, measured in grams or kilograms, is how much matter an object contains. The three traditional states are solid, liquid and gas. All matter is made of atoms.
  • Atoms cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means. Each is made of subatomic particles — protons, neutrons, electrons — characterised by location, charge and mass.
    • The nucleus at the centre holds protons and neutrons; electrons move around it at energy levels called shells — three-dimensional spaces, drawn as flat circles for convenience.
    • The innermost shell holds up to 2 electrons; the next holds up to 8. Atoms with more than 10 electrons have additional shells. Electrons with the most energy are farthest from the nucleus.
    • The number of electrons in the outermost shell determines an atom's chemical properties. Atoms whose outermost shells are not full tend to interact with other atoms.
    • Neutrons are neutral; protons positive; electrons negative. Electrons stay near the nucleus because they are attracted to its protons. Most atoms have equal numbers of protons and electrons and no net charge.

Elements and the periodic table

  • An element is a pure form of matter containing only one kind of atom — gold, silver, iron, oxygen. Earth is made of a little more than 100 elements; only about 20 are found in the human body, which consists mostly of oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen.
  • Every atom of an element has the same number of protons; all carbon atoms have six. That number is the atomic number.
  • The periodic table lists the elements with a one- or two-letter symbol — carbon C, chlorine Cl, gold Au from the Latin aurum.
    • Each proton and neutron has a mass of about 1 atomic mass unit (amu); an electron's mass is treated as zero. So atomic mass = protons + neutrons. Oxygen, atomic mass 16 and atomic number 8: eight protons, eight neutrons.
    • The table organises elements into groups (columns) whose members have the same number of electrons in the outermost shell — group 3 elements have three. Because that count governs reactivity, elements in the same group tend to behave the same way.

Isotopes and radiation

  • Atoms of one element can have different numbers of neutrons, giving slightly different atomic masses — isotopes. More than 300 occur naturally.
    • Carbon has three: all have six protons; most have six neutrons, some seven or eight — masses 12, 13 and 14, written 12C (the most common), 13C and 14C.
  • Radiation is energy moving through space — radio waves, light, heat, and the energy or particles given off by unstable isotopes as they break down. Unstable, radiation-emitting isotopes are radioisotopes; about 60 occur naturally.
  • Radiation can be harmful. Absorbed harmful radiation may damage organs such as the skin and lead to cancers (sunburn, Figure 2.3, is the most common radiation burn). It may leave no visible injury yet alter the hereditary material in the reproductive system's cells, causing defects in offspring. Radon, the Environmental Issue below, is the naturally occurring case.
  • Radiation is useful in medicine.
    • Diagnosis — x-ray imaging is the most familiar. Radioactive iodine (131I) is taken up by the thyroid gland in the neck, which normally accumulates iodine to regulate growth and metabolism; a small dose images the gland (Figure 2.4 contrasts a normal gland with an enlarged one). Larger doses kill thyroid cells when the gland is enlarged and overactive.
    • Therapy — radiation kills cancer cells, which divide more rapidly and have higher metabolic rates than most normal cells and so are more susceptible. An outside beam needs shielding of healthy tissue; a source can also be placed inside the body — radioactive seeds left in the prostate stop emitting within a year and are usually left in place.
    • Food irradiation — the What Would YOU Do? box and the logo in Figure 2.5.

2.2Chemical Bonds and Compounds

pp. 25–26
Big ideaAtoms with unfilled outer shells bond by sharing electrons (covalent) or transferring them (ionic), and the resulting compound's properties differ from those of its elements.

Compounds and covalent bonds

  • A compound is a new chemical substance formed from two or more elements, with characteristics usually different from those elements. Sodium is a silvery metal that explodes on contact with water; chlorine is a deadly yellow gas; together they form crystalline sodium chloride — table salt (Figure 2.6, four photographs).
  • The atoms or ions in a compound are held by chemical bonds, of two types: covalent and ionic. When atoms bond they lose, gain or share the electrons in their outermost shell. Figure 2.7 draws hydrogen (1 electron), carbon (2 + 4) and oxygen (2 + 6) to show how far short of full each outer shell is.
  • A covalent bond forms when two or more atoms share electrons in their outer shells.
    • Methane (CH4). Carbon's outer shell holds four electrons but can hold eight; hydrogen has one but its shell holds two. Carbon joins four hydrogens and every one of the five atoms ends with a filled shell.
    • A molecule is a chemical structure held together by covalent bonds. A compound needs two or more elements, so oxygen gas (O2) is a molecule but not a compound.
    • Molecular formulas list every element with a subscript for the number of atoms: sucrose is C12H22O11; three sucrose molecules is 3C12H22O11.
    • Structural formulas draw one line per shared pair.

Ionic bonds

  • An ion is an atom or group of atoms carrying a positive or negative charge. Charges come from the transfer (not sharing) of electrons. Lose an electron and there is one more proton than electrons — a positive charge; gain one and the charge is negative. Oppositely charged ions attract, and an ionic bond results from that mutual attraction.
  • Ions form because atoms tend to attain a complete outermost shell. Sodium chloride, worked through: sodium has one outer electron, chlorine seven. Sodium transfers that electron to chlorine. Sodium now has a full outer shell — because it loses its third shell entirely and the second becomes its outermost — and a positive charge (Na+). Chlorine's outer shell fills and it is Cl-. The ions attract.
  • Because they do not contain shared electrons, ionic bonds are weaker than covalent bonds.

2.3The Role of Water in Life

pp. 27–30
Big ideaWater's polarity and hydrogen bonding account for its solvent power, its high heat capacity and heat of vaporisation, and for the acid–base chemistry every biological reaction runs in.

Polarity and hydrogen bonds

  • Why water is unusual. Its solvent power, high heat capacity and high heat of vaporisation all trace to its polarity — the tendency of its molecules to have positive and negative regions — and to the hydrogen bonds between them.
  • In covalently bonded molecules electrons may be shared equally or unequally. Unequal covalent bonds are polar; molecules with unevenly distributed charges are polar molecules. In water the shared electrons spend more time near the oxygen, so the oxygen carries a slight negative charge and each hydrogen a slight positive one.
  • A hydrogen bond is the attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen and a slightly negative atom nearby — in water, hydrogen to oxygen; sometimes hydrogen to atoms of other elements. Weaker than ionic or covalent bonds, hence the dotted lines. Individually weak, collectively significant: they maintain the shape of proteins and of DNA and account for water's unique properties.

Properties of water

  • Water molecules are cohesive (cling together) and adhesive (cling to other molecules).
  • Excellent solvent. Because its molecules are polar, water dissolves both polar and charged substances. NaCl dissolves into independent ions: sodium ions are attracted to the negative regions of water molecules, chloride ions to the positive regions (Figure 2.11 draws each ion ringed by water molecules turned the right way round). Because of its solvent power and cohesiveness water is the body's main transport medium — the liquid component of blood, and of urine.
  • High heat capacity. About 67% of the human body is water — a 68 kg (150 lb) person carries about 45 kg (100 lb) of it — so we resist changes in body temperature. A great deal of heat is required to raise water's temperature because the hydrogen bonds holding molecules together must be broken. Blood also redistributes heat: fingers do not usually freeze on a frigid day because blood carries heat to them from the muscles.
  • High heat of vaporisation. A great deal of heat is needed to make water evaporate, again because hydrogen bonds must break before molecules can leave the liquid. Evaporating water carries away a lot of heat, cooling the surface — we rely on evaporating sweat to prevent overheating.
    • Heat stroke — sharp rises in body temperature, usually to 40 °C (104 °F) or higher, can damage the brain and other organs. At risk: the very young, the very old, and anyone doing strenuous activity in heat, especially if unaccustomed to it. Heat plus humidity is particularly dangerous because water vapour in the air inhibits the evaporation of sweat.
  • Water supply. We pollute seawater and freshwater, and a global shortage of freshwater is being driven by population growth and climate change — the Toilet to Tap essay.

Acids, bases, pH and buffers

  • Sometimes a water molecule dissociates: H–O–H ⇌ H+ + OH- — a hydrogen ion and a hydroxide ion. In equations an arrow is read "yields". The dissociated fraction is extremely small, so the amount of H+ in the body must be precisely regulated.
  • Acids and bases are defined by what happens in water. An acid is anything that releases H+ when placed in water — hydrochloric acid, HCl → H+ + Cl-. A base is anything that releases OH- — sodium hydroxide, NaOH → Na+ + OH-; the OH- reacts with H+ to form water and so reduces H+ concentration. Acids increase the concentration of H+; bases decrease it.
  • The pH scale runs 0 to 14; 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. At pH 6 the H+ concentration is 1 × 10-6 moles per litre; at pH 5 it is 1 × 10-5. (A mole is a specific number of atoms, molecules or ions.) Technically pH is the negative logarithm of H+ concentration.
    • The lower the pH, the greater the acidity, and each unit is a tenfold change: pH 5 is 10 times more acidic than pH 6, pH 4 is 100 times more acidic than pH 6.
  • Buffers keep pH from changing dramatically: they remove excess H+ when concentration rises and add H+ when it falls.
    • The carbonic acid–bicarbonate system keeps blood at about pH 7.4: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-. When H+ falls, carbonic acid dissociates and adds H+; when H+ rises, it combines with bicarbonate and leaves solution. Essential, because a drop from 7.4 to 7.0, or a rise to 7.8, can cause death in minutes.
    • The stomach is the exception. Almost every biochemical reaction in the body runs near pH 7, but hydrochloric acid gives the stomach a pH of about 1 to 3, killing swallowed bacteria and beginning protein breakdown. Acid backing into the oesophagus is "heartburn"; antacids are weak bases that neutralise some of it.
  • Acid precipitation — rain, snow or fog with pH below 5.6, the pH of natural precipitation. Burning fossil fuels releases sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, which react with atmospheric water to form sulfuric acid and nitric acid. Linked to forest decline and losses of fish and amphibians; the fine sulfate and nitrate particles are inhaled and cause asthma and bronchitis. Lower emissions have brought significant progress.

2.4Major Molecules of Life

pp. 31–39
Big ideaThe four classes of macromolecule — carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids — are polymers built from monomers by dehydration synthesis and taken apart by hydrolysis, and each class does a different job in the body.

Monomers, polymers, and the two reactions

  • Macromolecules are exceptionally large molecules. Polymers are macromolecules of many small, repeating subunits — monomers — linked in a chain: a pearl necklace, each monomer a pearl. A protein is a polymer of amino acids; glycogen is a polymer of glucose.
  • Dehydration synthesis (the condensation reaction) forms polymers: bonding one monomer to another releases a water molecule — one monomer donates OH, the other H.
  • Hydrolysis is the reverse: it requires the addition of water across the covalent bond; the H attaches to one monomer, the OH to the other, and the bond breaks. Critical in digestion — most foods are polymers too large to pass from the digestive tract into the blood, so they are hydrolysed into monomers that can be absorbed.

Carbohydrates

  • Carbohydrates — sugars and starches — provide fuel for the body. Made entirely of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with twice as many hydrogens as oxygens. Classified by size.
  • Monosaccharides (simple sugars) — the smallest units, three to seven carbons; five carbons is a pentose, six a hexose. Glucose, fructose and galactose are six-carbon sugars. Figure 2.14 draws glucose three ways — straight chain, ring with the carbons lettered, ring with the carbons omitted — all C6H12O6.
  • Oligosaccharides (oligo = few) — chains of a few monosaccharides joined by dehydration synthesis. A disaccharide is two: sucrose (table sugar) = glucose + fructose (Figure 2.15 shows the two rings joined through a bridging oxygen with water released); maltose (beer) = glucose + glucose; lactose (milk) = glucose + galactose.
  • Polysaccharides (poly = many) — long chains, most commonly of glucose; most store energy or provide structure.
    • Starch is the storage polysaccharide in plants; glycogen in animals — a short-term energy source broken down to release glucose, stored mainly in muscle and liver cells.
    • Cellulose is structural, in plant cell walls. Humans lack the enzymes to digest it, so it passes through unchanged — but it is important dietary fibre (roughage) that helps faecal matter move through the large intestine, and may reduce the incidence of colon cancer.

Lipids

  • Lipids do not dissolve in water: they are nonpolar, water is polar, and neither attracts the other. Three types matter to health: triglycerides, phospholipids, steroids.
  • Triglycerides — fats and oils — are one glycerol plus three fatty acids. A fatty acid is a chain of carbons bonded to hydrogens with the acidic group COOH at one end; it bonds to glycerol by dehydration synthesis (Figure 2.17).
    • Saturated fatty acids have only single bonds between carbons — "saturated with hydrogen". They pack closely, so butter is solid at room temperature.
    • Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more double bonds, which put kinks in the chain and prevent tight packing, so olive oil is liquid.
    • Trans fats — hydrogens added to unsaturated fats to stabilise them, extend shelf life or solidify them; hydrogenated vegetable oil is margarine. Found in many packaged snacks.
    • Energy density. Fats and oils provide about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrates or proteins, which makes fat the ideal long-term store; excess food energy is converted to fat globules in the cells of adipose tissue. Fat also cushions organs such as the kidneys, insulates, and absorbs and transports lipid-soluble vitamins. In excess it endangers the circulatory system (Ch. 12a).
  • Phospholipids — glycerol bonded to two fatty acids and a negatively charged phosphate group, with a small, usually polar variable group (R) on the phosphate. Two regions: the fatty-acid tail is nonpolar and hydrophobic ("water-fearing"); the glycerol–phosphate–R head is polar and hydrophilic ("water-loving").
  • Steroids — four carbon rings with varying attachments. Cholesterol is a component of the plasma membrane and the foundation from which estrogen and testosterone are made (Figure 2.19 draws all three on the identical four-ring skeleton). Blood cholesterol comes from the liver and from the diet; a high level is a risk factor for heart disease.

Proteins

  • A protein is a polymer of one or more chains of amino acids, twisted and folded into complicated structures. Thousands of different proteins provide structural support, transport, movement and regulation of chemical reactions — all built from a set of only 20 amino acids.
  • Amino acids — a central carbon bound to a hydrogen, an amino group (NH2), an acidic carboxyl group (COOH) and a variable group (side chain, R). Amino acids differ in R. Nonessential amino acids the body can make; essential amino acids must come from food.
  • Peptide bonds — special covalent bonds formed by dehydration synthesis, linking the carboxyl group of one amino acid to the amino group of the next (Figure 2.21: glycine + alanine → the dipeptide glycylalanine + water). Chains of a few amino acids are peptides — dipeptide (2), tripeptide (3) — chains of 10 or more are polypeptides, and protein is used for polypeptides with at least 50 amino acids.
  • Primary structure — the sequence of amino acids, determined by the genes; it dictates the protein's structure and function, and even slight changes can alter both. Sickle-cell anaemia results from the substitution of one amino acid in haemoglobin; the misshapen protein alters the shape of red blood cells, which can clog the tiny vessels of the brain and heart.
  • Secondary structure — helices and pleated sheets formed by bends and coils in the chain through hydrogen bonding. Alterations in the secondary structure of a nerve-cell surface protein can turn it into an infectious prion — Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, mad cow disease.
  • Tertiary structure — the overall three-dimensional shape; hydrogen, ionic and covalent bonds between side chains all contribute. Denaturation — heat or a pH change unravels the molecule and it loses its shape; even a minor change can mean loss of function.
  • Quaternary structure — some proteins have two or more polypeptide chains, each a subunit; quaternary structure is the assembled subunits, held largely by attractions between oppositely charged variable groups.

Enzymes

  • Enzymes — almost always proteins — speed up chemical reactions without being consumed. Without them most reactions in our cells would be far too slow to sustain life; with them, reactions proceed 10,000 to 1 million times faster.
  • The equation: E + S → ES → E + P. The substrate goes in, the product comes out. Maltase breaks maltose into glucose; sucrase breaks sucrose into glucose and fructose — an enzyme's name often resembles its substrate's. These are decomposition reactions; enzymes also speed synthesis reactions.
  • The active site. The substrate binds a specific location on the enzyme, forming the enzyme–substrate complex; binding orients the substrate so it can react; the products leave and the enzyme binds another substrate. A typical enzyme converts about 1000 substrate molecules per second.
  • Specificity. Each enzyme binds only one or a few substrates, because of the unique shape of its active site — active site and substrate fit like jigsaw pieces.
  • Cofactors are non-protein substances that help enzymes convert substrate to product; some sit permanently at the active site, others bind with the substrate. Organic (carbon-containing) cofactors — the vitamins — are called coenzymes; inorganic cofactors include zinc and iron.
  • Enzyme deficiency — lactase. Lactase digests the lactose in milk into glucose and galactose. Infants make enough; many adults do not, and undigested lactose reaches the large intestine, where bacteria produce gas and lactic acid — diarrhoea, cramps, bloating. Lactose-reduced milk and lactase tablets exist.

Nucleic acids and ATP

  • Genes, our units of inheritance, are segments of long polymers of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) — one of the two nucleic acids, with ribonucleic acid (RNA). Both are polymers of nucleotides joined by dehydration synthesis.
  • A nucleotide = a five-carbon (pentose) sugar + one of five nitrogen-containing bases + at least one phosphate group (Figure 2.24 draws it as phosphate sphere — pentose pentagon — base hexagon). The five bases: adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, uracil. Cytosine, thymine and uracil have a single ring; adenine and guanine have two. The base sequence in DNA and RNA determines the amino-acid sequence of a protein.
  • RNA is a single strand; its sugar is ribose; its bases are C, A, G and uracil. DNA is double-stranded: two strands held together by hydrogen bonds between the bases twist into a double helix; its sugar is deoxyribose; its bases are A, thymine, C, G. Figure 2.26's enlargement shows A–T bridged by two dotted hydrogen bonds and C–G by three — the same pairing the Ch. 21 tab and Lab 3 build on.
  • ATP — adenosine triphosphate — is a nucleotide: the sugar ribose, the base adenine, and three phosphate groups. Right now many ATP molecules in your cells are each splitting off a phosphate group; the energy the molecule stored by holding it is released and the cell traps it to do work.
  • ATP is formed from ADP (adenosine diphosphate) by bonding a phosphate to it in an energy-requiring reaction; the energy is stored in the new high-energy phosphate bond, which is easily broken when the cell needs it.
  • ATP is the energy currency of cells: all energy from breaking down molecules such as glucose must be channelled through ATP before the body can use it.

Stop And Think

1Fluorine has an atomic mass of 19 and an atomic number of 9. How many protons and neutrons are in the nucleus? How many electrons surround it? Name two elements you would expect to show the same reactivity. (p. 23)

Atomic number 9 means 9 protons. Atomic mass = protons + neutrons, so 19 − 9 = 10 neutrons. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons: 9 electrons, 2 in the first shell and 7 in the second. Reactivity is set by the outermost-shell count and the periodic table groups elements by it, so the elements in fluorine's column behave alike — chlorine and bromine (iodine is equally acceptable). All have seven outer electrons and need exactly one more.

2What would hydrolysis of a fat yield? (p. 33)

A triglyceride is one glycerol joined to three fatty acids by dehydration synthesis, so hydrolysis — adding water back across each bond — yields one glycerol and three free fatty acids, consuming three water molecules, one per bond.

3Which three elements are found in carbohydrates, fats and proteins? Which additional element do proteins always contain? (p. 35)

All three contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Proteins additionally always contain nitrogen, from the amino group (NH2) in every amino acid.

1Would the triglyceride shown in part (b) be a solid or a liquid at room temperature? Why? (Figure 2.17, p. 33)

Most likely a liquid, or at least softer than a fully saturated fat. Two of its three fatty acids are saturated and straight, but the third is unsaturated with a double bond that kinks the chain. The book's mechanism is packing: saturated chains pack closely (butter is solid), while kinks prevent tight packing (olive oil is liquid). One kinked tail in three is enough to interfere with close packing.

Environmental Issue

Radon Gas: A Killer That Can Be Stopped (p. 23)

  • Radon is a radioactive gas from the breakdown of uranium in rock, soil or water — invisible, odourless, seeping through foundation cracks and accumulating, especially in energy-efficient buildings that limit air exchange. Greatest exposure is at home. It can also arrive in well water and be released during showers.
  • The EPA estimates 1 in 15 US homes has an unacceptable level — 4 or more picocuries per litre. Radon causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths a year in the US, the leading cause after cigarette smoking; smoking plus radon is particularly risky.
  • Test all homes below the third floor with a hardware-store kit or a certified professional; a contractor can install a venting system that pulls radon from beneath the house. The Federal Radon Action Plan (2011) and Healthy People 2020 set awareness and testing goals.
  • Questions posed: would you pay higher dorm fees to fix a radon problem? Should testing be the buyer's or renter's responsibility, given that states do not require disclosure?
What Would YOU Do?

Food irradiation (p. 24)

  • Irradiation exposes an item to radiation; foods are irradiated to delay spoilage, extend shelf life and remove microorganisms, insects and parasites. The food does not become radioactive.
  • Supporters: test animals fed irradiated food show no adverse effects. Opponents: environmental risks of irradiation plants, and no long-term controlled trials on people of all ages.
  • Irradiated foods in the US include white potatoes, wheat flour, fresh meat and poultry, spinach and iceberg lettuce. A wholly irradiated product must carry the logo (Figure 2.5) and words such as "Treated with radiation"; an irradiated ingredient is listed as such without the logo.
  • Questions posed: is irradiating food safe? Would you eat it? The book supplies no answer.
Environmental Issue

Toilet to Tap (p. 36)

  • The water budget. 97.4% of Earth's water is ocean; about 2% is ice; that leaves 0.6% as freshwater — surface water and groundwater. We have damaged it by redirecting rivers, damming, draining wetlands and extracting groundwater faster than precipitation replaces it. The UN predicts that by 2030 half the world's population will face water shortages.
  • Singapore and Namibia recycle wastewater for drinking. Orange County's Groundwater Replenishment System (2008) treats sewage — primary effluent to secondary effluent — then runs it through an Advanced Water Purification Facility; half is injected into a seawater intrusion barrier (an underground dam of wells blocking seawater from the groundwater basin) and half goes to a percolation pond, filtering through gravel, sand and clay into the groundwater and eventually into drinking wells. The result meets or exceeds all drinking-water standards.
  • The World Water Council predicts recycled wastewater will be routine in cities worldwide within 30 years.
  • Questions posed: would you hesitate to drink water recycled from sewage that meets standards, and why? Will human ingenuity always produce the technology to avoid crises such as a freshwater shortage?
Consider This Case

Carter at football practice (p. 39)

  • First day of practice, hot and humid. Carter, back on campus only the day before from a northern state, takes off his helmet, holds his head, and answers with slurred speech and confusion.
  • What he is suffering from: heat stroke — a sharp rise in body temperature, usually to 40 °C or higher, damaging the brain; confusion and slurred speech are the signs.
  • Why: strenuous activity in heat he is unaccustomed to, with humidity — water vapour in the air inhibits the evaporation of sweat, so the body's main cooling mechanism (water's high heat of vaporisation) fails. The section names exactly this combination as particularly dangerous.

Ch. 1 is one argument. Life cannot be defined, only characterised — by seven traits (the molecules of life, cells, growth and reproduction, metabolism, response, homeostasis, and populations that evolve). The shared traits imply common ancestry, which classification records: three domains, four kingdoms inside Eukarya, and humans inside animals → vertebrates → mammals (hair, milk) → primates (forward-looking eyes). Life is organised in twelve nested levels from atom to biosphere. Studying it takes a method — observation, question, hypothesis, prediction, controlled experiment, conclusion — worked through on oatmeal and LDL cholesterol; the method has human forms (clinical trials in three phases, placebo, double-blind, informed consent; epidemiological studies); and because most people evaluate claims rather than test them, the chapter ends with critical thinking: source, procedure, interpretation, and correlation versus cause.

Ch. 2 is one fact and its consequences. The number of electrons in an atom's outermost shell determines its chemistry. Unfilled shells drive bonding — share (covalent) or transfer (ionic). Unequal sharing makes water polar, polarity gives hydrogen bonds, and hydrogen bonds explain water's solvent power, heat capacity and heat of vaporisation. Water's dissociation sets up acids, bases, pH and buffers — the carbonic acid–bicarbonate system holds blood at 7.4. Big molecules are built by dehydration synthesis and broken by hydrolysis, and the four classes divide by job: carbohydrates for fuel, lipids for storage, membranes and hormones, proteins for structure, transport, movement and catalysis, nucleic acids for information — with ATP as the nucleotide that carries the energy.
