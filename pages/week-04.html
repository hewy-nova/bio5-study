Did you know

  • Sujeet Desai plays seven instruments, has won Olympic medals in swimming and alpine skiing, holds a black belt in Tae Kwon Do, and has Down syndrome.
  • Unwound and laid end to end, the DNA from one cell's chromosomes would stretch more than 5 feet, at a diameter of 50 trillionths of an inch.
  • You developed from one of more than 8.3 million genetically different gametes your mother could have produced.

The chapter as one chain

  • Two divisions, two jobs (19.1). Mitosis builds and repairs the body; meiosis builds gametes.
  • What is being divided (19.2). The chromosome — DNA plus histones, genes in fixed order. Somatic cells are diploid: 46 chromosomes as 23 homologous pairs, 22 autosome pairs plus one sex pair.
  • When division happens (19.3). Division is one phase of the cell cycle. Interphase (G1, S, G2) is where DNA is replicated, producing sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
  • How body cells divide (19.4–19.5). P-M-A-T separates the sister chromatids so each daughter nucleus is diploid and identical; cytokinesis then pinches the cytoplasm apart.
  • Seeing the result (19.6). Because chromosomes condense during division, a cell can be frozen at metaphase, photographed, and arranged into a karyotype.
  • How gametes are made (19.7). A first division separates homologues, so two divisions turn one diploid cell into four haploid ones and the chromosome number stays constant across generations.
  • Why siblings differ (19.7). Crossing over in prophase I, independent assortment at metaphase I.
  • When it fails (19.7). Nondisjunction leaves gametes with extra or missing chromosomes — trisomy or monosomy — and the karyotype comes back as the diagnostic.

19.1Two Types of Cell Division

pp. 395
Big ideaMitosis makes genetically identical body cells for growth and repair; meiosis makes gametes with half the chromosome number, so fertilisation restores the original count.
  • Life begins as one cell. A zygote forms from the union of egg and sperm; by adulthood the body is trillions of cells. Even in adults many cells keep dividing for growth and tissue repair.
  • Mitosis. The nuclear division that produces identical body cells.
  • Meiosis. A special nuclear division producing gametes.
    • In females it happens in the ovaries and produces eggs; in males in the testes, producing sperm.
    • Gametes end up with half the chromosome number of the original cell.
  • Why halving matters. When egg and sperm nuclei unite, the chromosome number is restored, so body-cell number stays constant from one generation to the next.

Meiosis

Zygote

19.2Form of Chromosomes

pp. 396
Big ideaA chromosome is DNA plus histone proteins carrying genes in fixed order, and human somatic cells carry 46 of them as 23 homologous pairs — 22 autosome pairs plus one sex pair.
  • Chromosome. A tightly coiled combination of a DNA molecule and specialised proteins called histones, found in the nucleus. The DNA carries the information; the histones provide support and control gene activity.
  • Gene. A specific segment of DNA that directs synthesis of a protein, and so determines expression of a trait. Genes sit in a fixed sequence along a chromosome, like beads on a string.
  • Somatic cells. All cells except eggs and sperm; in humans, 46 chromosomes — 23 pairs, one member of each pair from the egg and one from the sperm.
  • Homologous chromosome pair (homologues). One maternal and one paternal chromosome carrying genes for the same traits.
  • Diploid (2n). Any cell with two of each kind of chromosome. In diploid cells genes also occur in pairs, at the same position on homologous chromosomes.
  • Sex chromosomes. One of the 23 pairs. XX genetically female, XY genetically male.
  • Autosomes. The other 22 pairs; they determine most inherited characteristics.

19.3The Cell Cycle

pp. 396–397
Big ideaCell division is only one phase of the cell cycle; the long interphase (G1, S, G2) is when the cell grows and replicates its DNA, producing the sister chromatids that division then separates.
  • Cell cycle. The whole sequence from a cell's origin in its parent's division through its own division into two daughter cells. Two major phases: interphase and cell division.
  • Interphase. The period between divisions; most of the cycle.
    • In actively dividing cells a full cycle may run 16–24 hours, with only 1–2 hours spent dividing.
    • It is not a resting period, as was once thought. The cell carries out its functions and grows.
    • G1 (first gap). Major growth before DNA synthesis begins.
    • S (synthesis). DNA is replicated.
    • G2 (second gap). Growth after DNA synthesis, before mitosis.
  • Chromatin and the replication product.
    • Through interphase the genetic material is long thin threads called chromatin — the state in which DNA can be replicated and genes can be active.
    • At the start of interphase each chromosome is one DNA molecule plus proteins.
    • During S the copies stay attached at a region called the centromere.
    • While attached, each copy is a chromatid; the two identical attached copies are sister chromatids.
  • Which cells divide.
    • G0. Some specialised adult cells (most nerve cells) exit late in G1 and function without dividing.
    • Others (liver cells) stop dividing but keep the ability to resume if repair is needed.
    • Others divide throughout life — skin cells replacing the numbers worn off daily.
  • Checkpoints. Proteins monitor the internal environment to confirm conditions are right and that DNA was replicated accurately. Healthy cells will not divide unless both are met. Cancer cells escape this regulation.
  • Two overlapping processes after interphase. Mitosis divides the nucleus; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, starting toward the end of mitosis.

Key terms

Cell cycle
the full sequence from a cell's origin to its own division
Interphase
G1, S and G2 together — everything that is not division
Centromere

Sister chromatids

Exam tip

DNA replication happens in interphase, not prophase. Directly tested — Reviewing the Concepts Q2.

Exam tip

One molecule, three names. Before S: one chromosome = one DNA molecule. After S, while attached: one chromosome = two sister chromatids. After they separate at anaphase, each is a chromosome again. The name changes; the DNA does not.

Conflict — recorded, not resolved

how much of interphase you are expected to know; §19.3 insists interphase is emphatically not a resting period and treats G0 as a distinct state (p. 397), while the chapter summary reduces it to "the period between cell divisions" and drops G1/S/G2 and G0 entirely (p. 409). The slide deck omits G0 and the checkpoints altogether — that material is book-side and lab-side, not lecture-side. Do not study interphase from the summary alone.

19.4Mitosis: Genetically Identical Diploid Body Cells

pp. 397–398
Big ideaMitosis is one continuous process conventionally split into four stages, and its result is two diploid daughter nuclei genetically identical to the parent.
  • Four stages, one continuous process. Prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase. The book stresses mitosis is dynamic and does not stop between stages.
  • Prophase.
    • Chromatin condenses into chromosomes as DNA wraps around histones, then loops and twists into a compacted structure.
    • Once condensed, DNA cannot be replicated and gene activity shuts down.
    • Condensation makes sister chromatids easier to separate without breaking.
    • The nuclear membrane begins to break down.
    • In the cytoplasm the mitotic spindle forms from microtubules associated with the centrioles, which move toward opposite ends of the cell.
  • Metaphase. Chromosomes attach to the spindle and line up at the equator. The alignment ensures each daughter cell receives one chromatid from each of the 46 chromosomes.
  • Anaphase. Sister chromatids separate, splitting at the centromere. Once separate, each former chromatid counts as a chromosome in its own right. Spindle fibres pull them to opposite poles.
  • Telophase. A nuclear envelope forms around each group, the spindle disassembles, and the chromosomes become threadlike again.

Exam tip

The single most useful distinction in the chapter. In mitotic metaphase chromosomes line up individually — one row of 46. In metaphase I of meiosis homologous pairs line up side by side — 23 pairs, a doubled row. This one difference answers the Stop and Think in 19.7 and the lab's metaphase questions.

Exam tip

Condensation is not just packaging. The book gives two functional reasons: replication and gene activity shut down, and the chromatids are less likely to break when pulled apart.

19.5Cytokinesis

pp. 398–399
Big ideaCytokinesis divides the cytoplasm by contracting a band of microfilaments into a furrow that pinches the cell in two, beginning during telophase.
  • Cytokinesis. Division of the cytoplasm; begins toward the end of mitosis, sometime during telophase.
  • A band of microfilaments where the chromosomes originally aligned contracts and forms a furrow.
  • The furrow deepens and eventually pinches the cell in two.

Key terms

Cytokinesis
division of the cytoplasm, beginning during telophase
Cleavage furrow
the contracting microfilament band that pinches the cell in two
Exam tip

Mitosis and cytokinesis are separate events that overlap in time. Mitosis divides the nucleus; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. A question asking what mitosis produces is asking about nuclei. 19.6Karyotypes

pp. 400
Big ideaBecause chromosomes condense during division, a cell can be arrested at metaphase and its chromosomes photographed and arranged by size and centromere position into a karyotype that reveals numerical or structural defects.
  • Why it works. Chromosomes shorten and thicken during division, which makes them visible under a light microscope.
  • The procedure.
    • White blood cells are taken from a blood sample and grown in nourishing medium.
    • The culture is treated with a drug that destroys the mitotic spindle, preventing separation and halting division at metaphase.
    • Cells are preserved, stained and photographed.
    • Chromosome images are paired by centromere location and overall length, then numbered largest to smallest.
  • Karyotype. The resulting arrangement, checked for irregularities in chromosome number or structure.
  • A karyotype is prepared just before division, so every chromosome shown is duplicated and consists of two chromatids.

Key terms

Karyotype
chromosomes arranged by size and centromere position for diagnosis
Exam tip

One mechanism, two questions. The drug halts cells at metaphase by destroying the spindle. That is the same mechanism as the chemotherapy Stop and Think in 19.4 and the "what if spindle fibres failed to form" question in Applying the Concepts. Learn it once, answer three times.

19.7Meiosis: Creation of Haploid Gametes

pp. 401–408
Big ideaMeiosis is two divisions that turn one diploid cell into four haploid cells — keeping the chromosome number constant across generations and, through crossing over and independent assortment, making every gamete genetically unique.

Outline

  • Haploid, and why halving is necessary.
    • Haploid (n). Gametes carry one member of each pair.
    • Function one: keeps chromosome number constant. If gametes were made by mitosis they would be diploid: 46 + 46 = 92, then 184, then 368, then 736. Even one extra copy of a single chromosome usually kills the embryo.
    • Function two: increases genetic variability — the raw material natural selection acts on.
  • Meiosis I — the reduction division. Separates the two homologues of each pair; produces two cells each with 23 chromosomes, each chromosome still two sister chromatids.
    • Each daughter gets one member of each pair — not a random 23. A cell with two copies of chromosome 3 and no chromosome 6 would not survive.
    • Prophase I: homologues line up next to one another by synapsis ("bringing together"). Crossing over occurs here.
    • Metaphase I: matched pairs position at the midline. The book's analogy: pairing socks before putting them in a drawer.
    • Anaphase I: members of each homologous pair separate.
    • Telophase I: cytokinesis begins, giving two daughter cells.
    • Interkinesis: a brief interphase-like period. It differs from mitotic interphase in that no DNA replication takes place.
  • Meiosis II — the mitosis-like division. Each chromosome lines up independently and the sister chromatids separate, in both daughter cells, giving four cells. Resembles mitosis except only 23 chromosomes align, versus 46.
  • From haploid cells to gametes. Spermatogenesis gives four functional sperm; oogenesis gives one ovum plus polar bodies, because unequal cytoplasmic division keeps the nutrients in the one egg. Ovulation occurs after meiosis I, at the secondary oocyte stage.
  • Two sources of genetic variability.
    • Crossing over — prophase I. Corresponding pieces of chromatids are exchanged between maternal and paternal homologues during synapsis. Because homologues align gene by gene, exchanged segments carry information for the same traits — but may direct different expressions (attached vs unattached earlobes).
    • Independent assortment — metaphase I. Which member of a pair faces which pole is random, like a coin flip, and each of the 23 pairs orients independently.
  • When the machinery fails.
    • Nondisjunction. Failure of homologues to separate in meiosis I, or of sister chromatids to separate in meiosis II. Both go to one daughter cell; the other gets none of that type.
    • Trisomy — three representatives of one chromosome. Monosomy — only one.
    • About 70% of miscarriages involve a fetus with an abnormal chromosome number.
    • Abnormal numbers of chromosome 21 or of the sex chromosomes are usually not fatal but cause a syndrome.
    • Turner (XO) — ~1 in 5,000 female infants. External appearance female; thick neck fold, short stature, wide chest, underdeveloped breasts; ovaries poorly developed in 90%, causing infertility. Most XO zygotes are lost as miscarriages.
    • Klinefelter (XXY) — ~1 in 500 to 1 in 1,000 newborn males. The extra X is twice as likely to come from the egg. Signs usually appear at puberty: small testes, inadequate testosterone, taller but less muscular, incomplete facial and body hair, slight breast development. Often sterile; not all XXY males show symptoms.
    • Triple-X (XXX) — most have normal sexual development and can conceive; some have learning disabilities and delayed language.
    • Jacob (XYY) — produced when the chromatids of a replicated Y fail to separate. Often taller than normal; some have slightly lower than normal intelligence.
    • Down (trisomy 21) — 1 in 700 infants.

The most common single error in this chapter. Meiosis I is the reduction division — homologues separate, 2n → n. Meiosis II is the mitosis-like division — sister chromatids separate, n → n. Reversing them costs several questions at once.

Exam tip

Two mechanisms, two stages. Crossing over in prophase I, during synapsis. Independent assortment at metaphase I. Reversing them is a standard trap.

Exam tip

Stop And Think

1Cancer cells divide frequently and without end. One class of chemotherapy drug blocks the formation of spindle fibres — why would that be effective? (p. 398)

Without a mitotic spindle there is nothing to attach the chromosomes at the equator during metaphase and nothing to pull the sister chromatids apart at anaphase, so mitosis stalls and cannot finish. Cells arrested that way cannot produce daughter cells and generally die. Because the drug hits cells at the moment they attempt division, it damages frequently dividing cancer cells far more than most normal tissue — which also explains why fast-dividing tissues (skin, gut lining, hair follicles, bone marrow) show the side effects.

2What would happen if a cell completed mitosis but did not complete cytokinesis? (p. 399)

Mitosis divides the nucleus, cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm — so the cell would end up with two complete, genetically identical nuclei inside one undivided cell, a binucleate cell. No daughter cells would be produced. The genetic material would have been distributed correctly; only the cytoplasmic split failed.

3Under a microscope, how could you tell metaphase of mitosis from metaphase I of meiosis? (p. 404)

In mitotic metaphase each chromosome lines up individually — a single row of 46, with one spindle fibre attaching to each side of a single centromere. In metaphase I the chromosomes are lined up as homologous pairs side by side — 23 pairs, a doubled row, with a fibre from one pole attached to one whole homologue and a fibre from the opposite pole to its partner. Paired homologues, and at higher magnification the contact points left by crossing over, mean meiosis I.

Figure Questions

1Describe the difference in the structure of a chromosome between the start of interphase and the end of interphase. (Figure 19.3, p. 397)

At the start of interphase (G1) each chromosome is a single unreplicated DNA molecule with its proteins, present as long, thin, uncondensed chromatin. By the end of interphase — after S and through G2 — each chromosome has been replicated and consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The chromosome count has not changed; the amount of DNA per chromosome has doubled.

Boxed essays

Ethical Issue

Trisomy 21 (p. 408)

  • One in 700 infants is born with three copies of chromosome 21.
  • Reported features: moderate to severe mental impairment; short stature; flattened nose, forward-protruding tongue, upward-slanting eyes, a fold of skin at the inner corner of each eye.
  • About 50% have heart defects, and many die as a result. Digestive blockage is also common and may need surgery shortly after birth.
  • Risk rises with maternal age: a 30-year-old is twice as likely as a 20-year-old; at 45 she is 45 times as likely.
  • Life expectancy is now approaching 60 years in many countries.
  • The essay's core argument: roughly 95% of positive screening results are wrong, all positives are encouraged toward invasive follow-up, and 1–2% of pregnancies tested invasively end in miscarriage — putting about 700,000 pregnancies a year at risk.
What Would YOU Do?

Testosterone treatment in Klinefelter syndrome (p. 408)

  • Prompt: if you had a son with Klinefelter syndrome, would you want him to have testosterone treatments after puberty?
  • XXY testes often stay small and produce inadequate testosterone.
  • That insufficiency drives above-average height with reduced muscularity, incomplete facial and body hair, and slight breast development.
  • Many XXY males are asymptomatic and never diagnosed, and sterility may occur regardless of treatment.
Consider This Case

Laura's prenatal test (p. 408)

  • Laura, in her early 40s, is pregnant. Prenatal testing shows two copies of chromosome 21, but the fetus is XYY.
  • How did the extra chromosome arise? Only the father can contribute a Y, so a fetus with two Y chromosomes received both from the sperm. The error is nondisjunction of the replicated Y during meiosis II of spermatogenesis — the two sister chromatids of the duplicated Y failed to separate at anaphase II. That YY sperm fertilised a normal X-bearing egg.
  • Her age is not the relevant factor. Maternal age is associated with trisomy 21, not with an extra paternal Y.
  • Would the extra Y cause fetal death? No. Abnormal numbers of the sex chromosomes are usually not fatal; XYY males are often taller than normal with some having slightly lower than normal intelligence.

End Of Chapter Questions

1The process of mitosis results in…

a) two haploid cells b) two diploid cells c) four haploid cells d) four diploid cells

b — two diploid cells

Mitosis is one division of a diploid cell giving each of two nuclei a full identical set. (a) fails because mitosis does not reduce the chromosome number. (c) describes meiosis, which needs two divisions. (d) is impossible — one division cannot yield four cells.

2DNA is synthesised (replicated) during…

a) interphase b) prophase c) metaphase d) anaphase

a — interphase, specifically S phase

Prophase is precisely when condensation shuts replication and gene activity down. Metaphase aligns already-replicated chromosomes and anaphase pulls existing chromatids apart — nothing new is made in any of the three.

3Crossing over occurs during which stage of meiosis?

a) prophase I b) metaphase I c) prophase II d) metaphase II

a — prophase I

Crossing over requires synapsis, which happens in prophase I. Metaphase I is independent assortment. In meiosis II homologues are no longer in the same cell, so there is no partner to exchange with.

4Which expresses an important difference between spermatogenesis and oogenesis?

Spermatogenesis produces four functional sperm; oogenesis produces one mature ovum and up to three polar bodies

Unequal cytoplasmic division concentrates the nutrients in one ovum. "A mature ovum is diploid" is false — both gametes are haploid. "Crossing over occurs only in spermatogenesis" is false — it is a feature of prophase I in both sexes.

5What kind of chromatid is attached at the centromere?

a) sister b) mother c) daughter d) programmed

a — sister

The two attached, genetically identical replicate copies are sister chromatids. "Maternal/paternal" describes whole homologues, not attached replicates; "daughter" applies to cells and nuclei.

6After mitosis, the number of chromosomes in a daughter cell is ______ those in the parent cell.

the same as

Mitosis separates sister chromatids, so each daughter receives one chromatid from each of the 46 chromosomes. "One-half" describes meiosis; "twice" never happens — replication doubles the DNA per chromosome, not the chromosome count.

7After meiosis, the number of chromosomes in a daughter cell is ______ those in the parent cell.

one-half

Meiosis I separates the members of each homologous pair, so each product carries 23 rather than 46.

8An abnormal number of chromosomes can result during meiosis because of…

a) crossing over b) recombination c) nondisjunction d) synapsis

c — nondisjunction

Crossing over and recombination shuffle content, not number. Synapsis is the normal pairing that makes correct separation more reliable, so it prevents this error rather than causing it.

9During meiosis, the processes of ______ and ______ increase genetic diversity.

crossing over (prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I)

Random fertilisation is a third source of variation but is not part of meiosis, so it does not belong in these blanks.

10______ chromosomes carry genes for the same traits.

Homologous

Sister chromatids are identical replicates of one chromosome, not two chromosomes from different parents. "Sex chromosomes" names one specific pair rather than the general relationship.

11______ is the pairing of chromosomes during meiosis.

Synapsis

Crossing over is what happens during synapsis, not the pairing itself. Fertilisation is the fusing of gametes.

12The stage of mitosis during which sister chromatids separate is ______.

Anaphase

Metaphase is only alignment — nothing has separated yet. Telophase is after separation.

13The stage of meiosis during which sister chromatids separate is ______.

Anaphase II

Anaphase I separates whole homologues, each still two chromatids. Answering "anaphase I" reverses the two divisions — the single most common error in this chapter. Answering just "anaphase" is incomplete, because meiosis has two.

14In a culture of actively dividing cells, one cell has half as much DNA as most of the others. Which stage is it in?

It has already passed anaphase — telophase/cytokinesis, or a newly formed G1 daughter cell

DNA content only drops by half at the moment the chromatids are partitioned into two separate nuclei. Prophase and metaphase cells still hold the full replicated complement, and an anaphase cell still contains all of it within a single cell. Flag: the question asks for a "stage of mitosis", but a cell whose DNA has actually halved is strictly a post-division G1 cell — check the lab or lecture key for the wording this course wants.

15What would happen if the spindle fibres failed to form during mitosis?

Mitosis would arrest; the cell would die or end up with one nucleus holding all replicated chromosomes

The chromosomes would have nothing to attach to and nothing to move them, so they could not align at metaphase or be pulled apart at anaphase. Same mechanism as the karyotype drug in 19.6 and the chemotherapy prompt in 19.4.

16What condition is indicated by the karyotype shown? (p. 410)

Klinefelter syndrome — 47,XXY

Autosome pairs 1–22 each contain two chromosomes, but the sex-chromosome box contains two X chromosomes and one Y. Not Turner (single X), not Down (21 shows the normal two copies), not Jacob (that would be XYY). Flag: read from a rendered image with no text layer — confirm against a print copy.

The chapter as one chain

  • Define the tool. Stem cells divide continually and stay unspecialised, so they can be steered into whatever cell type is needed.
  • Rank it by flexibility. Totipotent → pluripotent → multipotent → unipotent. More flexible means more useful, and means it came from earlier in development.
  • Add the rejection constraint. Self (MHC) markers develop across prenatal life and childhood, so younger-source cells and cells from the patient or a relative are less likely to be rejected. This makes the source question medical, not merely technical.

  • Route around the embryo problem. SCNT makes patient-matched embryonic stem cells; iPSCs get pluripotency out of an adult skin cell with no embryo destroyed.
  • Cash it out. Replacement cells, lab-grown and bioprinted organs, regrown limb tissue, and safer drug testing.

19a.1Stem Cells: Unspecialised Cells

pp. 413
Big ideaStem cells are the body's pool of undifferentiated, continually dividing cells, and both their usefulness and their rejection risk depend on how far along in development they came from.
  • The problem stem cells are meant to solve. Every disorder on the chapter's list traces to one of two causes: too little cell division, or defective cell function.
    • Too little division — spinal cord injury cannot repair itself, because neurons do not usually divide.
    • Defective function — Parkinson's disease (too few dopamine-producing neurons); haemophilia (missing clotting factors).
    • Named targets: heart attack damage, Parkinson's, type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer's, Lou Gehrig's disease, spinal cord injury.
  • Why most cells cannot do this job.
    • Most of the body's cells are specialised — muscle cells contract, neurons conduct impulses.
    • Specialisation is directed by cues in each cell's immediate environment: growth factors, surface proteins, salts, and contact with other cells.
    • All cells in one body share the same genetic makeup, so specialisation means particular genes have been switched on or off.
    • Specialised cells usually do not divide again.
  • Stem cells, by contrast. Relatively unspecialised cells that divide continually, keeping a pool of undifferentiated cells on hand. Given the right growth factor or hormone they can be coaxed into a chosen cell type.
  • The potency ladder.
    • Totipotent — all cell types in the body. From a fertilised egg.
    • Pluripotent — nearly every cell type. Embryonic stem cells, from an embryo about a week old.
    • Multipotent — several cell types. Some adult stem cells; cord blood and placenta.
    • Unipotent — only one cell type. Some adult stem cells.
  • Rejection and self markers.
    • Every person's cells carry self markers (MHC markers) identifying them as belonging in that body.
    • Self markers are genetically determined, so a relative's are more similar than an unrelated person's.
    • Cells not recognised as self are usually attacked and killed by the body's defence system.
    • Self markers develop during prenatal development and early childhood — so embryonic or fetal stem cells have less developed markers and are less likely to be rejected.

Two axes, not one. "Unspecialised" does not mean "unlimited". A unipotent adult stem cell is still a stem cell — it just has one destination.

19a.2Sources of Human Stem Cells

pp. 414–416
Big ideaHuman stem cells come from adult tissues, umbilical cord blood and placenta, and early embryos — plus two lab routes, SCNT and iPSCs, that generate patient-matched pluripotent cells.
  • Adult stem cells — unipotent and multipotent.
    • Present in adults but harder to locate than in an embryo. Found waiting in bone marrow, brain, skin, liver and other organs.
    • Diseased or dying cells supply the biological cues that summon stem cells and tell them what to become.
    • When an adult stem cell divides, one daughter usually specialises and the other stays a stem cell, replenishing the supply.
    • Less versatile than embryonic — usually one cell type or one lineage. The book's example: fat stem cells form only fat, bone and cartilage.
    • The section adds that adult stem cells are now known to be more versatile than once believed.
  • Umbilical cord and placental stem cells — multipotent.
    • Stem cells do not settle into an infant's bone marrow until a few days after birth; before that they circulate and pass through umbilical cord vessels.
    • Most cord blood stem cells are blood stem cells. Inside the body, blood stem cells make about 260 billion new cells a day — about an ounce of blood.
    • Cord blood also holds some that can become bone, cartilage, heart muscle, nerve tissue and liver tissue.
    • Advantages over adult bone marrow. Bone marrow harvesting needs a hospital procedure under general anaesthesia; cord blood is taken from a cord already removed. Less likely to be rejected, because the self markers have not fully developed. Less likely to carry infections.
    • The size limitation. Cord blood has a limited number of stem cells, so it is usually used for childhood diseases. The placenta holds several times more, and cord plus placenta together can treat adult illnesses.
    • Blood disorders already treated. Sickle-cell anaemia and beta-thalassaemia, both abnormal haemoglobin.
    • Leukaemia. Traditionally treated by bone marrow transplant, but most patients never find a tissue-type match. Immature markers on cord stem cells lower the rejection risk.
    • Social issues raised. Should privately banked blood be reserved for the donor or available to any matching person in need? Should banks be private or government-funded?
  • Embryonic stem cells — pluripotent.
    • Unused embryos from fertility clinics supply most stem cells in current research — created for reproduction, never used, destined for destruction.
    • At 6–7 days the embryo is a blastocyst, about the size of a pinhead, with an inner cell mass of about 20–30 cells adhering to one side. The inner cell mass is the stem cells. Extracted and cultured, they divide for years, creating a stem cell line.
    • Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT). The nucleus of a somatic cell is transferred into an egg whose own nucleus has been removed, and the new cell is stimulated to begin embryonic development. All cells of the resulting embryo are genetically identical, so SCNT is a form of cloning.
    • Dolly, born 5 July 1996, was the first mammal cloned from an adult cell — a mammary cell nucleus from an adult ewe's udder placed in an enucleated egg. The advantage of cloning from an adult rather than an embryo: you can confirm the animal has the desired trait before copying it.
    • Reproductive vs therapeutic cloning. Therapeutic cloning produces replacement cells genetically matched to a patient, avoiding immune rejection. If the patient's disease were genetic, a normal copy of the gene would be substituted before the nucleus went into the enucleated egg — done in mice, not yet in humans.
    • Using hSCNT, scientists formed an embryonic clone of a woman with type 1 diabetes and extracted healthy insulin-producing stem cells, now being safety-tested in mice.
  • Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Adult human skin cells caused to revert to pluripotent stem cells able to differentiate into any tissue type. Key advantage: no embryo is destroyed.

19a.3Potential Uses for Stem Cells

pp. 417–418
Big ideaThree uses are being pushed — replacing damaged cells, growing new organs in regenerative medicine, and testing drugs on human cells that will not survive ordinary culture.
  • Replacement for damaged cells.
    • Thousands of US clinical trials are in progress or recruiting.
    • Trials on umbilical cord stem cells to repair nerve cells in children with cerebral palsy.
    • Most work so far has been in nonhuman animals. Insulin-producing stem cells eliminated type 1 diabetes symptoms in mice; in rats, bone marrow stem cells move toward damaged heart muscle and promote strength.
  • Growing new organs.
    • Regenerative medicine rebuilds damaged organs by coaxing stem cells to fill lab-engineered tissue scaffolds.
    • The 2004 jaw case. A German man's jaw was removed for cancer. Doctors made a mesh mould shaped like the missing jawbone and implanted it below his shoulder blade with cow-derived bone mineral, a bone growth factor, and his own bone marrow blood. Once bone scans showed new bone had formed, the new jaw was removed with some muscle and blood vessels and implanted correctly. Four weeks later he could chew solid food.
    • Other organs. Urinary bladders and tracheas have been grown and transplanted — a scaffold in the right shape, the patient's own cells layered into tissue, then implanted. Grown from the patient's cells, so not rejected.
    • 3D bioprinting. A scaffold is printed; cells are removed from the target organ, separated by type and cultured — or stem cells from the patient's fat or blood generate the cell types. Modified inkjet printers lay the cells layer by layer. So far only simple structures — flat, tubular or hollow. Hearts and kidneys are the hope, not the present.
    • Regrowing limb tissue. A powder made from pig bladders, nicknamed "magic pixie dust". Pig bladders have an extracellular matrix containing collagen; the powder is thought to attract the body's own stem cells and give them the chemical signals to build new tissue — including skin, muscle and nerve.
  • Testing new drugs.
    • Before clinical trials a drug must be shown safe in animals and cultured human cells. The problem: heart, brain and liver cells do not survive in tissue culture.
    • Stem cells may provide a way to test new drugs before human testing.
    • Researchers are using iPSCs to create cells displaying the characteristics of a diseased cell. Skin samples from Alzheimer's patients are being collected for exactly this.

Not a new idea. The reason the lab-grown bladders, tracheas and jaw were not rejected is that they were grown from the patient's own cells. That is the self-marker logic from 19a.1 reappearing, not a separate principle.

Stop And Think

1Why would chemical signals be necessary for the development of new tissues? (p. 417)

Because a stem cell is unspecialised by definition — nothing inside the cell decides what it becomes. Specialisation is directed by cues in the cell's immediate environment (growth factors, surface proteins, salts, cell contact) which switch particular genes on and off. Every cell carries the same genes, so the only thing that can make one become skin and another muscle is the signal it receives. The pig-bladder powder works precisely because it supplies that signalling environment, which is why the regrown tissue contains skin, muscle and nerve rather than an undifferentiated mass.

Figure Questions

1Why is the nucleus removed from the donor egg? (Figure 19a.6, pp. 415-416)

So that the only nuclear genetic material in the reconstructed cell comes from the patient's donated somatic cell. The whole point of therapeutic cloning is genetic identity with the patient, so the replacement cells carry the patient's self markers and escape immune rejection. If the egg's own nucleus stayed, the cell would carry the egg donor's genes too, the embryo would not be a clone of the patient, and the cells produced would be rejected like any unmatched transplant.

The chapter as one chain

  • Start from meiosis. Homologues separate and each gamete gets one member of each pair. The rest of the chapter is the consequences.
  • Name the parts. Gene, allele, homozygous/heterozygous, dominant/recessive, genotype vs phenotype.
  • Turn meiosis into two laws. Alleles segregate; genes on different chromosomes assort independently.
  • Make the laws predictive. Punnett squares give 3:1 and 9:3:3:1; pedigrees run the same logic backward.
  • Complicate simple dominance. Codominance, incomplete dominance, pleiotropy, multiple alleles and polygenic inheritance are all cases where one gene → one either/or trait breaks down.

20.1Principles of Inheritance

pp. 420–430
Big ideaChromosome behaviour in meiosis explains inheritance — alleles segregate, and genes on different chromosomes assort independently — and Punnett squares and pedigrees turn that into predictions, subject to a long list of complications dominance alone cannot cover.
  • The vocabulary, restated from Ch. 19.
    • Alleles are different versions of a gene. Somatic cells carry two per gene, one on each homologue, at the same specific location.
    • Homozygous — two copies of the same allele. Heterozygous — two different alleles.
    • Dominant allele — its effects show whether or not an alternative is present. Recessive allele — masked in the heterozygote, expressed only when homozygous.
    • Genotype — the precise set of alleles. Phenotype — the observable trait. The dominant form shows whether the individual is AA or Aa, so appearance does not always reveal genetic makeup.
  • The two laws.
    • Homologues segregate during meiosis I, so an egg or sperm has only one member of each pair — the law of segregation.
    • Each homologous pair lines up independently and orientation is random, so allele pairs for genes on different chromosomes segregate independently — the law of independent assortment.
    • Mendel worked this out by crossing pea plants knowing nothing about chromosomes.
  • One-trait crosses. Identify each parent's possible gametes, then use a Punnett square. A monohybrid cross Ff × Ff gives genotypes 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff and phenotypes 3 freckled : 1 unfreckled.
  • Two-trait crosses. Valid only if the genes are on different chromosomes. A dihybrid cross FfWw × FfWw makes four gamete types, a 4 × 4 square, and offspring 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. The double recessive is 1 in 16.
  • Pedigrees. A chart showing genetic connections in a family, letting you deduce an unknown genotype from ancestors and descendants.
    • Conventions: each generation on its own line, most ancestral at top; males are squares, females circles; a horizontal line connects mating partners; affected individuals carry a coloured symbol.
    • Marfan syndrome — autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder. The allele for fibrillin produces a nonfunctional protein. Connective tissue is everywhere, so symptoms are widespread; a serious consequence is weakness in large blood vessel walls, which can tear or burst.
    • Cystic fibrosis — recessive, abnormally thick mucus. About 1 in 2,000 US infants; average length of life 24 years. Three primary signs: abnormally salty sweat, digestive problems, respiratory problems. About 1 in 22 US Caucasians is a carrier.
    • A carrier displays the dominant phenotype but is heterozygous — which is why CF children are usually born to healthy parents who had no idea.
  • What makes an allele dominant or recessive. Usually the dominant allele produces a normal functional protein while the recessive produces an altered nonfunctional form or none. Albinism: melanin production depends on the enzyme tyrosinase; one dominant copy makes enough, two recessive copies make only nonfunctional tyrosinase.
  • When simple dominance breaks down.
    • Complete dominance — the heterozygote shows the dominant trait only.
    • Codominance — the heterozygote shows both traits at once, because both alleles produce functional proteins. Type AB blood: IA and IB make two different surface polysaccharides and both appear.
    • Incomplete dominance — the heterozygote shows something in between, because only one allele makes a functional product so the heterozygote gets one dose instead of two. Sickle-cell trait.
    • Sickle-cell, worked. HbSHbS = sickle-cell anaemia; when blood oxygen falls the cells sickle and clump, which can clog capillaries and damage vital organs. HbAHbS = sickle-cell trait; generally healthy, but sickling may occur during a prolonged drop in blood oxygen, as at high elevation.
    • Pleiotropy — one gene leading to many effects. Sickle-cell again: sickled cells break down, clog vessels and accumulate in the spleen, damaging heart, brain, lungs, kidneys, muscles and joints.
    • Multiple alleles — three or more forms of a gene in the population, though any one individual still has only two. ABO blood: IAIA or IAi = A; IBIB or IBi = B; IAIB = AB; ii = O.
  • Polygenic inheritance. Two or more genes, often on different chromosomes, producing one trait. More genes means smoother gradations and greater extremes.
    • Height model. Three genes A, B, C with dominant alleles adding height. Two AaBbCc parents give seven height classes. Either extreme has probability 1/64; the most likely outcome is medium height at 20/64.
    • Skin colour is likewise polygenic — probably at least four genes beyond the albinism allele, giving nine classes.
  • Genes on the same chromosome. Genes on one chromosome tend to be inherited together because the chromosome moves into a gamete as a unit. Such genes are linked and usually do not assort independently — but crossing over can unlink them.
  • Sex-linked genes.
    • X and Y are not truly homologous — Y is much smaller and they do not carry all the same genes.
    • X has about as many genes as a typical autosome; most have no corresponding allele on Y and are X-linked genes.
    • A male expresses virtually all alleles on his single X, recessive ones included. So recessive X-linked phenotypes are much more common in males.
    • A son cannot inherit an X-linked recessive allele from his father — to be male he inherited the Y, not the X — so it can only come from his mother.
    • Named X-linked recessive disorders: red-green colour blindness; haemophilia A (factor VIII) and B (factor IX); Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
  • Sex-influenced genes. Some autosomal genes are powerfully influenced by sex hormones, so expression differs between males and females.
    • Male pattern baldness. Expression requires both the allele and testosterone. The allele acts as dominant in males and recessive in females: a male with one or two copies goes bald; only homozygous women do.

Watch out

The easiest way to lose a two-trait question. The law of independent assortment applies only to genes on different chromosomes. Exam tip

Codominance vs incomplete dominance. Codominance means both products are made and both show — AB blood carries both polysaccharides. Incomplete dominance means only one product is made, at half dose, so the phenotype lands in between. "Both at once" is codominant; "blended" is incomplete.

Exam tip

Each child is independent. A Punnett square gives the probability for each child separately. Two children of Ff × Ff parents is not "one freckled, one not" — each has its own 3/4 chance. Reviewing questions 4 and 5 and Applying question 5 all test this one idea.

Exam tip

Sex-linked is not sex-influenced. Sex-linked genes are physically on a sex chromosome. Sex-influenced genes are autosomal, and it is the hormone level that changes whether the allele acts dominant or recessive. Male pattern baldness is sex-influenced, which is why father-to-son transmission is possible.

20.2Breaks in Chromosomes

pp. 431
Big ideaChromosomes can physically break, and the resulting loss (deletion) or gain (duplication) of a segment causes disorders that have nothing to do with which allele you inherited.
  • How breaks happen. Certain chemicals, radiation, or viruses. Breakage also occurs as an essential part of crossing over — and rarely chromosomes are misaligned during crossing over, so one chromatid loses a segment and the other gains one.
  • Deletion — the loss of a piece of chromosome.
    • Most common type: the tip breaks off and fails to move into the same daughter cell as the rest.
    • Deleting more than a few genes on an autosome is usually lethal.
    • Cri-du-chat syndrome ("cry of the cat") — the most common human deletion, loss of a small region near the tip of chromosome 5. The infant has a high-pitched cry like a kitten, caused by an improperly developed larynx. Round face; wide-set, downward-sloping eyes; misshapen ears. Not usually fatal, but causes severe mental impairment. 1 in 50,000 live births.
  • Duplication — the addition of a piece of chromosome.
    • In general a small duplication is less harmful than a deletion of comparable size.
    • Repeat expansions. Disorders also arise when certain DNA sequences are duplicated many times over.
    • Fragile X syndrome — an abnormally long sequence of repeats makes the X chromosome fragile and easily broken, and the repeated DNA can shut down activity of the entire chromosome. The most common form of inherited mental impairment: roughly 1 in 1,250 males and 1 in 2,500 females. Exactly how it causes the impairment is not known.

20.3Detecting Genetic Disorders

pp. 432–433
Big ideaGenetic testing is available before birth, at birth and in adulthood, and each option is a trade-off between what the information buys and what it costs.
  • The landscape. More than 4,000 disorders are known to have roots in our genes. Normal healthy parents can carry recessive alleles for disorders such as CF and Tay-Sachs disease, a lipid metabolism disorder causing death usually between ages 1 and 5. Where specific tests are unavailable, pedigree analysis lets prospective parents weigh the risk without answering with certainty.
  • Prenatal testing. Usually recommended when a defective gene runs in the family, or when the mother is older than 35 — age raises the risk of homologues failing to separate.
    • Amniocentesis. Amniotic fluid withdrawn by needle; living cells sloughed off the fetus float in it. Usually 15–20 weeks. Finds chromosome-number abnormalities and disease alleles, plus biochemical tests — a high alpha-fetoprotein level suggests a neural tube defect. Small miscarriage risk. Diagnostic.
    • Chorionic villi sampling (CVS). Chorionic villi removed by gentle suction; chorion cells have the same genetic material as fetal cells. Several weeks earlier than amniocentesis, results within a few days. Slightly greater miscarriage risk. Diagnostic.
    • Cell-free fetal DNA. Fetal DNA circulating in the mother's blood. Much earlier than either; primarily trisomy 21, 13 and 18. No miscarriage risk, but a higher false-positive rate. A screen — a positive routes back to amniocentesis or CVS.
    • CVS can prevent a birth defect outright. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia will cause a female fetus to develop abnormal genitalia unless treated with hormones from week 10 to 16; CVS tells a physician whether treatment is needed.
  • Newborn testing — PKU. A simple blood test routinely screens newborns for phenylketonuria. People with PKU produce a defective enzyme and cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine, an imbalance that causes brain damage. The enzyme cannot be corrected, but a strict diet prevents the damage. Foods with aspartame must be avoided — digestion separates it into phenylalanine and aspartic acid.
  • Adult testing. Predictive tests identify people at risk who do not yet have symptoms.
    • Familial adenomatous polyposis — colon cancer develops in nearly everyone with the alleles, so a diagnosed person can be inspected routinely and polyps removed, preventing cancer.
    • Genetic counsellors help people understand their risks and the medical consequences.
    • ApoE, a cholesterol-transport protein, comes in three forms. Two ApoE-2 alleles cause catastrophically high blood cholesterol, so a physician can prescribe cholesterol-lowering medication.

Stop And Think

1Your brother inherited Mom's freckles; you, like your father, do not have them. Why must your mother be heterozygous? (p. 423)

Freckles are dominant, so an unfreckled person must be ff. Your father has no freckles, so he is ff and can only pass f. You have no freckles, so you are ff — meaning you received an f from your mother too. Your mother has freckles, so she carries at least one F; since she also passed an f, she is Ff. A square of Ff × ff gives 1/2 freckled and 1/2 unfreckled — exactly the family described. Had she been FF, every child would have been freckled.

Figure Questions

1If a parent were homozygous recessive for freckles and heterozygous for widow's peak, how many gamete types could form? (Figure 20.6)

Two. The freckle gene contributes only f, so it cannot vary; the hairline gene is Ww and contributes either W or w. That gives fW and fw. The number of gamete types is 2n where n is the number of heterozygous gene pairs — here n = 1.

Ethical Issue

Gene Testing (pp. 432-433)

  • Testing people with no symptoms to determine whether they carry genes influencing their chances of developing certain diseases.
  • For: people who learn they are at risk of a treatable or preventable condition can act. Voluntary Tay-Sachs screening cut the number of affected children tenfold in many communities.
  • Against: the psychological consequences can be devastating, and many disorders can be neither prevented nor treated. Huntington's disease gives no hint of itself until past child-bearing years; about 60% are diagnosed between 35 and 50, with death usually within 10–15 years, and a bearer has a 50% chance of passing it on.
  • Privacy: results may not stay private and could be used by employers and by life and health insurers.

Consider This Case

Cri-du-chat and Sarah's fear (p. 433)

  • Katie and Doug's child Lulu has cri-du-chat. Katie's sister Sarah knows she shares 50% of her genes with Katie and fears her own children may be born with it. Her doctor said that although cri-du-chat is genetic, it is not inherited from either parent.
  • The explanation: "genetic" and "inherited" are not the same thing. Cri-du-chat is a chromosome structural accident — a piece near the tip of chromosome 5 breaks off and fails to travel into the same daughter cell. It arises anew, typically during gamete formation, rather than being carried as an allele.
  • So neither Katie nor Doug carries anything to pass on, and there is nothing for Sarah to have inherited. Sharing 50% of Katie's genes is irrelevant, because the cause is not a gene Katie has.

End Of Chapter Questions

1Which cross could produce offspring with the recessive phenotype?

a) AA × aa b) Aa × Aa c) AA × Aa d) AA × AA

b — Aa × Aa

The recessive phenotype requires an aa child, so both parents must be able to donate a recessive allele. AA × aa gives all Aa — the allele is present but masked. AA × Aa has no a from the AA parent. AA × AA has no a at all.

2All of these have a 50% probability of heterozygous offspring except…

a) AA × aa b) Aa × Aa c) AA × Aa d) Aa × aa

a — AA × aa

Every child receives A from one parent and a from the other, so offspring are 100% heterozygous, not 50%.

3Which is an example of a phenotype?

a) a man with haemophilia b) a female carrier for CF c) XY d) a heterozygote

a — a man with haemophilia

A phenotype is an observable trait. "Carrier", "XY" and "heterozygote" are all statements about which alleles are present, which is genotype.

4Huntington's is caused by a dominant allele. Mother has it, father carries nothing. Probability the first child is normal?

a) 0% b) 25% c) 50% d) 100%

50% — assuming the mother is heterozygous

This question is defective as printed. It does not say whether the mother is homozygous or heterozygous. Hh × hh gives 50% affected; HH × hh gives 100% affected and a 0% chance of a normal child. The intended answer depends on assuming heterozygous. Ask Whalen rather than guessing.

5In the previous example, probability the second child is normal?

The same as the first — 50%

Each conception is independent. A Punnett square gives the probability for each child separately; earlier children do not change later odds.

6How many different gametes could Aabbcc form? (genes on different chromosomes)

a) 2 b) 4 c) 16 d) 64

a — 2

Only the A gene is heterozygous, so 21 = 2 gamete types: Abc and abc.

7How many different gametes could AaBbCc form? (genes on different chromosomes)

a) 2 b) 4 c) 16 d) 64

8 — which is not among the printed options

This question is defective as printed — none of the four choices is correct. Three heterozygous pairs give 23 = 8 gamete types: ABC, ABc, AbC, Abc, aBC, aBc, abC, abc. The printed choices are 2, 4, 16 and 64. Ask Whalen rather than guessing at an answer key.

8Two CF carriers — probability of a child who is also a carrier?

a) 0% b) 25% c) 50% d) 100%

c — 50%

Cc × Cc gives 1 CC : 2 Cc : 1 cc. The heterozygous carriers are 2 of 4.

9A trait controlled by many genes is described as being ______.

polygenic

Distinguish from multiple alleles, which is many versions of one gene in the population, not many genes.

10The ______ is the physical expression of one or more genes, and the ______ is the set of alleles the person possesses.

phenotype; genotype

Phenotype is what you can see; genotype is what is written.

11Two copies of the same allele is ______; two different alleles is ______.

homozygous; heterozygous

Homo = same, hetero = different, zygo = joined together.

12Genes for different traits located on the same chromosome are described as being ______.

linked

Linked genes tend to be inherited together and usually do not assort independently — though crossing over can unlink them.

13Klaus has red-green colour blindness (X-linked recessive); Helen is homozygous for normal vision. Probability of a colour-blind daughter? A colour-blind son?

0% for both

Every daughter gets Klaus's affected X and one normal X from Helen, so all daughters are unaffected carriers. Every son gets Klaus's Y and a normal X from Helen, so no son is affected. A son cannot inherit an X-linked allele from his father.

14George and Sue's infant Sammy has galactosaemia, an autosomal recessive disorder. Chances a second child has it?

25%

Sammy is homozygous recessive, so both parents must be carriers. Cc × Cc gives 1/4 affected — and each pregnancy is independent of the last.

15Why are offspring of first cousins more likely to have harmful recessive traits?

They share recent ancestors, so they are more likely to carry the same rare recessive allele

A harmful recessive shows only when homozygous. Related parents are far more likely both to have inherited the same rare allele from a shared grandparent, so the chance of pairing two copies rises.

16A woman with type AB blood names a man with type O as father of her type AB child. Could he be the father?

No

A type O man is ii and can contribute only i. An AB child must have IA from one parent and IB from the other. The mother can supply only one of those, so the father must supply the other — which ii cannot do.

17Straight hair is incompletely dominant over curly. A curly-haired and a wavy-haired parent have a wavy first child. Probability the second is wavy?

50%

Wavy is the heterozygote under incomplete dominance. Curly (homozygous) × wavy (heterozygous) gives 1/2 curly and 1/2 wavy. Each child is independent, so the first child being wavy does not change the second.

Write

The two halves

  • Two main divisions of the cycle: interphase and the mitotic phase.
  • G1 — cell growth and protein synthesis. S — DNA replication and replication of the centrosome. G2 — further growth and protein synthesis.
  • Interphase is not a rest period. The cell carries out all its everyday functions and prepares to divide.
  • G0 — some specialised cells (most nerve cells) exit late in G1 and never divide again. Liver cells stop but can resume.
  • Checkpoints. The lab treats the G2 checkpoint at length: proofreading catches replication errors before division. If repair fails, the cell signals self-destruction — apoptosis.

  • A mutation can result in a completely nonfunctional protein that is catastrophic for the cell — or, on the other hand, a mutation in structure or function can be beneficial.

2 · Mitosis

Draw

The four stages in order with chromosomes drawn in each — condensing at prophase, single file at the metaphase plate, chromatids pulled apart at anaphase, two nuclei re-forming at telophase. Use the pop-bead colours: yellow for one parental chromosome, red for the other.

  • prophase

  • anaphase
  • telophase
  • prometaphase
  • mitotic spindle
  • metaphase plate
  • centromere
  • sister chromatid
  • kinetochore

Write

P-MAT, by characteristic event

  • Purpose: growth (add new cells) and repair (replace old or damaged cells).
  • Prophase. Chromosomes condense so they can move to opposite poles without tangling; spindle fibres form from the centrosomes; the nuclear membrane breaks down.
  • Metaphase. Spindle fibres attach to the centromere and pull the chromosomes to the equator. One line of 46 replicated chromatid pairs.
  • Anaphase. Spindle fibres shorten, pulling the centromeres apart. Separated sister chromatids are now called chromosomes. 46 go to each end.
  • Telophase. Prophase in reverse — chromosomes decondense, mitotic spindle breaks down, nuclear membrane re-forms. Two distinct nuclei.
  • Cytokinesis overlaps telophase and sometimes the end of anaphase. Actin filaments contract at the equator, forming the cleavage furrow, and pinch the cytoplasm into two.
  • The final product: one diploid (2n) parent cell with 46 replicated chromosomes → two diploid (2n) daughter cells with identical DNA, 46 chromosomes each.
  • Before mitosis the parent cell is still 46 chromosomes (2n), not 92 — it has 92 chromatids. Getting this right is what makes the replication numbers work.

3 · Meiosis

Draw

Two divisions side by side. Meiosis I with homologous pairs at the plate (a doubled row) and whole homologues separating; meiosis II with single chromosomes at the plate and chromatids separating. Mark crossing over at prophase I.

  • meiosis I

Write

The two divisions

  • Purpose: produce gametes. Only in the gonads — ovaries and testes.
  • One diploid cell in, four haploid cells out. Two divisions, each with the same four stages as mitosis.
  • Meiosis I is the reduction division — homologues separate, 2n → n. Meiosis II is the mitosis-like division — sister chromatids separate, n → n.
  • Crossing over happens in prophase I, during synapsis, when homologues are aligned gene by gene.
  • Independent assortment happens at metaphase I — which member of each pair faces which pole is random, and each of the 23 pairs orients independently.
  • Interkinesis: no DNA replication. That is the whole difference from a normal interphase, and the reason the chromosome number halves.
  • Metaphase I vs mitotic metaphase — the single most useful distinction. Mitosis: 46 chromosomes individually, one row. Meiosis I: 23 pairs side by side, a doubled row.
  • Spermatogenesis gives four functional sperm; oogenesis gives one ovum plus polar bodies, because unequal cytoplasmic division keeps the nutrients in the one egg.
  • Nondisjunction — failure to separate, in meiosis I (homologues) or meiosis II (sister chromatids). Gives trisomy or monosomy.

Write

  • A gene is the specific DNA on a chromosome that codes for a particular trait.
  • A trait is the expressed characteristic — the product — that occurs when the gene is transcribed and translated into a protein.

  • A chromosome is a tightly coiled combination of DNA and histone proteins. The DNA carries the information; the histones give support and control gene activity.

Write

The vocabulary pairs

  • Genotype — the combination of alleles present for a particular gene. Phenotype — the trait expression, based on genotype and the relative dominance of the alleles.
  • Homozygous — same alleles (homo = same). Heterozygous — different alleles (hetero = different).

7 · Patterns of Inheritance

Draw

A branching chart of the patterns — complete dominance, codominance, incomplete dominance, multiple alleles, polygenic, pleiotropy — with the lab's ice-cream genotype beside each.

  • complete dominance
  • codominance
  • incomplete dominance
  • multiple alleles
  • polygenic
  • pleiotropy
  • law of segregation

Write

What the heterozygote shows

  • Law of segregation. Homologues separate at meiosis I, so each gamete carries only one allele of each gene. Half the gametes bear one allele, half the other.
  • Law of independent assortment. Each homologous pair lines up independently, so alleles for genes on different chromosomes segregate independently.
  • Complete dominance — heterozygote shows the dominant trait only.
  • Codominance — heterozygote shows both at once. AB blood.
  • Incomplete dominance — heterozygote is intermediate. Hazel eyes (lab) / sickle-cell trait (text).
  • Multiple alleles — three or more versions exist in the population; any individual still has two. ABO.
  • Polygenic — several genes combine to give a continuously varying trait. Height, skin colour.
  • Pleiotropy — one gene, many effects. Sickle-cell damages heart, brain, lungs, kidneys, muscles and joints.

8 · Linked Traits

Draw

Two genes drawn on the same chromosome travelling into a gamete together, next to a crossing-over event that separates them.

  • linked genes
  • crossing over
  • independent assortment
  • homologous pair
  • chromatid

Write

  • Genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together, because the whole chromosome moves into a gamete as a unit. These are linked genes.
  • Linked genes usually do not assort independently — which is the one exception to the second law.
  • "Usually" matters: crossing over can unlink them. The closer two genes sit, the less often a crossover falls between them.
  • Consequence for problems: a two-trait Punnett square giving 9:3:3:1 is valid only if the two genes are on different chromosomes. Drop that qualifier and the answer is wrong.

9 · Sex-Linked Traits

Draw

An X and a Y side by side, drawn to scale, with a gene marked on X that has no counterpart on Y. Beside it a cross showing an affected father passing his X only to daughters.

  • X chromosome
  • Y chromosome
  • X-linked gene
  • carrier
  • sex-influenced trait
  • autosome
  • SRY

Write

  • X and Y are not truly homologous — Y is much smaller and they do not carry all the same genes.
  • Y carries very few genes, but one of them determines male development. Without it the embryo develops female.
  • X has about as many genes as a typical autosome, and most have no counterpart on Y — these are X-linked genes.
  • A male expresses virtually every allele on his single X, recessive ones included. So recessive X-linked phenotypes are much more common in males.
  • A son cannot inherit an X-linked recessive from his father — to be male he took the father's Y, not his X. It can only come from his mother.
  • A heterozygous daughter is a phenotypically normal carrier.
  • Named X-linked recessive disorders: red-green colour blindness, haemophilia A and B, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
  • Sex-linked is not sex-influenced. Sex-influenced traits are autosomal, and hormone level decides whether the allele acts dominant or recessive — male pattern baldness is dominant in males, recessive in females.

Write

The method

  • Step 1: work out the gamete types each parent can make. Step 2: columns are one parent's gametes, rows the other's; each box combines its row and column.
  • Number of gamete types = 2n, where n is the number of heterozygous gene pairs. Aabbcc → 2. AaBbCc → 8.

11 · Use of Pedigree Chart

Draw

  • male (square)
  • female (circle)

Write

The conventions

  • A pedigree is a chart of the genetic connections in a family, used to deduce a genotype that phenotype alone cannot reveal.
  • Conventions: each generation on its own horizontal line, most ancestral at the top; males are squares, females circles; a horizontal line connects mating partners; affected individuals carry a coloured symbol.
  • Autosomal dominant — caused by a dominant allele on a non-sex chromosome. Appears in every generation. Marfan syndrome.
  • Autosomal recessive — can skip generations, and affected children are often born to two unaffected carriers. Cystic fibrosis — about 1 in 22 US Caucasians is a carrier.

L1.2Haploid gametes, diploid soma, and when your gametes were made

2 slides
Big ideaGametes carry one member of each of the 23 pairs; every other cell carries two. A female's entire lifetime supply of eggs is made before she is born.
  • The count, which he made the class say out loud. Eggs and sperm have 23 chromosomes. Every other cell in the body has 46.
    • Gametes are haploid (n) — one member of each homologous pair.
    • Somatic cells are diploid (2n) — two copies of every chromosome, one from each parent.
  • Homologous chromosomes. Mom's chromosome 1 and Dad's chromosome 1. Same genes, same positions — but the versions of those genes need not be the same.
  • Oogenesis timing, which he spent real time on.
    • A female makes all the eggs she will ever have while she is still developing inside her mother.
    • She is born with about 25% of them; the other ~75% are reabsorbed.
    • They sit in stasis until menarche, the first menstrual cycle, and then the timer restarts.
    • The eggs have a usable lifespan and are as healthy now as they will ever be. He returns to this in L1.9 as the mechanism behind maternal age and nondisjunction.

L1.3Two divisions, and what each one separates

2 slides
Big ideaMeiosis I separates homologues; meiosis II separates sister chromatids. Both run the same four phases, and in anaphase you are always separating something.
  • Meiosis is two rounds of nuclear division and two rounds of cell division, giving four genetically unique haploid daughter cells.
  • Why it exists, in his two reasons:
    • Keeps the chromosome number constant from generation to generation.
    • Increases genetic variability in the reproductive population — and in the population as a whole.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes from one another. Diploid in, haploid out.
  • Meiosis II separates sister chromatids from one another — and it "happens just like mitosis", except starting with 23 chromosomes instead of 46.
  • The same four phases run in both, and in mitosis: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase. He asked the class to be able to say what distinguishes one from the next.
  • His rule for anaphase. "Always in anaphase we're separating things from each other." Anaphase I separates homologues; anaphase II separates sister chromatids.
Exam tip

Lecture He ran the "which phase separates sister chromatids?" question as a talk-to-your-neighbour, then asked it again four more times through the session in different wordings. Anaphase I = homologues. Anaphase II = sister chromatids. He said outright he was asking the same question four different ways because he wanted it understood.

L1.4Meiosis I, stage by stage

7 slides
Big ideaPreceded by S phase, meiosis I condenses and pairs the homologues, crosses them over, lines the pairs up two abreast, then pulls the pairs apart — a reduction division.
  • Preceded by interphase. He pushed the class hard on this: the DNA was copied during S phase ("synthesis — that's another name for replication"). Interphase is everything outside the M phase.
    • The identical copies are joined at the centromere as sister chromatids.
  • Prophase I.
    • Chromosomes condense and supercoil — from chromatin, which is uncondensed DNA, to 46 individually identifiable chromosomes you could karyotype.
    • Synapsis — homologous chromosomes pair up.
    • The paired homologues form a tetrad: four chromatids together (tetra = four).
    • Crossing over — non-sister chromatids literally cross and swap parts.
  • Metaphase I.
    • Homologous pairs line up at the metaphase plate, two lines of 23 — 46 chromosomes at the midline.
    • He stressed the mix: some paternal chromosomes on one side, some on the other. Not all of one parent's on one side.
    • All attached to spindle fibers, which come from the centrosome — the microtubule assembly factory from Ch. 3. Spindle fibers are microtubules.
  • Anaphase I. The homologues of each pair are pulled to opposite poles.
  • Telophase I. Nuclear envelopes reform; cytokinesis cuts the cell in two. Result: two haploid daughter cells that are no longer genetically identical.
  • Interkinesis. A very short break before meiosis II.
    • No DNA replication.
    • The chromosomes stay supercoiled — they do not decondense back to chromatin.
Watch out

Lecture At metaphase I there are 46 chromosomes at the midline in 23 pairs — he called it out as "not a trick question" and the class still hesitated. At metaphase II there are 23.

Lecture No replication in interkinesis and the chromosomes stay condensed. Both are easy marks and both differ from a normal interphase.

L1.5Meiosis II, stage by stage

7 slides
Big ideaMeiosis II runs like mitosis on a haploid cell — 23 chromosomes line up, the centromeres tear, and four genetically unique haploid cells result.
  • Prophase II. Chromosomes condense; nuclear membrane breaks down.
  • Metaphase II. 23 chromosomes line up along the midline — "some are red, some are blue", his shorthand for maternal and paternal after crossing over.
  • Anaphase II. Spindle fibers pull until the chromatid tears at the centromere. "As soon as we tear and become separated, I am no longer a chromatid. I am a chromosome again."
    • 23 chromosomes go each way.
  • Telophase II. Nuclear membranes reform at opposite poles; cytokinesis pinches the cell in two.
  • The result: four genetically unique haploid daughter cells.
  • Why they are unique even before fertilisation. Even lined up at metaphase II, the two sister chromatids were already not identical, because of crossing over in prophase I.
Exam tip

Lecture His naming rule, said as the chromatids separate: a chromatid becomes a chromosome the moment it is separated. That renaming is a common exam trip.

L1.6Mitosis vs. meiosis, side by side

3 slides
Big ideaMitosis gives two identical diploid cells; meiosis I is the only reduction division; meiosis II resembles mitosis but starts and ends haploid.
  • Both start the same way — a cell with duplicated chromosomes, two chromatids each.
  • The difference is how they line up. In meiosis I the homologues pair and come to the midline together. In mitosis the homologues move independently of each other.
  • Crossing over is a meiosis thing. He pointed at the animation: "my blue chromosome got some red on it — did you even see that happen? That doesn't happen in mitosis."
  • Mitosis → two genetically identical diploid cells, each with two complete sets.
  • Meiosis I → two genetically unique haploid cells, each with one set. This is the reduction division.
  • Meiosis II → from one of those, two genetically unique haploid cells; four in total counting both.
  • Neither mitosis nor meiosis II is a reduction division. "Only in meiosis I is the number of chromosomes divided in half."
Watch out

Lecture Meiosis I is the reduction division — not meiosis II, not mitosis. He said this three separate ways while walking Figure 19.10.

L1.7Spermatogenesis and oogenesis

3 slides
Big ideaThe same four cells become four sperm in a male but one egg and up to three polar bodies in a female, because the egg keeps everything.
  • Spermatogenesis — four haploid cells become four sperm, highly specialised for transport. The sperm's job is to deliver genetic information to the egg.
  • Oogenesis — one ovum plus up to three polar bodies, which "go on to become nothing".
    • The one egg keeps everything: all the organelles, all the mitochondria, all the cytoplasm, all the nutrients, and its 23 chromosomes.
    • The sperm delivers DNA. It injects its nucleus.
  • Fertilisation. The two nuclei swell into pronuclei, then fuse into one nucleus with two copies of every chromosome.
  • Where meiosis happens: only in the gonads. He made the class say it. It runs continuously in the male.
  • The egg is arrested. In answer to a student question: meiosis does not complete in the female until fertilisation occurs. The egg is stuck in meiosis II until fertilised; only then does it finish, giving rise to the last polar body, and then the nuclei fuse.

L1.8The two sources of variation

5 slides
Big ideaCrossing over swaps matching pieces between non-sister chromatids in prophase I; independent assortment randomises which side of the plate each homologue takes in metaphase I. They are different events in different phases.
  • Crossing over.
    • Happens in prophase I, between non-sister chromatids — maternal with paternal.
    • The pieces exchanged are corresponding pieces: the exact same genes at the exact same loci. What differs is the allele — the variation of the gene.
    • Alleles are different versions of the same gene.
  • Independent assortment.
    • Happens at metaphase I. Which side of the metaphase plate a given homologue takes has no bearing on what any other pair does.
    • Not all maternal chromosomes end up on one side. With 23 pairs orienting independently, the number of combinations is enormous.
    • The requirement is only that one of each pair goes each way.
  • Why variation matters, in his words. If everyone is the same, everyone thrives in the same conditions — and when conditions change, everyone suffers the same way. Variation means some are predisposed to survive the obstacle.
Watch out

Lecture Crossing over = prophase I, between non-sister chromatids. Independent assortment = metaphase I, between pairs. Two events, two phases, two mechanisms. He asked which was which more than once.

Exam tip

Lecture "You should be able to compare and contrast mitosis to meiosis — what they consist of, what they make, where they happen, why we do them, and whether there is an exchange of genetic information between homologues." That is close to a printed exam question.

L1.9Nondisjunction, aneuploidy and the syndromes

8 slides
Big ideaA pair that fails to separate leaves one cell with an extra chromosome and one short — usually lethal, and the risk climbs with maternal age.
  • What goes wrong. In anaphase I, 22 pairs separate normally but for one chromosome both homologues go the same direction. One cell ends with 24 chromosomes, the other with 22.
  • Nondisjunction is the failure to separate — homologues in meiosis I, or sister chromatids in meiosis II.
  • Aneuploidy — "the wrong number, or an atypical number, of chromosomes". The imbalance usually causes developmental abnormalities, possibly miscarriage.
  • Trisomy — three representatives of a chromosome in a cell. Monosomy — only one.
  • Down syndrome = trisomy 21. He was categorical in both directions: everyone with three copies of chromosome 21 has Down syndrome, and everyone with Down syndrome has an extra copy of 21. The cause is the imbalance, and that is the only reason.
  • Maternal age. Risk of nondisjunction rises from around 35, because the eggs have been in storage since before birth — see L1.2. Older eggs, more nondisjunction, more trisomy and monosomy.
  • Monosomy is lethal, with one exception. "Immediate miscarriage. That is lethal." Except XO — Turner syndrome, which he called the only non-lethal monosomy he knows of.
    • She has one X instead of two, develops largely normally, but is sterile — gamete production never works out.
  • Sex-chromosome nondisjunction. Sperm can end up carrying both X and Y, or no sex chromosome at all.
    • Turner syndrome: XO. Klinefelter: XXY — two X's like a female plus a Y; as long as SRY is functional he develops as male, with abnormalities in development and adulthood.
  • Who supplies the Y. Half of a male's sperm carry X, half carry Y. All eggs carry X. He made a point of the history: women were blamed for centuries for failing to produce a son, and only the father can supply a Y.
Watch out

Lecture Turner syndrome (XO) is the only non-lethal monosomy he named. Every other monosomy he described as an immediate miscarriage.

L1.13Chapter 20 opens: alleles, loci, genotype and phenotype

9 slides
Big ideaA gene is a segment of DNA at a fixed address; alleles are its variants; genotype is what you carry and phenotype is what shows.
  • The setup. Genetic information is carried on chromosomes, in equal numbers in egg and sperm — 23 each. Fertilisation creates a combination that has never existed before.
  • Traits are physical characteristics: the interplay between the proteins made and the environment, internal or external. Produced by the action of one or more genes directing proteins.
    • He flagged polygenic traits — many genes involved — as coming later, and said he would keep it simple first.
  • Alleles — different forms of a gene, producing different versions of the protein and so different versions of the trait. His example: an allele for freckles, and an allele for no freckles.
  • Locus — the address of a gene on a chromosome. Plural loci. He asked for the word specifically.
    • Each member of a homologous pair carries the same genes at the same loci; the alleles at those loci may differ.
  • Homozygous — two copies of the same allele. Heterozygous — one copy of each.
  • Dominant — the effect is detected regardless of the other allele. Recessive — only seen when two are present.
  • Genotype — the alleles present, the genetic composition. Phenotype — the physical trait you can observe.
    • Capital letter = dominant allele. Lowercase = recessive allele. FF, Ff, ff.
    • Homozygous dominant → dominant phenotype. Heterozygous → dominant phenotype. Homozygous recessive → recessive phenotype, the only time the recessive shows.
  • His dominant-trait examples: freckles, widow's peak, unattached (free, floppy) earlobes, tongue rolling. Recessive: no freckles, straight hairline, attached earlobes, inability to roll the tongue.
Watch out

Lecture On the heterozygous phenotype he stopped himself: "Dominant — in most instances, not always. I'll give you the exceptions." Codominance, incomplete dominance and pleiotropy are all in the Ch20 deck (§20.1) and were not covered here. Do not over-generalise from this session.

L1.14Segregation, independent assortment, and Mendel

3 slides
Big ideaThe two Mendelian laws are meiosis restated in allele terms — segregation is anaphase I, independent assortment is metaphase I.
  • Law of segregation. During gamete formation, the two alleles for each gene separate as the homologous chromosomes move to opposite poles.
    • He drove the class to the specific phase: anaphase I, which implies meiosis I.
  • Law of independent assortment. Each chromosome is inherited independently of the others. Which side of the line a homologue takes has no bearing on any of the other 22 pairs.
  • Gregor Mendel.
    • A monk, 1800s, in what is now the Czech Republic — then Austria.
    • Studied how single genes are inherited using several plants, most notably pea plants.
    • One-trait cross = monohybrid. Two-trait cross = dihybrid.
    • He worked roughly 100 years before Watson and Crick, and before DNA was confirmed as the genetic material (1940s onward) — which is the part Whalen was impressed by.
  • What is coming Wednesday. He closed by naming it: the Punnett square — what it is, how it is used, and practice predicting offspring genotypes and phenotypes from parental ones, and reading backwards from a child to the parents.
Exam tip

Lecture Segregation = anaphase I. He asked "be specific" and would not accept "meiosis". The two Mendelian laws are the two meiosis events from L1.8 under different names — learning them as one thing halves the work.

L2.2Punnett squares, and the two ratios that always hold

9 slides
Big ideaRows are one parent's gametes, columns the other's; a heterozygous monohybrid cross is 1:2:1 by genotype and 3:1 by phenotype, every time.
  • What the square is. A matrix predicting the genetic makeup of offspring from two known genotypes. Rows = one parent, columns = the other. Each allele drops into every box in its row or column, producing every possible combination.
  • Why it works. It is the law of segregation drawn out — alleles separate because the chromosomes carrying them separate.
  • The asymmetry that matters. Genotype tells you phenotype. Phenotype does not tell you genotype. Seeing the dominant trait tells you there is at least one dominant allele and nothing about the other.
  • Homozygous dominant × homozygous recessive. All offspring heterozygous, all showing the dominant phenotype. None can show the recessive.
  • Monohybrid cross, both parents heterozygous (Ff × Ff):
    • Genotypic ratio 1 : 2 : 1 — one homozygous dominant, two heterozygous, one homozygous recessive.
    • Phenotypic ratio 3 : 1 — "always, always, always" — because the recessive phenotype needs both recessive alleles.
  • This class does monohybrid crosses only. One trait.
  • Dihybrid, shown but not assessed. Both parents heterozygous for two traits (FfWw × FfWw) → a 4×4, 16-square grid and a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotypic ratio, every time. Each parent makes four kinds of gamete.
  • How the grid grows. Monohybrid 4 squares, dihybrid 16, trihybrid 64.
Exam tip

Lecture 1:2:1 genotypic, 3:1 phenotypic for a heterozygous monohybrid cross. He repeated "always" three times. 9:3:3:1 for the dihybrid, which he showed but excluded from this class.

Lecture "Genotype gives you phenotype; phenotype does not give you genotype." He built the whole pedigree section on this — expect it as a question in words rather than as a square.

L2.3Pedigree charts

2 slides
Big ideaSquares are male, circles female, shaded affected — and the point is deducing genotypes you cannot see, including where the honest answer is "not enough information".
  • The notation. Squares = male. Circles = female. Shaded = affected. Unshaded = unaffected. Multiple generations, read top to bottom.
  • Why bother. Pedigrees are "especially useful in following recessive alleles" — the ones you cannot see in a heterozygous carrier.
  • His worked dominant-trait example. Unaffected father × affected mother, six children, three affected.
    • Dad is unaffected, so he is homozygous recessive and can only give a recessive allele.
    • Each affected child must therefore be heterozygous (Mm) — the only way to show a dominant trait with a recessive-only father.
    • Each unaffected child is homozygous recessive (mm).
  • The rule underneath it. The only way to be affected by a dominant trait is to carry at least one dominant allele. The only way to be affected by a recessive trait is to be homozygous recessive.
  • Carriers. Heterozygous, showing the dominant phenotype, carrying the recessive allele and able to pass it on without ever suffering it. This is why recessive traits appear to skip generations.
  • His worked recessive-trait example. Two unaffected parents, four children, two affected.
    • Both affected children are cc.
    • Both parents must therefore be carriers, Cc — it takes two recessive alleles to be affected.
    • The unaffected siblings cannot be resolved. Each is either CC or Cc. "Don't know. Can't tell. Not enough information."
  • He used himself as the example. He is red-green colourblind — X-linked recessive, inherited from his mother, since the gene sits on the X and his only X came from her.
Watch out

Lecture "Not enough information" is a real answer and he wants it given. An unaffected child of two carriers is CC or Cc and the pedigree cannot distinguish them. Guessing one is wrong; saying you cannot tell is right.

Exam tip

Lecture Work pedigrees from the homozygous recessive individuals outward — they are the only genotypes you can read directly off the chart, and everything else is deduced from what their parents must have been able to give.

L2.4Genetic disorders, carriers, and albinism

3 slides
Big ideaMost genetic disorders are recessive because the dominant allele usually makes a working protein and the recessive one makes a broken version of the same protein.
  • Genetic disorders are usually caused by recessive alleles, which is why carriers exist and why the trait can hide for generations.
  • A carrier displays the dominant phenotype, is heterozygous, carries the recessive allele, does not suffer the condition, and can pass it to their children.
  • The mechanism, generalised. The dominant allele often produces a functional protein; the recessive allele produces one that does not work properly. Dominance is usually "having at least one working copy".
  • Albinism, his worked example.
    • The inability to produce melanin, the brown pigment, made in specialised cells called melanocytes in skin and hair.
    • The dominant allele produces functional tyrosinase, the enzyme that acts on the amino acid tyrosine.
    • The recessive allele produces a tyrosinase that does not function — something is wrong with the protein, so it cannot act on tyrosine.
  • The student question he answered with it. Two dark-skinned parents having a child with albinism is not a contradiction — the child cannot make melanin at all, which is what produces the colour.
Exam tip

Lecture Albinism is his template for why recessive means recessive — one functional enzyme copy is enough. If you can retell tyrosinase → tyrosine → melanin, you can reconstruct the logic for most recessive disorders.

L2.5Complete dominance, codominance, incomplete dominance

3 slides
Big ideaThree different things can happen in a heterozygote — you see only the dominant, you see both in full, or you see a blend.
  • Complete dominance. The heterozygote shows the dominant allele's trait and the recessive is not seen at all.
  • Codominance. Both alleles are expressed, in full, at the same time — not a blend.
    • His flower example: red × white gives white flowers with red on them, or red flowers with white on them. Both expressed in their entirety.
    • ABO blood typing is the course's main codominance example — see L2.6.
  • Incomplete dominance. The heterozygote shows something in between the two.
    • Red × white gives pink. Homozygous dominant = red, homozygous recessive = white, heterozygous = pink.
  • A student asked what decides which applies to a given gene. His answer: that is beyond the scope of this class. Some genes have a clear dominant; for others neither allele is dominant over the other, and that is where codominance and incomplete dominance come from. It operates at a different level than the Punnett square.
Watch out

Lecture Codominance is both, in full. Incomplete dominance is a blend. Red-and-white speckled versus pink. He was asked this directly and answered it twice — it is the distinction most likely to be tested.

Exam tip

Lecture He explicitly declined to explain why a given gene behaves one way. Do not lose time on it; know the three patterns and their signature outcomes.

L2.6Multiple alleles: the ABO blood groups

4 slides
Big ideaThree alleles, one gene. I^A and I^B are codominant with each other and both dominant to i, which gives four phenotypes and the whole transfusion table.
  • Multiple alleles means three or more forms of a given gene exist across the population — though any one person still carries only two.
  • What determines the type. Polysaccharides (antigens) on the surface of red blood cells. Type A has the A polysaccharide, B has B, AB has both, O has neither.
  • The three alleles: I^A, I^B and i.
    • I^A and I^B are codominant with one another.
    • Both are dominant to i.
    • He noted he prefers writing lowercase i rather than leaving it blank.
  • The genotypes:
    • Type A — I^A I^A or I^A i
    • Type B — I^B I^B or I^B i
    • Type AB — I^A I^B, the only way
    • Type O — ii, the only way
  • The Rh factor is a separate antigen — the D antigen — giving the positive / negative. Eight combinations in all: A±, B±, AB±, O±.
  • Transfusion logic, as he ran it.
    • Type O has neither antigen, so anyone can receive it — the universal donor.
    • A type O person makes antibodies against both A and B, so giving them A or B blood makes it clot and can kill them. They can only receive O.
    • Type A can receive A or O. Type B can receive B or O.
    • Type AB can receive from anyone — the universal recipient — but can only donate to AB.
  • The lab is coming. A spring-loaded lancet finger prick, a drop of blood onto a card with anti-serum, and clumping (agglutination) or no clumping tells you which antigens are present.
  • Extra credit. Bring in paperwork showing your blood type for 10 points. He noted that is 5% of a 200-point exam.
Exam tip

Lecture 10 points of extra credit for documentation of your blood type. He offered it unprompted and pointed out it is worth about a letter grade on an assignment. Free marks.

Watch out

Lecture He warned the class not to trust the lab result over paperwork — the classroom anti-sera can be degraded. If the card disagrees with your records, believe your records.

Exam tip

Lecture ABO is the exam's codominance example and its multiple-alleles example at once. AB and O are the diagnostic genotypes: each has exactly one possible genotype, while A and B each have two.

L2.7Pleiotropy and sickle-cell anemia

5 slides
Big ideaOne typo in the beta-globin gene sickles red cells under low oxygen, and because blood goes everywhere, one gene produces failures in every organ.
  • Pleiotropy — one gene having many effects. Sickle-cell is the course's example.
  • The molecular cause. A typo in a single gene — the one coding the beta-globin chain of hemoglobin — swapping one amino acid. He hedged on which way round the substitution runs.
  • The trigger is hypoxia. Carriers and affected individuals are usually fine day to day; low oxygen sets it off — high elevation, hard exercise, athletic exertion.
  • What happens. Red cells change shape — "instead of being shaped like an orange, they get shaped like a banana" — and no longer fit through the capillaries, the smallest vessels. They lodge, blood backs up behind them, and a clot forms.
  • Where the clot lands is which disease you get — his pleiotropy argument:
    • Lungs → pulmonary embolism; the region is ventilated but not perfused, so you cannot catch your breath.
    • Brain → stroke, and "those are forever".
    • Coronary artery → heart attack.
    • Kidney → filtration efficiency drops.
    • Spleen → sickled cells accumulate and break down there.
    • Muscle → tissue death; damaged myoglobin filters through the kidneys and discolours the urine.
  • How it is diagnosed, from his clinical aside: chest X-ray first to exclude obstruction, then a V/Q scan — an inhaled radioactive aerosol for ventilation, injected radioactivity for perfusion, the two images overlaid to find a lobe segment getting air but no blood.
  • Trait versus disease. Three outcomes: normal red cells, sickle-cell trait (carrier — very unlikely to sickle unless severe hypoxia), or sickle-cell anemia. Affected individuals face repeated hospitalisation and blood thinners, and cannot pursue athletic careers.
  • The upside: malaria resistance. Carriers are resistant. He used this to open a long tangent on why malaria — which he called 100% preventable — has not been eradicated by GMO mosquitoes, and on sterile-male mosquito release as the smaller-scale approach actually in use.
Watch out

He got the substitution backwards, and said so himself. He said "I think we switch out a valine for a glutamic acid" — hedged. The actual change is glutamic acid → valine at position 6 of the beta-globin chain. Learn it in that direction.

Exam tip

Lecture Sickle-cell is the answer to both "pleiotropy" and "incomplete dominance" in this chapter, and he flagged that dual role explicitly. The clot-location list is the pleiotropy argument — one gene, six organs.

L2.8Polygenic inheritance

3 slides
Big ideaHeight, skin colour and eye colour are each controlled by several genes plus the environment, which is why they form smooth bell curves rather than categories.
  • Polygenic inheritance — variation in a trait that always involves more than one gene.
  • He retracted the simple model directly. "We've talked about eye colour — big B or little B, brown or blue. It's not that easy. I was lying to you." At least five genes are involved in eye colour.
  • The evidence is the variation itself. Brown eyes are not all one brown; blue eyes are not all one blue; hazel and green are not intermediate versions of either. An iris close-up shows flecks of gold, yellow, black and several browns at once.
  • Environment layers on top. UV exposure raises melanocyte activity and darkens skin — which is why you tan in summer and are a different colour in winter.
  • Melanin is not one pigment, which he previewed for Ch. 4:
    • Eumelanin — brown to black.
    • Pheomelanin — red to brown.
    • Carotene — yellow to orange.
    • Your natural colour is the blend, visible only where the sun does not reach.
  • How melanin protects. Packaged into vesicles called melanosomes and passed to other cells, where they shield the stem cells in the bottom layer of the epidermis from UV. Damage to that single layer propagates into every cell derived from it — that is how skin cancer starts, carcinoma and melanoma, which he flagged for Ch. 4.
  • The signature of a polygenic trait: a bell curve. Most of the population near the average, tails at both ends, genetic and environmental contributions both present.
Exam tip

Lecture A bell-curve distribution is the giveaway for polygenic inheritance. Discrete categories mean one gene; a smooth continuum means several plus environment.

Exam tip

Lecture The melanin material here is Ch. 4 preview, delivered a week before the chapter, and he said so. It will be lectured properly in Week 6 — but he has now said it twice.

L2.9Linked genes

1 slides
Big ideaGenes on the same chromosome travel together and break the law of independent assortment — and the only thing that unlinks them is crossing over in prophase I.
  • Linked genes sit on the same chromosome and are usually inherited together.
  • They do not assort independently, which is the exception to Mendel's second law.
  • Distance decides linkage strength. Genes very close together almost never have a crossover between them. The further apart they are, the more likely a crossover falls between them — and that is how linkage distance is measured.
  • When a crossover does occur between them, they unlink — one stays on the parental chromosome, the other moves to the non-sister chromatid.
  • When can genes be unlinked? Prophase I. Only prophase I. "No other time. That's when crossing over is happening."
Watch out

Lecture He stopped the class here and said: "If that doesn't click, please go back and review." Then he named the exam scope in the same breath — Ch. 1, 2, 3, 19, 20, 21 and Labs 1–4. This is the moment he told you what to study.

Exam tip

Lecture Prophase I is now the answer to three separate questions in this week — where crossing over happens, where synapsis happens, and where linked genes are unlinked. One phase, three marks.

L2.10Sex-linked genes

5 slides
Big ideaThe X carries genes the Y has no counterpart for, so a male's single X is unopposed and X-linked recessive conditions show up far more often in men.
  • Everyone has an X. "You ain't got one, you're not here." Some have one, some two, some three. One Y makes you a genetic male regardless of how many X's accompany it.
  • The asymmetry. The X is a full-sized chromosome carrying a normal complement of genes. The Y is tiny with very few active genes and, beyond triggering male development, does little.
  • X-linked means the gene is on the X and the Y has no corresponding gene, so a male has no second copy to mask it.
  • Why X-linked recessive conditions are more common in males. A son's X can only have come from his mother — his father gave him the Y. A father passes either an X or a Y, never both.
  • His own case: red-green colourblindness. The red and green cones are encoded on the X; his came from his mother. He can distinguish red from green in daily life but cannot read Ishihara dot plates.
  • His other examples:
    • Hemophilia — the blood does not clot; he called them "bleeders", and noted the finger-prick lab could reveal an unknown case if bleeding continues 30 minutes later.
    • Duchenne muscular dystrophy — breakdown of the protein dystrophin, so sarcomeres and muscle fibres degrade, causing muscle wasting and eventual paralysis.
  • The carrier-mother cross he walked. Carrier mother (one normal X, one affected X) × unaffected father (normal X, normal Y) gives four outcomes: one unaffected daughter, one carrier daughter, one unaffected son, and one affected son — the one who received the affected X from his mother and a Y from his father.
Exam tip

Lecture A son's X always comes from his mother. That single fact generates the entire X-linked inheritance pattern, and he asked the class to justify it rather than recite it.

Exam tip

Lecture Three named X-linked conditions: red-green colourblindness, hemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy. He gave a mechanism for each.

L2.11Sex-influenced genes

2 slides
Big ideaSex-influenced genes are autosomal, not sex-linked — the same genotype expresses differently because males and females carry different hormone levels.
  • The distinction he drew. Sex-influenced genes are on the first 22 pairs — the autosomes. They are not on a sex chromosome. What differs is that their expression is influenced by sex hormones.
  • The hormones — testosterone, estrogen, progesterone. Everyone has all of them, in different amounts, and that difference is what changes expression.
  • Male pattern baldness, his worked example.
    • Expression depends on both the presence of the allele and the presence of testosterone.
    • A heterozygous male can show pattern baldness, because his testosterone level is high enough.
    • A homozygous female will typically show it only later in life — her estrogen masks the effect for decades, and as estrogen declines the testosterone that was always present begins to show. He mentioned chin whiskers as the same effect.
Watch out

Lecture Sex-influenced ≠ sex-linked. Sex-influenced is autosomal with hormonal modulation; sex-linked is physically on the X or Y. He set them side by side deliberately, which is exactly how a question would set them.

Exam tip

His timeline for declining estrogen ("gone by 50") is a rough teaching simplification, not a clinical figure. The mechanism — masking, then unmasking — is the examinable part.

L2.12Breaks in chromosomes

5 slides
Big ideaDeletions lose a piece and duplications add one; both wreck the addressing system the cell uses to find a gene.
  • Causes. Chemicals, radiation, viruses — collectively mutagens. The result is a change in the structure and function of the chromosome.
  • Deletion — a piece of the chromosome is lost.
    • His analogy: breaking a plate and gluing back 99 of 100 pieces. It is not what it was, and the missing piece may have mattered.
    • Cri du chat — a break losing the tip of chromosome 5. Named for the infant's cry, which he described as sounding like a hurt kitten. Lifelong mental, emotional and physical challenges.
  • Duplication — an extra piece is added.
    • The damage is to addressing. If a gene should sit four reading frames from the centromere and a repeat has been inserted, counting to that position lands on the wrong thing. The gene is still present but effectively unfindable when it is needed.
    • Fragile X syndrome — a duplication of a region on the X chromosome producing a visible fragile site.
Exam tip

Lecture Deletion → cri du chat, chromosome 5. Duplication → Fragile X. One example each, both named, both likely.

Exam tip

Lecture His framing for duplication is worth keeping: the harm is not the extra material itself but that everything downstream shifts address. That connects back to reading frames from Ch. 21.

L2.13Detecting genetic disorders

6 slides
Big ideaFour testing routes, each trading information against risk — and the maternal-age argument for testing at all is the egg-storage story from Lecture 1.
  • When he would recommend prenatal testing. A defective gene running in the family — established by a pedigree — or a mother older than 35.
  • Why 35. Eggs have been in storage since before birth. By the mid-thirties they are near the end of their usable life, and nondisjunction becomes more common — a low baseline of a few percent, rising sharply and "almost exponentially" after 35. His advice: have children earlier, or be prepared.
  • Amniocentesis. Draw amniotic fluid, which contains fetal cells. Culture them to proliferate, then karyotype. Looking for abnormal chromosome numbers — trisomies, and monosomies like Turner syndrome (XO), which he again named as the only non-lethal monosomy.
  • Chorionic villus sampling (CVS). Take a piece of the chorionic villi — the finger-like projections of the chorion, revisited in Ch. 23. Those cells share the fetus's genetic makeup. He would not choose it: it carries a slightly greater risk of triggering a miscarriage than amniocentesis, and he spoke about a loss in his own family.
  • Non-invasive screening — cell-free fetal DNA in the mother's blood. Fetal DNA circulates in maternal blood and persists for decades. Can be done much earlier and risks no miscarriage, but has a higher rate of false positives.
  • Newborn testing — PKU. All newborns are screened. A child with phenylketonuria cannot metabolise the amino acid phenylalanine; the entire management is a strict lifelong diet, which is why detecting it at birth matters.
  • Adult predictive testing. Commercially available and expanding (he referenced 23andMe). Identifies risk long before symptoms. He put the ethical question to the class — would you want to know a diagnosis with a fixed onset and a short course? — and let them argue it.
  • Risk alleles are probabilities, not verdicts. An allele raising breast-cancer risk by 800% means eight times more likely, not certain. He set that against the ~1 in 8 baseline lifetime risk, and noted these cancers are driven partly by the very hormones of normal development.
Exam tip

Lecture The trade-off is the answer. CVS = earlier than amnio but higher miscarriage risk. Cell-free DNA = earliest and safest but more false positives. Amniocentesis = the reference standard. A question here will be about which you would choose and why.

Exam tip

Lecture Turner syndrome (XO), the only non-lethal monosomy — he has now said this in both Week 4 lectures. Twice-said is exam-flagged.

The week, told once

Week 4 is one mechanism told three times. Chromosomes behave a particular way during meiosis; genetics is that behaviour restated in allele terms; and every disorder in the week is that behaviour going wrong — either the wrong number of chromosomes (nondisjunction), the wrong structure (deletion, duplication), or the wrong allele.

Get that framing right and most of the week collapses into one idea. Ch. 19 describes homologues separating at meiosis I and sister chromatids separating at meiosis II. Ch. 20's two laws are literally just that: segregation is homologues separating, and independent assortment is each pair orienting at random. A Punnett square is a bookkeeping device for meiosis.

  • Two divisions, two jobs. Mitosis → two identical diploid cells, everywhere in the body, for growth and repair. Meiosis → four unique haploid gametes, only in the gonads.
  • The reduction is in meiosis I. Homologues separate, 2n → n. Meiosis II is the mitosis-like one — sister chromatids separate, n → n. Reversing these two is the single most common error in the week.
  • Variation comes from two places, at two stages. Crossing over in prophase I; independent assortment at metaphase I.
  • Alleles are just the two versions of a gene sitting at the same locus on homologues. Homozygous/heterozygous, dominant/recessive, genotype/phenotype are all names for configurations of those two.
  • Simple dominance is the exception, not the rule. Codominance (both show), incomplete dominance (intermediate), multiple alleles, polygenic inheritance and pleiotropy are all the ways one-gene-one-either-or-trait breaks down.
  • Independent assortment breaks too. Linked genes ride the same chromosome; X-linked genes have no partner on Y; sex-influenced genes need a hormone to be expressed.
  • Three failure modes, three chapters' worth of disorders. Wrong number — Down, Turner, Klinefelter, triple-X, Jacob. Wrong structure — cri-du-chat, Fragile X. Wrong allele — cystic fibrosis, Marfan, sickle-cell, albinism, haemophilia.
  • Stem cells (19a) are the counterweight. Everything else this week is division going wrong; 19a is division deliberately harnessed. Its two axes — potency and rejection risk — both point the same way: earlier source, more useful and less likely to be attacked.

  4. Interkinesis has no DNA replication — that's why the number halves.
  5. Mitotic metaphase: 46 chromosomes in one row. Metaphase I: 23 pairs, a doubled row.
  6. DNA replicates in interphase (S phase), never in prophase.
  7. One molecule, three names — chromosome, then two sister chromatids, then chromosomes again after anaphase.
  8. Mitosis divides the nucleus; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. They overlap, and cytokinesis starts during telophase.
  9. Four stages of mitosis. Fold prometaphase into prophase, whatever the lab figure shows.
  10. Nondisjunction = failure to separate. Trisomy = three copies, monosomy = one.
  11. Five sex-chromosome/trisomy conditions: Down (21), Turner (XO), Klinefelter (XXY), triple-X (XXX), Jacob (XYY).
  12. ~70% of miscarriages involve an abnormal chromosome number.
  13. Karyotype = arrest at metaphase with a spindle-destroying drug, then arrange by size and centromere position.
  14. Segregation = homologues separate. Independent assortment = only for genes on different chromosomes.
  15. Monohybrid 3:1. Dihybrid 9:3:3:1. Gamete types = 2n, n = heterozygous pairs.
  16. Each child is independent — the square doesn't remember the last one.
  17. Codominance = both show (AB blood). Incomplete dominance = intermediate (hazel eyes in lab, sickle-cell trait in text).
  18. Pleiotropy = one gene, many effects. Polygenic = many genes, one trait.
  19. Linked genes don't assort independently — but crossing over can unlink them.
  20. A son can't get an X-linked allele from his father. He got the Y.
  21. Sex-linked ≠ sex-influenced. Baldness is autosomal and hormone-dependent.
  22. A carrier looks normal. That's why CF children are born to healthy parents.
  23. Deletion and duplication are structural — nothing there is dominant or recessive, and neither parent carries anything.
  24. Cri-du-chat = chromosome 5 deletion, 1 in 50,000. Fragile X = repeat expansion, commonest inherited mental impairment.
  25. A small duplication is less harmful than a deletion of the same size.
  26. CVS is earlier but riskier than amniocentesis. Cell-free fetal DNA is earliest and safest but is a screen, not a diagnosis.
  27. PKU — the enzyme isn't fixed, the diet just removes the substrate.
