Did you know

  • You had more bones when you were born (perhaps as many as 300) than you do now (perhaps as few as 206), because some bones fuse as you age.
  • The thighbone (femur) accounts for about 25% of your height.

OPENING

The previous chapter covered the organ systems. This chapter considers the skeletal system and its role in determining physical appearance — the structure of bone and why it supports our bodies against gravity, and the joints where bones meet and how they permit movement. "You may be surprised to learn that bone is a dynamic, living tissue." (p. 86)

The chapter as one chain

  • Bone does six jobs, not one — support, movement, protection, mineral storage, fat storage, and blood cell production.
  • Its properties come from two components — mineral salts make it hard, collagen makes it resilient, and losing either one ruins it in a different way.
  • It is built on a cartilage model and never stops changing — growth plates lengthen it until the sex hormones shut them down, and remodeling continues for life.
  • Remodeling is also calcium regulation — PTH and calcitonin move calcium between bone and blood, which is why the skeleton and blood chemistry are one story.
  • Bone answers to mechanical stress — weight-bearing thickens it, disuse costs nearly a third of its mass in weeks, and osteoporosis is that balance tipped the wrong way.
  • 80 axial bones protect; the appendicular skeleton moves — and the joints between them trade stability against range.

5.1Bone Functions

pp. 87
Big ideaThe skeleton does six distinct jobs, and only one of them is holding you up.
  • Support. A rigid framework supporting soft tissues. The leg bones and backbone hold the body upright; the pelvic girdle supports the abdominal organs.
  • Movement. Places of attachment for muscles. Contraction of muscles allows bones to move at joints.
  • Protection. Shields internal organs — heart and lungs within the chest cavity, the brain within the skull.
  • Storage of minerals. Particularly calcium and phosphorus, released to the rest of the body when needed.
  • Storage of fat. Energy-rich fat in yellow bone marrow, metabolised when the energy is needed.
  • Blood cell production. In the red marrow of certain bones.

Key terms

skeleton
the framework of bones and cartilage
Exam tip

Two of the six are storage and they store different things in different places — minerals in the matrix, fat in yellow marrow — and a seventh job, blood cell production, happens in red marrow. Three separate answers that all sound like "inside the bone."

5.2Bone Structure

pp. 87–88
Big ideaBones mix dense compact bone with latticed spongy bone, and the marrow inside them is two different tissues doing two different jobs.
  • The 206 bones come in a range of sizes and shapes. Most bones contain both compact and spongy bone, in proportions depending on the bone's size and shape.
  • Compact bone — very dense, with few internal spaces. Forms most of the shaft of long bones such as those of the arms and legs. SEE FIGURE 5.1
    • Except at the joint surfaces it is covered by the PERIOSTEUM, a glovelike membrane that nourishes the bone. It contains blood vessels and nerves as well as cells that function in bone growth and repair; the vessels pass through it into the underlying bone.
    • When a bone is bruised or fractured, most of the pain results from injury to the periosteum.
  • Spongy bone — a latticework of tiny beams and thin plates with open areas between, bracing the bone from within. Found largely in small flat bones such as most bones of the skull, and in the heads (enlarged ends) and near the ends of the shafts of long bones.
  • Marrow, two kinds.
    • Red marrow — fills some spongy bone in adults (ribs, pelvis, backbone, skull, sternum, long-bone ends). Where blood cells form.
    • Yellow marrow — fills the cavity in the shaft of adult long bones. A fatty tissue used for energy storage.

Key terms

compact bone
dense bone with few internal spaces; most of a long bone's shaft
periosteum
the membrane covering compact bone; nourishes it and carries the pain
spongy bone

Exam tip

"Most of the pain is the periosteum" is the kind of fact that reads as trivia and answers a question. The bone itself is not what hurts.

Exam tip

Red and yellow marrow sit in different places as well as doing different jobs — red in spongy bone at the ends and in flat bones, yellow in the shaft cavity. Location alone distinguishes them.

5.3Bone as a Living Tissue

pp. 89
Big ideaBone is living tissue built on a nonliving matrix — minerals for hardness, collagen for resilience — laid down over a cartilage model that growth plates keep lengthening until hormones shut them down.
  • The osteon — compact bone is highly organised living tissue containing a microscopic repeating structural unit, the OSTEON. SEE FIGURE 5.1
    • Each osteon is mature bone cells (OSTEOCYTES) arranged in concentric rings around a CENTRAL CANAL.
    • Each osteocyte lies in a LACUNA (pl. lacunae) in the hardened matrix. Tiny canals connect nearby lacunae and eventually the central canal, and osteocytes have projections extending through them to touch neighbouring cells.
    • Nutrients, oxygen and wastes pass cell to cell, to or from the blood vessels in the central canal.
  • The matrix is nonliving, and it is what you notice.
    • Hardness comes from mineral salts, primarily calcium and phosphorus.
    • Resilience comes from strands of the strong elastic protein collagen woven throughout.
    • Without the calcium and phosphorus salts, bone would be rubbery and flexible like a garden hose. Without the collagen, it would be brittle and crumbly like chalk.
    • In some disorders bones do bend, causing bowlegs — for example rickets, due to greatly reduced calcium salts.
  • The cartilage model. In embryonic development most of the skeleton is first formed of cartilage. SEE FIGURE 5.2
    • Unlike mature bone cells, which cannot divide because they are enclosed in a solid matrix, cartilage cells divide and multiply quickly — so the model can grow as fast as the fetus does.
    • From the third month and through prenatal development, cartilage is gradually replaced by bone.
  • Cartilage becomes a long bone. SEE FIGURE 5.3
    • It begins with a collar of bone around the shaft, produced by OSTEOBLASTS (blast = beginning or bud), which secrete collagen and then deposit calcium salts on it.
    • The collar supports the shaft as the cartilage inside breaks down, leaving the marrow cavity. Blood vessels carry osteoblasts in, and they fill it with spongy bone.
    • Osteoblasts cannot divide. Once one has formed matrix around itself it is an OSTEOCYTE — a mature bone cell, the principal cell of bone tissue.
    • Near birth, bone growth centres form in the ends of long bones and spongy bone begins to fill them.
  • Two cartilage structures remain.
    • A cap of cartilage over each end, where one bone glides over another in a joint.
    • The EPIPHYSEAL PLATE — the growth plate — separating each end from the shaft. Cartilage cells within it divide, forcing the end of the bone farther from the shaft; as bone replaces the newer cartilage nearer the shaft, the bone lengthens. The diameter also enlarges as the bone lengthens. SEE FIGURE 5.4
  • Hormones and bone growth.
    • In childhood, growth is powerfully stimulated by growth hormone from the anterior pituitary, which prompts the liver to release growth factors producing a surge of growth in the growth plate.
    • Thyroid hormones modify growth hormone's activity to ensure the skeleton grows with the proper proportions.
    • At puberty the growth spurt is driven by rising sex hormones (testosterone and estrogen), which first stimulate the growth-plate cartilage cells into a frenzy of division.
    • The same hormones then end it — division slows, growth plates thin as bone replaces cartilage, and finally the bone ends fuse with the shaft. Growth generally stops about age 18 in females and 21 in males.
    • Bones can no longer lengthen at that stage, but they can continue to widen.

Watch out

The two matrix components fail in opposite directions, and the book gives you both similes. No mineral salts → rubbery, like a garden hose. No collagen → brittle and crumbly, like chalk. A question naming either failure is asking which component is missing.

Watch out

Osteoblast versus osteocyte is a before-and-after, not two cell types. The osteoblast lays down matrix; once walled in by it, the same cell is called an osteocyte. Osteoblasts cannot divide — only cartilage cells can, which is why the fetal model is cartilage in the first place.

Exam tip

The sex hormones both start and stop the growth spurt. They drive the frenzy of cartilage division and then close the plates. A question about why growth stops at 18 or 21 has the same answer as why it accelerated at 13.

5.4The Role of Fibroblasts and Osteoblasts in Repairing Bone Fractures

pp. 90
Big ideaA fracture heals in a fixed sequence — clot, fibroblasts, cartilage callus, bony callus — and the repair deliberately overshoots before it is trimmed back.
  • Bones break when the physical force applied is greater than the strength of the bone. More common in children, whose bones have not reached peak density, and in older adults, whose bones have lost density and become brittle.
  • Two independent axes describe a fracture.
    • Nondisplaced — the bone may or may not break completely through, but the ends remain aligned. Displaced — the broken ends are not aligned, and must be realigned for healing to occur.
    • Closed — the broken ends remain within the skin. Open — a broken end punctures the skin. The bone in an open fracture may retreat beneath the skin, but the risk of bone infection is still higher than with a closed fracture.
  • Healing, in order. SEE FIGURE 5.5
    • Bleeding from blood vessels in the bone and periosteum, then a clot — a HEMATOMA — at the break.
    • Within a few days, connective tissue cells called FIBROBLASTS grow inward from the periosteum and invade the clot.
    • The fibroblasts secrete collagen fibers forming a mass, the CALLUS, linking the broken surfaces. Some fibroblasts then transform into cartilage-producing cells and secrete cartilage into it.
    • Osteoblasts from the periosteum invade and transform that cartilage into new bone — a BONY CALLUS. During this it becomes thicker than the undamaged bone and protrudes from it.
    • In time the extra material is broken down and the healed part returns to nearly normal size.

Key terms

fracture
a break, when applied force exceeds the strength of the bone
hematoma
the clot that forms at the break
fibroblast
connective tissue cell that invades the clot and secretes collagen
callus
the collagen mass linking the broken surfaces
bony callus

Watch out

The periosteum supplies both repair cell types — the fibroblasts that build the soft callus and the osteoblasts that convert it. That is the same membrane that carries the pain in 5.2, which makes it the single most load-bearing structure in the chapter.

Exam tip

Displaced/nondisplaced and open/closed are independent. A fracture is one of each, so there are four combinations. Questions exploit the assumption that they are one scale.

5.5Bone Remodeling

pp. 91–92
Big ideaBone is deposited and broken down for life, which keeps it strong and simultaneously regulates blood calcium — and the balance answers to hormones and to mechanical stress.
  • Remodeling continues after full height is reached — a lifelong process of deposition and breakdown. It keeps bones strong by repairing tiny cracks, such as might occur jumping from a high place.
  • Two cells, opposite jobs. Osteoblasts deposit bone. OSTEOCLASTS break it down, releasing calcium and other minerals to be reabsorbed by the body.
  • So remodeling is also how blood calcium is regulated — important because calcium has a role in nerve and muscle function and in blood clotting.
  • Hormonal control.
    • Parathyroid hormone (PTH), from the parathyroid glands embedded in the tissues of the thyroid gland, acts when blood calcium falls below normal. It stimulates osteoclasts, releasing calcium from bone into the blood. PTH increases blood calcium.
    • Calcitonin, from the thyroid gland, is released in children when blood calcium is high. It decreases blood calcium by causing calcium to be deposited in bone. Its importance in bone remodeling is not clear.
    • Estrogen — in women and, to a lesser extent, men — promotes calcium absorption from the digestive system, stimulates bone formation, and impairs the ability of osteoclasts to break down bone.
  • Stress shapes bone. New bone forms along the lines of stress. Bone forms in response to stress and is absorbed when it is not stressed.
    • Weight-bearing exercise such as walking or jogging thickens the layer of compact bone, giving a stronger bone.
    • Frequently used bones may change shape — continual practice may enlarge the knuckles of pianists and the big toes of ballet dancers.
    • A few weeks without stress can cost a bone nearly a third of its mass — a concern for astronauts in weightlessness and for a person whose leg is in a cast.
  • When breakdown outpaces deposition, bone becomes weak and easy to break. That imbalance is osteoporosis, a progressive loss in bone density.
  • Why the drugs work.
    • Bisphosphonates (Fosamax, Actonel, Boniva) inhibit the osteoclasts that break down bone, but not the osteoblasts that build it.
    • SERMs — selective estrogen receptor modulators, e.g. Evista — mimic the effects of estrogen on bone.
    • Calcitonin can be used because it increases the calcium deposited in bone.

Key terms

remodeling
lifelong bone deposition and breakdown
osteoclast
the cell that breaks bone down, releasing calcium
parathyroid hormone (PTH)
raises blood calcium by stimulating osteoclasts
calcitonin
lowers blood calcium by depositing it in bone
osteoporosis

Watch out

Three cells, and the names differ by two letters. Osteoblast builds, osteocyte is the retired builder walled into the matrix, osteoclast breaks down. Every hormone in this section acts by pushing one of the three.

Watch out

PTH and calcitonin oppose each other, and only one of them is certain. PTH raises blood calcium; calcitonin lowers it; the book says outright that calcitonin's importance in remodeling is not clear and that it is released in children. Do not treat them as a symmetrical pair.

Exam tip

Each osteoporosis drug maps onto one mechanism in this section, which is why they are listed here rather than in a clinical chapter. Bisphosphonates hit osteoclasts, SERMs imitate estrogen, calcitonin drives deposition.

Worked example from the text

The knuckles of pianists and the big toes of ballet dancers — the book's own illustration that bone answers to use.

5.6Axial Skeleton

pp. 93–94
Big ideaEighty bones — skull, vertebral column and rib cage — built to protect and support the internal organs.
  • The skeleton divides in two. SEE FIGURE 5.6
    • Axial (orange in the figure) — the skull, the vertebral column, and the bones of the chest region (sternum and rib cage). 80 bones. In general it protects and supports the internal organs.
    • Appendicular (light brown) — the pectoral girdle, the pelvic girdle, and the limbs.
  • The skull is the most complex bony structure in the body; its principal divisions are the cranium and the face. SEE FIGURE 5.7
    • Several skull bones contain air spaces called SINUSES, which make the head lighter and serve as resonating chambers for the voice.
  • The cranium protects the brain, houses the structures of hearing, and provides attachment for the muscles of the head and neck. Eight (or sometimes more) flat bones.
    • Frontal (single) — the forehead and front of the brain case.
    • Parietal (two) — the top and sides of the skull.
    • Occipital — the back of the head; surrounds the FORAMEN MAGNUM, the opening through which the spinal cord passes.
    • Temporal — part of which forms the temples.
    • Sphenoid — bow-tie shaped; the cranium's floor.
    • Ethmoid — the smallest bone of the cranium; separates the cranial cavity from the nasal cavity. The olfactory nerves reach the brain through tiny holes in it.
  • Fontanels. Before and shortly after birth the cranial bones are joined by membranous areas — the "soft spots". They allow the skull to be compressed during birth and accommodate the rapid enlargement of the brain. Replaced by bone by age 2. SEE FIGURE 5.8
  • The face — 14 bones, supporting sensory structures and anchoring most facial muscles.
    • Nasal — the bridge of the nose, paired and fused at the midline. Inside, the nasal septum (the vomer plus part of the ethmoid) divides the left and right chambers.
    • Zygomatic (paired) — the cheekbones; flat areas form part of the floor of the eye sockets, and an extension joins one from the temporal bone to make the zygomatic arch.
    • Lacrimal — the smallest facial bones, at the inner corners of the eyes. A duct through each drains tears into the nasal chambers, which is why the nose runs when we cry.
    • Maxillae — the upper jaw. Most other facial bones join them, so the upper jaw has an important role in facial structure. They form part of the hard palate, the rest formed by the two palatine bones behind. If the maxillae fail to fuse at the midline below the nasal septum, the mouth and nasal cavities do not fully separate and a CLEFT PALATE results — easily corrected by surgery.
    • Mandible — the lower jaw, also two bones joined at the midline. It meets the skull at the temporal bone, forming the temporomandibular joint, which lets the mouth open and close. Clenching or grinding under emotional stress stresses that joint, producing headaches, toothaches or even earaches — TMJ syndrome.
  • The vertebral column — a series of bones through which the spinal cord passes; each is a vertebra. 26 vertebrae. SEE FIGURE 5.9
    • 7 cervical (neck), C1–C7 · 12 thoracic (chest), T1–T12 · 5 lumbar (lower back), L1–L5 · 1 sacrum, from the fusion of five sacral vertebrae · 1 coccyx, from the fusion of four.
    • Scoliosis — "twisted disease", an abnormal curvature to the left or right. The commonest form has no known cause, affects over 1.5 million adolescents, primarily females, and usually begins during and progresses through the adolescent growth spurt. Treated if needed with a brace or surgery.
    • Why the sacrum fuses — extra strength where the backbone meets the pelvic girdle, necessary because of the stress from the weight of the vertebral column and the powerful movements of the leg.
    • The coccyx serves no function and is regarded as a vestigial tail. But its upper part is supplied with nerves and can be extremely painful when injured.
    • Intervertebral disks — pads of fibrocartilage cushioning the vertebrae above the sacrum; their smooth lubricated surfaces give the column flexibility. As they compress over the years a person becomes shorter, an effect pronounced when coupled with osteoporosis.
    • "Slipped disk" is a misnomer — the disk does not move, it bulges. Bulging inward presses the spinal cord, interfering with muscle control and perception. Bulging outward against a spinal nerve causes great pain; the sciatic nerve down the back of the leg is among the most often affected, and the resulting sciatica can be cripplingly painful.
    • Lower back pain accounts for more missed workdays than any medical problem besides colds, and is notoriously hard to diagnose — pain may originate solely from muscle rather than bone, and muscles do not show up on x-ray films. Some cases are weak abdominal muscles that cannot counteract the powerful back muscles, letting the vertebrae misalign.
  • The rib cage — 12 pairs of ribs attaching at the back to the thoracic vertebrae. SEE FIGURE 5.10
    • At the front the upper 10 pairs attach by cartilage, directly or indirectly, to the sternum. Their flexibility lets the ribs take some blows without breaking and move during breathing.
    • The last two pairs do not attach to the sternum and are called FLOATING RIBS — though still attached at the back.

Exam tip

Two "smallest" bones, in different places. The ethmoid is the smallest bone of the cranium; the lacrimal bones are the smallest of the face.

Exam tip

A bulging disk does opposite things depending on direction. Inward → spinal cord → loss of muscle control and sensation. Outward → spinal nerve → pain. The question is which way it bulged.

5.7Appendicular Skeleton

pp. 95
Big ideaTwo girdles and the limbs hanging off them — the pectoral trades stability for range, the pelvic does the opposite.
  • A girdle is a skeletal structure that supports the limbs. The pectoral girdle connects the arms to the rib cage; the pelvic girdle connects the legs to the vertebral column, enabling the body to move from place to place.
  • Pectoral girdle — the scapulae (shoulder blades) and the clavicles (collarbones). SEE FIGURE 5.11
    • The clavicles are more curved in males than in females — one way to tell the sex of a skeleton.
    • Each clavicle forms a relatively rigid bridge between a scapula and the sternum.
    • One corner of the roughly triangular scapula has a socket taking one end of the HUMERUS, the upper arm bone.
    • The humerus meets the radius and ulna at the elbow. The elbow is formed by the extension of the ulna past the junction with the humerus.
    • Radius and ulna meet the eight carpals at the wrist; these join the five metacarpals of the hand. Each finger has three phalanges; the thumb has two.
  • Carpal tunnel — a narrow opening through the carpal bones, carrying a nerve that controls sensation in the fingers and some hand muscles, plus TENDONS, bands of connective tissue attaching muscle to bone.
    • Repetitive motion can inflame those tendons so they press against the nerve — numbness or tingling in the hand, and pain in wrist, hand and fingers. Carpal tunnel syndrome, seen in barbers, cab drivers and pianists, and increasingly with keyboards and video games.
  • Pelvic girdle — much more rigid than the pectoral girdle. SEE FIGURE 5.12
    • The two pelvic bones attach behind to the sacrum, curve down and round to the front, and join a cartilage disk at the PUBIC SYMPHYSIS.
    • Male and female hips are easily distinguishable: the opening of the pelvic girdle in the female is wider, to facilitate childbirth.
  • The leg — the femur rotates within a socket in the pelvis; at the knee it meets the tibia (shinbone), with the smaller fibula running down the side. The junction is covered by the patella. At the ankle the lower leg bones meet the tarsals, connected to the metatarsals, connected to the toe bones, also called phalanges.

Exam tip

Two ways to sex a skeleton appear in this one section — the clavicles are more curved in males, and the female pelvic opening is wider. Both are stated outright.

Exam tip

Phalanges is the answer twice — fingers and toes use the same word. Three per finger, two in the thumb.

Watch out

Carpal tunnel syndrome is a nerve problem caused by tendons. The tendons inflame; the nerve is what hurts. A question asking what is compressed wants the nerve, not the tendons.

5.8Joints

pp. 96–98
Big ideaJoints are classified by what holds them together, and the freely movable synovial joints share one anatomy and fail in a handful of characteristic ways.
  • Joints are the places where bones meet, classified by their components and structure as fibrous, cartilaginous or synovial. Some allow no movement, some slight, some are freely movable.
  • Fibrous joints — held together by fibrous connective tissue, with no joint cavity; most permit no movement.
    • The joints between adult skull bones, the SUTURES, are considered immovable because the bones are interlocked and held tightly.
    • In fact sutures move ever so slightly, and that movement serves as a shock absorber when you hit your head. In old age sutures close completely.
  • Cartilaginous joints — rather rigid cartilage holds the bones together; some immovable, some allowing slight movement. Found between vertebrae, in the attachment of ribs to the sternum, and at the pubic symphysis.
    • In a pregnant woman, hormones loosen the cartilage of the pubic symphysis, allowing the pelvis to widen to ease childbirth.
  • Synovial joints — most joints of the body, freely movable; because of them muscles can manoeuvre the body into thousands of positions. All share:
    • A thin layer of cartilage on the surfaces that move past one another, reducing friction so the bones slide without grating.
    • A two-layered joint capsule. The inner layer, the SYNOVIAL MEMBRANE, secretes viscous clear synovial fluid into the joint cavity, which lubricates and cushions. The outer layer is continuous with the covering membranes of the bones.
    • Reinforcement by LIGAMENTS — strong straps of connective tissue that hold the bones together, support the joint, and direct the movement of the bones.
    • Hinge joints — knee, elbow — permit motion in only one plane, like a hinge on a door. SEE FIGURE 5.13
    • Ball-and-socket joints — shoulder, hip — allow movement in all planes; you can swing your arm in a complete circle. SEE FIGURE 5.14
  • Damage to joints.
    • A SPRAIN is damage to a ligament, from slight overstretching to a serious tear. A torn ligament brings swelling and enough pain to inhibit movement. Ligaments carry a concentration of pain receptors very sensitive to stretching and swelling, so the injury may be less severe than the pain suggests.
    • Treatment order matters: ice first, then heat. Ice constricts blood vessels and reduces bleeding, which generally stops by the next day; heat then dilates vessels so blood carries in nutrients, oxygen and anti-inflammatory chemicals. Like tendons, ligaments have few blood vessels and heal slowly.
    • ACL tear — common in gymnasts and football and soccer players. When the knee is bent the ACL acts as a restraining wire restricting front-to-back twisting between femur and tibia; an external blow to a bent knee can stretch it, and if the force exceeds its strength it tears. SEE FIGURE 5.15
    • BURSAE (sing. bursa, "pouch or purse") — fluid-filled sacs lined with synovial membrane, the body's own gliding surface. Found where skin rubs over bone and between tendons and bones, muscles and bones, and ligaments and bones.
    • BURSITIS — injury or repeated pressure inflames a bursa, which swells with excess fluid. Throwing a baseball or swinging a tennis racket may cause it in the elbow. Intense pain, worse when the joint moves, not relieved by resting in any position. Not serious; usually subsides within a week or two, though a physician may drain the fluid.
  • Arthritis — a general term for joint inflammation; more than 100 kinds.
    • Osteoarthritis — degeneration of joint surfaces from wear and tear. The slippery cartilage disintegrates until the bones grind together, causing intense pain and stiffness. Most likely in weight-bearing joints — hip, knee, spine — occasionally fingers or wrist. Prevention: control weight, and exercise — weights strengthen the supporting muscles, stretching gives greater range.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis — far more threatening. Inflammation of the synovial membrane; the accumulating fluid causes swelling, pain and stiffness, and constant irritation can destroy the cartilage, which may be replaced by fibrous connective tissue that further impedes movement.
    • It is an AUTOIMMUNE DISEASE — the body mistakenly attacks its own synovial membranes as if they were invading foreign matter. It varies in severity but is permanent, normally affecting fingers, wrist, knees, neck, ankles and hips. Sometimes the only effective treatment is to replace the damaged joint.

Osteoarthritis is wear; rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune. That single distinction drives everything else — which joints, what is destroyed, and whether it can be prevented by controlling weight.

Watch out

Ligament, tendon, cartilage — three connective tissues doing three jobs. Ligament bone-to-bone at a joint; tendon muscle-to-bone (5.7); cartilage the low-friction surface. Both ligaments and tendons have few blood vessels and heal slowly.

Exam tip

Ice first, then heat, and the book gives the reason for each. Ice constricts to stop bleeding; heat dilates to deliver healing. Reversing them is the wrong answer for a stated physiological reason.

Stop And Think

1(p. 89) We have considered the mechanism by which long bones grow and the role of growth hormone in that process. Why would it be ineffective for a short, middle-aged person to be treated with growth hormone to stimulate growth?

Growth hormone works through the growth plates, stimulating cartilage cells there to divide. In a middle-aged adult the epiphyseal plates have long since fused — cartilage has been fully replaced by bone and the bone ends have joined the shaft — so there is no cartilage left for the hormone to act on. Bones can still widen, but they cannot lengthen. Derived from 5.3; no printed answer.

2(p. 90) Osteogenesis imperfecta is a condition in which the activities of osteoblasts and fibroblasts are abnormally low. What effects would you expect this to have on the skeletal system?

Both cell types are needed to build and repair bone. Low osteoblast activity means too little matrix laid down — thin, weak bones that fracture easily. Low fibroblast activity means the collagen callus that starts fracture repair forms poorly, so breaks heal slowly and badly. Since collagen supplies bone's resilience (5.3), the bones would also be brittle rather than merely thin. Derived from 5.3 and 5.4; no printed answer.

3(Figure 5.2 question, p. 89) It is recommended that young adult women consume 1100 to 1300 mg of calcium each day, but that requirement doubles during pregnancy. Explain why.

The fetal skeleton is laid down as cartilage and then replaced by bone, and that ossification requires calcium salts deposited by osteoblasts. The calcium comes from the mother. If dietary calcium is insufficient the fetus's needs are met by withdrawing calcium from the mother's own bones through the remodeling and PTH mechanisms of 5.5, costing her bone density. Derived from 5.3 and 5.5; no printed answer.

4(Consider This Case, p. 98) Elsa is a tennis champion whose right arm is repeatedly described as powerful. She slipped on ice, fell forward, caught herself on both outstretched arms, and broke her left arm. Why would only the left arm break when there was equal force on both arms? How would you test the hypothesis?

Bone forms in response to stress and is absorbed when it is not stressed (5.5). Years of serving have loaded her right arm far more than her left, so the right humerus, radius and ulna have thicker compact bone and greater density — the same principle as the pianist's knuckles. Equal force therefore exceeded the strength of the weaker left arm only. To test it: compare bone density in the two arms — a DEXA scan or x-ray of both — and predict the right will be denser; more generally, compare dominant and non-dominant arm density across a group of one-sided athletes against non-athletes. Derived from 5.5; no printed answer.

OPENING

The previous chapter covered bones, which support us against gravity, and joints, which allow movement. This chapter is how muscle contraction produces that movement — muscle structure, the mechanism of contraction, how nerves control it, and the energy sources that fuel it. (p. 101)

The chapter as one chain

  • Skeletal muscle only pulls, so it works in antagonistic pairs — one shortens while its partner relaxes, and nothing pushes a bone back.
  • The whole mechanism is one nested structure — muscle → fascicle → fiber → myofibril → myofilament → sarcomere — and every later idea sits at one of those levels.
  • Contraction is actin sliding along myosin, powered by a four-step cross-bridge cycle that runs on ATP hundreds of times a second.
  • Calcium is the switch and ATP is the fuel, and they are separate — calcium uncovers the binding sites, ATP cocks and releases the heads. Rigor mortis, curare, botulism and muscular dystrophy are each one of those two failing.
  • Strength is graded two ways — how many motor units fire (recruitment) and how fast they are restimulated (summation → tetanus).
  • Four energy sources are used in order of duration — stored ATP, creatine phosphate, anaerobic pathways, aerobic respiration — and slow- vs fast-twitch cells are that same trade-off built into the cell.

6.1Function and Characteristics of Muscles

pp. 102
Big ideaThree kinds of muscle share four properties; skeletal muscle is the one under voluntary control, and it does far more than move you.
  • Three kinds of muscle — SKELETAL, CARDIAC, SMOOTH. Each has distinct qualities and functions, but all share four traits:
    • EXCITABLE — they respond to stimuli.
    • CONTRACTILE — they can shorten.
    • EXTENSIBLE — they can stretch.
    • ELASTIC — they return to their original length after being shortened or stretched.
  • This chapter is about skeletal muscle. Smooth and cardiac muscle are covered in Chapters 4 and 12.
  • Functions of skeletal muscle:
    • Movement, including facial expression.
    • Posture — remaining erect despite the pull of gravity.
    • Support for internal organs — the muscles forming the abdominal wall and the floor of the pelvic cavity.
    • Moving blood and lymph — contraction pushes against veins and lymphatic vessels.
    • Heat generation — contraction generates heat, which warms the body above most environmental temperatures we experience.
  • Unlike cardiac and smooth muscle, skeletal muscle is under VOLUNTARY control.

6.2Skeletal Muscles Working in Pairs

pp. 103
Big ideaA muscle can only pull, so movement in two directions takes two muscles; origin stays put, insertion moves.
  • Most muscles work in pairs or groups.
    • SYNERGISTIC muscles — muscles that must contract at the same time to produce a movement.
    • ANTAGONISTIC PAIRS — most muscles; they produce a movement when one of the pair contracts and the other relaxes. SEE FIGURE 6.1
  • When one member of the pair contracts it shortens and pulls on one of the bones meeting at a joint. Whenever an antagonistic muscle contracts and pulls on a bone, its partner, which has an opposing action, must relax. For the bone to move back, the first must relax and the other must contract.
  • BICEPS (top of the upper arm) and TRICEPS (back of the arm): contracting the biceps FLEXES and bends the arm at the elbow; contracting the triceps EXTENDS and straightens it.
  • ORIGIN AND INSERTION. Most major muscles used for locomotion and manipulation are attached to bones, each end by a TENDON, a band of connective tissue. A muscle is often attached to two bones on opposite sides of a joint.
    • ORIGIN — the end attached to the bone that remains relatively stationary.
    • INSERTION — the end attached to the bone that moves.
    • The bones act as levers.
  • The body has more than 600 skeletal muscles. SEE FIGURE 6.2
  • INJURIES. A contracting muscle actually pulls on the tendon connecting it to bone.
    • TENDINITIS — excessive stress inflames a tendon; most is due to overuse, misuse (as when lifting improperly), or age. Tendons heal slowly because they are poorly supplied with blood vessels. The most effective treatment is rest — if it hurts, do not use it.
    • MUSCLE PULL / STRAIN / TEAR — used almost interchangeably for damage to a muscle or its tendons caused by overstretching. May also include damage to small blood vessels in the muscle, causing bleeding and pain. Treatment: ice to reduce swelling and keeping the muscle stretched.
    • "TEXT NECK" — a stiff neck from damage to neck muscles and ligaments caused by hunching over a phone. Avoided by taking frequent breaks, sitting upright, and rotating the shoulders with the arms at the side.

6.3Contraction of Muscles

pp. 104–107
Big ideaMuscle is a nest of structures ending in the sarcomere, and contraction is actin sliding along myosin — switched on by calcium, powered by ATP.
  • NESTED STRUCTURE, outside in. SEE FIGURE 6.3
    • Each individual muscle is covered by a membrane of fibrous connective tissue.
    • Muscles are formed of smaller bundles, FASCICLES, each wrapped in its own connective tissue sheath.
    • The connective tissue sheaths merge and condense at the ends of the muscle to form the TENDONS.
    • Inside the fascicles are the MUSCLE FIBERS — the muscle cells themselves. Skeletal muscle cells can be several centimetres long; muscle cells in the thigh can be 30 cm (1 ft).
  • STRIATIONS. Skeletal muscle is also called STRIATED (striped) muscle because the cells show pronounced bands under the microscope.
    • The striations come from the orderly arrangement of elongated MYOFIBRILS, specialised bundles of proteins within the cell.
    • Each myofibril contains two types of MYOFILAMENT: the thicker MYOSIN filaments and a greater number of the thinner ACTIN filaments.
    • The bundles of myofilaments make up about 80% of the cell volume.
  • SARCOMERES. Along its length each myofibril has tens of thousands of contractile units called SARCOMERES.
    • The ends of each sarcomere are marked by dark bands of protein, the Z LINES.
    • One end of each actin filament is attached to a Z line. Myosin filaments lie in the middle of the sarcomere, their ends partially overlapping the surrounding actin filaments. The degree of overlap increases when the muscle contracts.
  • SLIDING FILAMENT MODEL — a muscle contracts when actin filaments slide along myosin filaments, increasing the overlap and shortening the sarcomere. When many sarcomeres shorten, the whole muscle contracts. SEE FIGURE 6.4
    • Actin — two chains of spherical actin molecules, "resembling two strings of beads twisted around each other to form a helix."
    • Myosin — molecules shaped like two-headed golf clubs; the shafts of several hundred lie along the filament and the heads protrude along each end in a spiral pattern. The club-shaped myosin heads are the key to movement.
  • THE CROSS-BRIDGE CYCLE.
    • Resting sarcomere — the myosin heads have already split ATP into ADP + inorganic phosphate (Pi). The energy released swivels the heads, extending them toward the Z lines — "analogous to cocking a pistol so that it is poised and ready to fire."
    • Step 1 — cross-bridge attachment. The myosin heads attach to the nearest actin filament. Bound, a head acts as a bridge between thick and thin filaments, which is why myosin heads are also called CROSS-BRIDGES.
    • Step 2 — bending of the myosin head, the POWER STROKE. On binding, the stored energy is released, swinging the heads forcefully back to their bent positions and pulling the actin filaments toward the midline of the sarcomere — "analogous to pulling the trigger on a pistol." ADP and Pi are released from the head during the power stroke.
    • Step 3 — cross-bridge detachment. New ATP molecules bind to the myosin heads, causing them to disengage from actin.
    • Step 4 — myosin reactivation. The heads split the ATP into ADP and Pi and store the energy, beginning the cycle again.
    • This cycle is repeated hundreds of times a second.
  • CALCIUM AND THE REGULATORY PROTEINS. Muscle contraction is controlled by the availability of calcium ions. SEE FIGURE 6.5
    • Muscle cells contain TROPONIN and TROPOMYOSIN, which together form the troponin–tropomyosin complex.
    • During relaxation the actin–myosin binding sites are covered by that complex.
    • Contraction occurs when calcium ions enter the sarcomere and bind to troponin, changing its shape. That shifts tropomyosin away from the myosin-binding sites on actin, exposing them.
  • WHERE THE CALCIUM COMES FROM.
    • Calcium ions are stored in the SARCOPLASMIC RETICULUM, an elaborate form of smooth endoplasmic reticulum — picture "a sleeve of thick lace surrounding each myofibril."
    • TRANSVERSE TUBULES (T TUBULES) are tiny cylindrical pockets of the muscle cell's plasma membrane scattered through the cell. They carry signals from motor neurons deep into the cell, to virtually every sarcomere.
  • RIGOR MORTIS — it takes an ATP molecule to break the cross-bridges.
    • Soon after death calcium leaks out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, initiating contraction. Contraction continues as long as ATP is present, but after death ATP is no longer produced and the supply runs out.
    • Without ATP the cross-bridges cannot be broken, and within 3 to 4 hours after death the muscles become stiff.
    • Actin and myosin gradually break down, letting the muscles relax again after 2 to 3 days.
  • ROLE OF NERVES. The stimulus leading to calcium release is a nerve impulse from a MOTOR NEURON; the junction between the tip of a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle cell is the NEUROMUSCULAR JUNCTION. SEE FIGURE 6.6 1. A nerve impulse reaches a neuromuscular junction. 2. The impulse causes release of ACETYLCHOLINE, a neurotransmitter, from small packets in the tip of the motor neuron. 3. Acetylcholine diffuses across a small gap and binds to receptors on the muscle cell membrane, changing its permeability and creating an electrochemical message similar to a nerve impulse. 4. The message travels along the plasma membrane, into the T tubules, then to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, opening channels that release calcium ions. Calcium combines with troponin, the binding sites are exposed, and the muscle contracts.
  • RELAXATION — when the nerve impulse stops, membrane pumps quickly clear the sarcomere of calcium ions, the troponin–tropomyosin complexes move back over the binding sites, and the muscle relaxes. The contraction of other muscles then stretches the sarcomere back to its original length.
  • MUSCLE CRAMPS — low blood levels of calcium, magnesium or potassium are possible causes of cramps, forceful involuntary contractions. Usually relieved by stretching or gently rubbing the muscle.
  • MUSCULAR DYSTROPHY — if too many calcium ions enter the cell they destroy proteins within it, causing it to die.
    • A general term for a group of inherited conditions.
    • DUCHENNE muscular dystrophy, one of the most common forms, is caused by a defective gene for the protein DYSTROPHIN. Lack of dystrophin allows excess calcium into the muscle cell, killing it.
    • Dead muscle cells are replaced by fat and connective tissue, so the muscles become progressively weaker.

Watch out

Calcium and ATP do two different jobs and the exam trades on the confusion. Calcium is the switch — it uncovers the binding sites. 6.4Voluntary Movement

pp. 108
Big ideaA muscle cell is all-or-none, so whole-muscle strength is graded two ways — how many motor units fire, and how fast they are restimulated.
  • An individual muscle cell contracts completely each time it is stimulated, but only some of the cells in a whole muscle contract at once. The combined output determines the extent and strength of the contraction. Two ways to vary contraction of a whole muscle: 1. Changing the NUMBER of muscle cells contracting at a given moment. 2. Changing the FORCE of contraction in individual cells by altering the FREQUENCY of stimulation.
  • MOTOR UNITS AND RECRUITMENT. SEE FIGURE 6.7
    • A MOTOR UNIT is a motor neuron and all the muscle cells it stimulates. All the muscle cells in a given motor unit contract together.
    • On average about 150 muscle cells per motor unit, but quite variable.
    • Precise, finely controlled movements — fingers, eyes — have SMALL numbers of cells per motor unit; less precise movements — thigh, calf — have many. A single motor neuron in a tiny eye muscle may control only three muscle cells; one in a calf muscle may control thousands.
    • RECRUITMENT — the nervous system increases the strength of contraction by increasing the number of motor units being stimulated.
    • The muscle cells of a given motor unit are spread throughout the muscle, so stimulating a single motor unit makes the entire muscle contract, but only weakly. Lifting a table and lifting a fork use the same muscles but different numbers of motor units.
    • Smoothness comes from timing — the nervous system stimulates different motor units so they are not all active simultaneously or for the same amount of time.
    • MUSCLE TONE — even in a relaxed muscle some motor units are active, though not the same ones all the time, so the muscle is firm and solid even when not in use. A muscle that lacks muscle tone is limp.
  • MUSCLE TWITCHES, SUMMATION, TETANUS. SEE FIGURE 6.8
    • MUSCLE TWITCH (a) — brief artificial stimulation makes some cells contract.
      • LATENT PERIOD — the interval between reception of the stimulus and the beginning of contraction.
      • The contraction phase is quite short, followed by a longer relaxation phase.
      • Twitches are too brief to be part of normal movements — but they generate heat to maintain body temperature when we shiver.
    • SUMMATION (b) — if a second stimulus arrives before the muscle is fully relaxed, the second twitch is stronger; the second contraction is added to the first. Summation occurs because an increasing number of muscle cells are stimulated to contract.
    • TETANUS (c) — when stimuli arrive so frequently that there is no time for any relaxation before the next, the muscle goes into a sustained, powerful contraction.
    • FATIGUE — tetanus cannot continue indefinitely. Eventually the muscle cannot produce enough ATP and lactic acid accumulates, so it stops contracting despite continued stimulation.

Tetanus here is a normal physiological state, not the disease. It is what a muscle does whenever you hold anything.

Exam tip

Motor unit size is inversely related to precision. Eye and finger muscles have tiny motor units; calf and thigh muscles have huge ones. The exam asks this as "which has more cells per motor unit."

Exam tip

6.5Energy for Muscle Contraction

pp. 109
Big ideaFour energy sources in a fixed sequence, chosen by how long the activity lasts — and oxygen debt is the bill for the anaerobic middle.
  • A single contracting muscle cell can require as much as 600 TRILLION ATP molecules per second simply to form and break cross-bridges.
  • FOUR SOURCES, used in sequence by duration and intensity. SEE FIGURE 6.9 1. ATP stored in muscle cells — depleted in about 6 seconds of vigorous exercise. 2. CREATINE PHOSPHATE stored in muscle cells — a high-energy bond connects the creatine and phosphate. A resting muscle contains about SIX TIMES as much stored creatine phosphate as stored ATP, and its energy converts ADP to ATP. Supports the next 10 seconds. Diving, weight lifting and sprinting are powered entirely by ATP and creatine phosphate reserves. 3. ANAEROBIC (without oxygen) metabolic pathways. 4. AEROBIC (with oxygen) respiration.
  • GLUCOSE AND GLYCOGEN — the primary fuel for either pathway is glucose, coming mainly from GLYCOGEN stored in muscle and liver cells. When a cell runs short of ATP and creatine phosphate, enzymes begin converting glycogen to glucose.
    • About 1.5% of a muscle cell's total weight is glycogen.
    • Long-term activity may deplete a muscle's glycogen reserves — the overwhelming fatigue runners call "hitting the wall", cyclists call "bonking", and boxers call becoming "arm weary."
  • ANAEROBIC VS AEROBIC.
    • The cardiovascular system can supply enough oxygen for aerobic pathways to power low levels of activity, even prolonged.
    • Anaerobic pathways produce ATP 2.5 TIMES FASTER than aerobic pathways, so during strenuous activity lasting 30 or 40 seconds they supply the ATP. Burstlike activities such as tennis or soccer rely nearly completely on anaerobic pathways.
    • In anaerobic pathways the pyruvic acid produced in glycolysis is converted to LACTIC ACID.
    • During more prolonged activity the body gradually switches back to aerobic pathways. Oxygen comes from two sources: oxygen bound to hemoglobin in the blood (the heart rate rises and blood is shunted to the neediest tissues) and MYOGLOBIN, an oxygen-binding pigment within muscle cells.
    • Aerobic pathways produce more than 90% of the ATP for intense activity lasting longer than 10 minutes, and nearly 100% for a truly prolonged effort such as a marathon.
  • OXYGEN DEBT — after prolonged exercise a person continues to breathe heavily for several minutes. The extra oxygen repays the debt created by muscles using more ATP than aerobic metabolism provided.
    • Most of that oxygen is used to generate the ATP needed to convert LACTIC ACID BACK TO GLUCOSE.
    • In addition, the oxygen released by myoglobin is replaced and glycogen and creatine phosphate reserves are restored.

6.6Slow-Twitch and Fast-Twitch Muscle Cells

pp. 110
Big ideaThe aerobic/anaerobic trade-off of 6.5 built into two cell types — endurance versus power — and distributed unequally through the body.
  • SLOW-TWITCH — contract slowly when stimulated, but with enormous endurance.
    • Dark and reddish, because packed with the oxygen-binding pigment MYOGLOBIN and richly supplied with blood vessels.
    • Contain abundant MITOCHONDRIA, where aerobic ATP production occurs.
    • Because they can produce ATP aerobically for a long time, they are specialised for prolonged, strong contractions.
  • FAST-TWITCH — contract rapidly and powerfully, but with far less endurance.
    • Have a form of an enzyme that splits ATP bound to myosin more quickly, so they make and break cross-bridge attachments faster and contract more rapidly.
    • Wider diameter, packed with more actin and myosin, which adds to their power.
    • Rich in glycogen and dependent more heavily on ANAEROBIC ATP production, so they tire more quickly.
  • DISTRIBUTION — unequal through the body.
    • Abdominal muscles need to contract steadily rather than rapidly, balancing the powerful, slow-twitch back muscles so we stand upright.
    • Fast-twitch cells are more common in the legs and arms, because limbs may be called on to move quickly.
  • PEOPLE VARY, partly genetically.
    • The leg muscles of endurance athletes such as marathoners are about 80% slow-twitch.
    • Those of sprinters are about 60% fast-twitch.
    • To some extent these differences are genetic — a "fast-twitch person" can build endurance only to a degree.

Key terms

slow-twitch muscle cells
dark, myoglobin-rich, aerobic, high endurance
fast-twitch muscle cells
pale, glycogen-rich, anaerobic, powerful but quick to fatigue
Exam tip

The colour is the mnemonic. Dark = myoglobin = oxygen = aerobic = endurance = slow-twitch. Every property follows from the oxygen story.

Exam tip

The chicken question on the "Applying the Concepts" page is this section. Ground-dwelling birds run constantly (dark leg meat, slow-twitch) and fly only in short bursts (white breast meat, fast-twitch).

6.7Building Muscle

pp. 111–112
Big ideaAerobic exercise improves supply and does not enlarge muscle; resistance exercise above 75% of maximum force enlarges existing cells — and both reverse if you stop.
  • Exercise can greatly influence muscle development. The hormone TESTOSTERONE also builds muscle. Different kinds of exercise produce different results.
  • AEROBIC EXERCISE — walking, jogging, swimming; enough oxygen is delivered to keep the muscles going for long periods.
    • Fosters development of new blood vessels servicing the muscles and more mitochondria.
    • Increases muscle coordination, improves digestive tract movement, and increases the strength of the skeleton by exerting force on the bones.
    • Brings cardiovascular and respiratory improvements — it enlarges the heart so that each stroke pumps more blood.
    • Aerobic exercise generally does NOT increase muscle size.
  • RESISTANCE EXERCISE — lifting heavy weights; the source of muscular development. SEE FIGURE 6.11
    • To build muscle tone and mass you have to make your muscles exert more than 75% of their maximum force.
    • These exercises can be very brief — only a few minutes every other day for a year are needed to build 50% more muscle and improve tone and strength.
    • The bulk results from an increase in DIAMETER of the existing muscle cells — although some researchers suggest heavy exercise splits or tears the cells and each part regrows larger.
    • Machines vs free weights — Nautilus-type machines automatically adjust the resistance, valuable because muscles are weaker at some parts of their range of motion than at others; the design makes lifting easier where the muscle is weaker and harder where it is strong. Free weights require more training to use safely.
    • You have to keep at it — all the muscle mass built last year began to disappear 2 weeks after the training stopped.
  • OTHER BENEFITS — exercise improves mood, reduces depression and anxiety, improves sleep quality; weight-bearing exercise strengthens bones; it burns calories in a weight-loss program.
  • STARTING SAFELY — begin slowly; seek medical advice first if you have a medical condition; warm up by walking and gently stretching to improve blood flow; increase duration gradually; cool down by walking and stretching as before.
  • DOMS — delayed onset muscle soreness, tenderness and weakness a day or two after intense exercise.
    • Especially common after exercises that force a muscle to LENGTHEN WHILE IT IS CONTRACTING.
    • More likely after running downhill than on level ground — also walking down stairs, climbing down a mountain, skiing, horseback riding.
    • The cause is not known for certain — suggested causes are microscopic muscle damage or an inflammatory response.

Key terms

aerobic exercise
sustained activity with adequate oxygen delivery
resistance exercise
lifting against a load above 75% of maximum force
delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS)
soreness a day or two after intense exercise
Watch out

Muscles get bigger by widening existing cells, not by adding new ones. The chapter states the diameter increase as the main explanation and flags cell splitting as a minority view.

Exam tip

75% of maximum force is the number to remember — it is the threshold the chapter gives for building mass, and Figure 6.11's caption repeats it.

Exam tip

DOMS comes from lengthening under load. That is why downhill running hurts more than uphill, which is counterintuitive enough to be an exam question.

Stop And Think

1(p. 106) How would the degree of rigor mortis help a forensic scientist determine the approximate time of death of a corpse?

Rigor mortis follows a known timetable driven by ATP. Calcium leaks from the sarcoplasmic reticulum soon after death and starts contraction; once the ATP supply runs out the cross-bridges cannot be broken, so stiffness sets in within 3 to 4 hours. It then passes as actin and myosin break down, after 2 to 3 days. A body that is still limp died very recently or several days ago; one that is rigid died a few hours to a couple of days ago. Reading where in that cycle the body sits brackets the time of death. Derived from 6.3; no printed answer.

2(p. 107) Curare prevents acetylcholine from binding to muscle. The diaphragm is a voluntary skeletal muscle necessary for breathing. How do poison arrows cause death?

Contraction depends on acetylcholine binding to receptors on the muscle cell membrane to create the electrochemical message that travels into the T tubules and releases calcium. Curare blocks that binding, so no calcium is released, the binding sites stay covered by troponin–tropomyosin, and the muscle cannot contract. Because the diaphragm is skeletal muscle, it is paralysed along with the rest — breathing stops and the victim suffocates. Derived from 6.3; no printed answer.

3(p. 109) Creatine supplements boost performance in sports requiring short bursts but not endurance sports. Why? And creatine does not increase muscle mass, yet enhances sprint performance — how?

Creatine phosphate is the second energy source, good for roughly the 10 seconds after stored ATP runs out, and it is used entirely by diving, weight lifting and sprinting. Endurance events run on aerobic pathways, which creatine does not feed — so supplementing it adds nothing past the first several seconds. It enhances sprinting without adding mass because it is fuel, not structure: more stored creatine phosphate means more ADP converted back to ATP during the burst, so the existing cross-bridge cycling can continue at full rate a little longer. Derived from 6.5; no printed answer.

4(Consider This Case, p. 112) Hakeem, 22, ate some of his aunt's canned tomatoes. The next morning his speech was slurred and he had trouble standing and walking. Botulism is suspected; the botulinum toxin prevents the release of acetylcholine at neuromuscular junctions. Explain how his symptoms are related to this effect, and why botulism food poisoning can be fatal.

Without acetylcholine release, the nerve impulse never reaches the muscle cell — no membrane message, no T tubule signal, no calcium release, no contraction. The result is flaccid paralysis, which explains slurred speech (the fine motor units of tongue and face, which have very few cells each, fail first and most visibly) and difficulty standing and walking (the large postural and leg muscles lose the tone described in 6.4). It is fatal because the diaphragm is a skeletal muscle driven by the same kind of junction: when it is paralysed, inhalation stops and the person suffocates. Compare curare — curare blocks the receptor, botulinum blocks the release; the downstream failure is identical. Derived from 6.3 and 6.4; no printed answer.

5(Applying the Concepts, p. 114) "Texting thumb" is a form of tendinitis. What would cause it? What would the symptoms be? How would you treat it?

Overuse. Tendinitis is inflammation of a tendon caused by overuse, misuse or age, and repeated thumb movement on a handheld device is sustained repetition of one small motion. Symptoms would be pain and inflammation at the base of the thumb, worse with use. Treatment is rest — "if it hurts, do not use it" — and the chapter's reason matters: tendons are poorly supplied with blood vessels and so heal slowly, which is why rest rather than activity is the effective treatment. Derived from 6.2; no printed answer.

6(Applying the Concepts, p. 114) A scientist proposes a muscle-relaxing drug that works by flooding the muscle cell with calcium ions. As CEO, would you finance it?

No. Calcium does the opposite of what the pitch claims: it binds troponin, shifts tropomyosin off the binding sites, and initiates contraction. Flooding the cell with calcium would cause sustained contraction, not relaxation — relaxation happens when membrane pumps clear calcium out. Worse, 6.3 states that excess calcium entering a muscle cell destroys proteins and kills it — the mechanism of Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The drug would be both ineffective and toxic. Derived from 6.3; no printed answer.

7(Applying the Concepts, p. 114) Chickens run to escape predators and fly only short distances. How does that explain the distribution of white and dark meat?

Meat is skeletal muscle and its colour is myoglobin. The legs are used constantly and for sustained effort, so they are built of slow-twitch cells — myoglobin-rich, richly vascularised, mitochondria-packed, and therefore dark. The breast muscles power short, powerful bursts of flight, so they are fast-twitch — glycogen-rich, anaerobic, low in myoglobin, and therefore white and quick to fatigue. Derived from 6.6; no printed answer.

8(What Would You Do?, p. 111) Gene therapy may soon let athletes repair and rebuild muscle. Would you use it to repair damaged muscle tissue? To build muscle and enhance performance? Should any of these uses be illegal?

An opinion prompt, not a factual one — the book supplies the context (the IOC and World Anti-Doping Agency are concerned; the therapy repairs and rebuilds muscle in old mice, and human trials are under way in muscular dystrophy) and expects a reasoned position. The line most answers draw is therapy versus enhancement, and the chapter's steroid essay supplies the parallel: the side effects fall hardest on the reproductive and cardiovascular systems, and greater muscle strength is not matched by stronger tendons and ligaments, so enhancement carries injury risk on its own terms.

Figure Questions

1Figure 6.9 (p. 109) — Which energy source would fuel walking up a single flight of stairs?

ATP already stored in the muscle cells. Climbing one flight takes only a few seconds, and stored ATP covers about 6 seconds of vigorous activity — the effort ends before creatine phosphate is needed. Derived from 6.5; no printed answer.

2Figure 6.10 (p. 110) — Which type of muscle cell is best suited for cross-country skiing? Which for weight lifting?

Cross-country skiing — slow-twitch, because it is prolonged and aerobic, and slow-twitch cells are myoglobin-rich with abundant mitochondria. Weight lifting — fast-twitch, because it is a short, maximal burst powered by stored ATP and creatine phosphate, and fast-twitch cells are wider, richer in actin and myosin, and contract rapidly. Derived from 6.5 and 6.6; no printed answer.

Ethical Issue

Anabolic Steroid Abuse (p. 111)

  • Allegations and confessions of anabolic steroid use have rocked professional sports, reflecting a growing problem of steroid use in general, particularly among young adults.
  • Steroid abuse was at least 1% lower in 2011 than in 2000 among students in grades 8–12. A 2007 confidential NIDA-funded survey found 1.6% of eighth-graders, 1.8% of tenth-graders and 2.7% of seniors had used steroids. Of the students who admitted steroid use, 57% said steroid use by professional athletes influenced their decision.
  • Anabolic steroids are a class of drugs related to the steroid hormone TESTOSTERONE. These synthetic hormones mimic testosterone's ability to stimulate the body to build muscle, often giving a dramatic increase in strength, by stimulating protein formation in muscle cells and reducing the rest needed between workouts.
  • More than 70 possible unwanted side effects, from acne to liver cancer. The most common and most serious involve the liver, the cardiovascular system and the reproductive system.
  • Cardiovascular problems — heart attacks and strokes — may not show up for years, but reproductive effects are more immediate.
  • Male users: the testicles often become smaller, and the user may become sterile and impotent. These effects occur because anabolic steroids inhibit natural testosterone production.
  • Female users develop irreversible masculine traits — deeper voice, growth of body hair, loss of scalp hair, smaller breasts, enlarged clitoris.
  • The intended effect carries its own injury risk — greater muscle strength is not accompanied by a corresponding increase in the strength of tendons and ligaments, and injuries to those may take a long time to heal.
  • Alex Rodriguez was suspended for a year over steroid abuse (photo caption).

Write

The six functions

  • Support — a rigid framework holding soft tissues; leg bones and backbone hold the body upright, the pelvic girdle supports the abdominal organs.
  • Movement — attachment points for muscles; bones move at joints when muscles contract.
  • Protection — heart and lungs in the chest cavity, brain in the skull.

The four structures

  • Diaphysis — the shaft, mostly compact bone.
  • Epiphysis — the enlarged end, mostly spongy bone with a compact shell, capped with cartilage where it glides on another bone.
  • Medullary cavity — the space inside the shaft, holding yellow marrow in an adult.

Exam tip

Red and yellow marrow differ in place as well as job. Red in spongy bone — ribs, pelvis, backbone, skull, sternum, long-bone ends — and it makes blood cells. Yellow in the medullary cavity of the shaft, and it stores fat. Location alone answers the question.

Exam tip

The lengthening story and the fusing story are the same story. Sex hormones drive the growth-plate cartilage into a frenzy of division at puberty, then close the plate. After fusion a bone can still widen — it cannot lengthen.

Write

What it is

  • Compact bone is very dense, with few internal spaces. It forms most of the diaphysis of a long bone and the outer shell everywhere else.
  • The osteon is its microscopic repeating structural unit — mature bone cells (osteocytes) in concentric rings around a central canal.
  • Each osteocyte lies in a lacuna in the hardened matrix. Tiny canals connect nearby lacunae and eventually the central canal, and osteocytes send projections through them to touch neighbouring cells.
  • Nutrients, oxygen and wastes pass cell to cell, to and from the blood vessels in the central canal.

What the matrix is made of, and why it matters

  • Hardness comes from mineral salts — mainly calcium and phosphorus.
  • Resilience comes from collagen, a strong elastic protein woven through it.
  • Without the mineral salts bone would be rubbery like a garden hose. Without collagen it would be brittle and crumbly like chalk.

What it does

  • Bears weight and compression — which is why it is thickest along the shaft, where the load runs.
  • Provides the attachment surface for tendons and ligaments.
Watch out

Osteoblast and osteocyte are the same cell before and after. The osteoblast secretes collagen and deposits calcium salts; once walled into the matrix it is an osteocyte. Osteoblasts cannot divide — only cartilage cells can.

Exam tip

Compact and spongy bone differ in arrangement, not in composition. Both are hardened by calcium and phosphorus. One is packed with osteons, the other is an open latticework.

Write

What it is

  • A latticework of tiny beams and thin plates with open areas between, bracing the bone from within.
  • Found largely in small flat bones — most of the skull — and in the epiphyses and near the ends of the shafts of long bones.
  • The open spaces hold red marrow, where blood cells are formed.

What it does

  • Strength without weight. The lattice braces the bone along the lines of stress using a fraction of the material solid bone would need.
  • Houses red marrow, so it is where blood cell production happens.
  • Absorbs and distributes force arriving at the joint surface, spreading it into the shaft rather than letting it land on one point.

Exam tip

Spongy bone is where remodeling shows first. Bone answers to mechanical stress — weight-bearing thickens it, disuse thins it, and osteoporosis is that balance tipped the wrong way. The lattice is what thins.

Write

What the appendicular skeleton is

  • A girdle is a skeletal structure that supports the limbs. The appendicular skeleton is the two girdles and the limbs hanging from them — the half of the skeleton built to move the body from place to place, where the axial half protects and supports the internal organs.
  • The pectoral girdle connects the arms to the rib cage. The pelvic girdle connects the legs to the vertebral column.
  • 206 bones in the adult skeleton, 80 of them axial — everything else is appendicular.

The pectoral girdle and the arm

  • Pectoral girdle — the two scapulae (shoulder blades) and the two clavicles (collarbones).
  • Each clavicle forms a relatively rigid bridge between a scapula and the sternum.
  • The scapula is roughly triangular, and one corner carries the socket that takes one end of the humerus, the upper arm bone.
  • Humerus → radius and ulna → 8 carpals → 5 metacarpals → phalanges.
  • The elbow is formed by the extension of the ulna past its junction with the humerus.
  • Each finger has three phalanges; the thumb has two. Toes use the same word.

The pelvic girdle and the leg

  • Pelvic girdle — the two pelvic bones, and much more rigid than the pectoral girdle.
  • Each pelvic bone attaches behind to the sacrum, curves down and round to the front, and there the two meet a cartilage disk at the pubic symphysis.
  • Femur → tibia and fibula → tarsals → metatarsals → phalanges.
  • The femur rotates within a socket in the pelvis. At the knee it meets the tibia (shinbone), with the smaller fibula running down the side, and the patella covers the junction.

Carpal tunnel

  • Carpal tunnel — a narrow opening through the carpal bones, carrying a nerve that controls sensation in the fingers and some hand muscles, plus tendons, bands of connective tissue attaching muscle to bone.
  • Repetitive motion inflames those tendons so they press against the nerve — numbness or tingling in the hand, and pain in the wrist, hand and fingers. That is carpal tunnel syndrome, seen in barbers, cab drivers and pianists, and increasingly with keyboards and video games.

Telling the sex of a skeleton — both ways are in this one topic

  • The clavicles are more curved in males than in females.
  • The opening of the pelvic girdle is wider in the female, to facilitate childbirth.
Watch out

Carpal tunnel syndrome is a nerve problem caused by tendons. The tendons inflame; the nerve is what hurts. A question asking what is compressed wants the nerve.

Exam tip

The chapter never counts the appendicular bones. It gives 206 in the adult skeleton and 80 axial, and leaves the subtraction to you. Learn the bones in order along each limb rather than a total.

What every synovial joint has

  • A thin layer of cartilage on the surfaces that move past one another, reducing friction so the bones slide without grating.
  • A two-layered capsule. The inner layer — the synovial membrane — secretes viscous clear synovial fluid into the joint cavity, which lubricates and cushions.
  • Ligaments — strong straps of connective tissue that hold the bones together, support the joint and direct the movement.
  • Hinge joints (knee, elbow) move in one plane. Ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip) move in all planes.

The failures

  • Sprain = damage to a ligament, from slight overstretching to a full tear. Ligaments are dense in pain receptors sensitive to stretching and swelling, so the injury may be less severe than the pain suggests.
  • Ice first, then heat. Ice constricts vessels and reduces bleeding; heat then dilates them to bring in nutrients, oxygen and anti-inflammatory chemicals. Ligaments, like tendons, have few blood vessels and heal slowly.
  • Bursae — fluid-filled sacs lined with synovial membrane, found where skin rubs over bone and between tendons, muscles or ligaments and bone. Bursitis is an inflamed bursa: intense pain, worse on movement, not relieved by resting in any position; usually subsides in a week or two.
  • Osteoarthritis is wear — cartilage degenerates until bones grind; weight-bearing joints (hip, knee, spine); prevented in part by controlling weight and exercising.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune — the body attacks its own synovial membranes; fluid accumulates, cartilage is destroyed and may be replaced by fibrous tissue. Permanent.
Watch out

Ligament, tendon, cartilage — three connective tissues, three jobs. Ligament: bone to bone at a joint. Tendon: muscle to bone. Cartilage: the low-friction surface. Both ligaments and tendons are poorly supplied with blood and heal slowly.

Write

The levels, in order

  • Muscle → fascicle → muscle fiber → myofibril → myofilament → sarcomere. Each individual muscle is covered by a membrane of fibrous connective tissue; the sheaths merge and condense at the ends to form the tendons that attach muscle to bone.
  • Muscle fibers are the muscle cells. They can be several centimetres long — a thigh muscle cell can reach 30 cm.
  • Myofibrils are bundles of contractile protein; the myofilaments make up about 80% of the cell volume.
  • A sarcomere is the contractile unit, running Z line to Z line. Each myofibril has tens of thousands of them along its length.

How muscles are arranged on the skeleton

  • Origin — the attachment on the bone that stays relatively stationary. Insertion — the attachment on the bone that moves. The bones act as levers.
  • Antagonistic pairs — one contracts and pulls a bone one way; its partner must relax. To move the bone back, the first relaxes and the other contracts. Biceps flexes the arm at the elbow; triceps extends it.
  • Synergistic muscles — muscles that contract at the same time to produce one movement.
  • The body has more than 600 skeletal muscles.
Watch out

Origin and insertion are defined by which bone moves, not by which end is closer to the body.

Write

The model in one sentence

  • A muscle contracts when actin filaments slide along myosin filaments, increasing the overlap and shortening the sarcomere. When many sarcomeres shorten, the whole muscle contracts. The filaments do not shorten — the sarcomere does.

The cycle, four steps

  • Resting. The myosin heads have already split ATP into ADP + P_i_; the stored energy has swivelled them out toward the Z lines — cocked, like a pistol.
  • 1 — Cross-bridge attachment. The heads bind the nearest actin filament. A bound head bridges thick and thin filament — hence cross-bridge.
  • 2 — Power stroke. The stored energy is released, swinging the heads back to their bent positions and pulling actin toward the midline of the sarcomere. ADP and P_i_ leave the head.
  • 3 — Detachment. New ATP binds the myosin heads and they let go of actin.
  • 4 — Reactivation. The heads split that ATP and store the energy, cocking again. The cycle repeats hundreds of times a second.

What switches it on

  • Calcium is the switch. At rest the troponin–tropomyosin complex covers the myosin-binding sites on actin. Ca2+ binds troponin, which changes shape and shifts tropomyosin off the sites, so myosin can bind.
  • Calcium is stored in the sarcoplasmic reticulum and released when the signal arrives; relaxation happens when membrane pumps clear calcium back out and the complex re-covers the sites.
Watch out

Calcium and ATP do different jobs. Calcium uncovers the binding sites. ATP cocks the head and, in a separate step, detaches it. The pull itself is powered by energy stored from an earlier ATP split, not by the ATP that binds in step 3.

Watch out

Rigor mortis is the proof. After death calcium leaks out and starts contraction, but ATP production stops — and without ATP the cross-bridges cannot be broken. Stiffness by 3–4 hours; it passes after 2–3 days as actin and myosin break down. If ATP powered the pull rather than the release, a dead muscle would go limp.

Write

The four steps

  • 1. A nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction — the junction between the tip of a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle cell.
  • 2. The impulse causes release of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter, from small packets in the neuron tip.
  • 3. Acetylcholine diffuses across a small gap and binds receptors on the muscle cell membrane, changing its permeability and creating an electrochemical message like a nerve impulse.
  • 4. The message runs along the plasma membrane, into the T tubules, then to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, opening channels that release calcium ions — and Block 10 takes over.

What the T tubules are for

  • Transverse tubules are tiny cylindrical pockets of the muscle cell's plasma membrane reaching into the cell. They carry the signal from the surface deep inside, to virtually every sarcomere — not the calcium, the signal.

Motor units, if the topic runs long

  • A motor unit is a motor neuron and all the muscle cells it stimulates, and all of them contract together — about 150 cells on average.
  • Fine control means small motor units: an eye muscle neuron may control 3 cells, a calf muscle neuron thousands.
  • Recruitment — the nervous system raises the strength of contraction by stimulating more motor units.
Exam tip

Three poisons break three different steps of this one drawing, and they make good presentation material. Botulinum toxin stops acetylcholine being released. Curare stops it binding. Both cause flaccid paralysis, and both kill by paralysing the diaphragm, which is skeletal muscle. Duchenne muscular dystrophy is the opposite failure — too much calcium enters and kills the cell.

Exam tip

The T tubule is the answer to "how does a surface signal reach the middle of a 30 cm cell." That is the exam framing.
