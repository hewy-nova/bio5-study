Did you know

The chapter as one chain

  • Living requires energy — the digestive system breaks food into simple molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids) that are carried to cells and used to make ATP; some energy is lost as heat.
  • Two ways to break down glucose — cellular respiration (needs oxygen) and fermentation (doesn't). Both are catabolic pathways.
  • Respiration is stepwise on purpose — burning glucose in one step would waste its energy as heat; four phases capture it as ATP.
  • Glycolysis, transition reaction, citric acid cycle — glucose is split, carbons are stripped off as CO2, and electrons are loaded onto NADH and FADH2.
  • The electron transport chain cashes in — carrier proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane pass electrons to oxygen, making 32 of the 36 ATP.
  • Without oxygen, fermentation keeps glycolysis running — it regenerates NAD+ but nets only 2 ATP; in human muscle it produces lactic acid.

3.7Cellular Respiration and Fermentation in the Generation of ATP

pp. 58–62
Big ideaCells capture the energy in glucose as ATP. Oxygen-dependent cellular respiration yields 36 ATP per glucose; fermentation works without oxygen but yields only 2.
  • Energy source. Digestion (Chapter 15) breaks carbohydrates, proteins and fats into glucose, amino acids and fatty acids; the blood carries these to cells, where some of their bond energy makes ATP and some is given off as heat. The section focuses on carbohydrates.
  • Two pathways for glucose. Cellular respiration requires oxygen; fermentation does not.
  • Metabolism. All the chemical reactions in a cell, organized into metabolic pathways: a series of enzyme-driven steps that modify a starting molecule into a product.
    • Catabolic pathways break complex molecules into simpler ones and release energy (cellular respiration, fermentation).
    • Anabolic pathways build complex molecules from simpler ones and consume energy.
  • Cellular respiration. The oxygen-requiring pathway that breaks down glucose; final products are carbon dioxide, water and energy.
    • Burning glucose with oxygen in a beaker happens in one step and loses all the energy as heat. Cells go step by step and keep much of the energy as high-energy bonds in ATP (ATP is formed from ADP + phosphate, which takes energy; see Chapter 2).
    • Four phases, all running continuously: (1) glycolysis, in the cytoplasm; (2) transition reaction, (3) citric acid cycle and (4) electron transport chain, in the mitochondria.
    • Products of one reaction become the substrates (raw materials) for the next. Electron transfer between molecules is the key to capturing energy: as electrons pass along a chain of intermediates, their energy is used to make ATP.
    • Blood delivers oxygen (enters cells by simple diffusion) and glucose (enters by facilitated diffusion).
  • Glycolysis (glyco = sugar, lysis = splitting). SEE FIGURE 3.22
    • Glucose (6 carbons) is split into two 3-carbon sugars, which are converted into two pyruvate (3 carbons each).
    • Several steps, each with its own enzyme. The first steps spend 2 ATP to prepare glucose for splitting; later steps make 4 ATP, a net gain of 2 ATP.
    • Produces 2 NADH when electrons are donated to the coenzyme NAD+. Coenzymes are organic molecules that help enzymes convert substrate to product.
    • Needs no oxygen and releases only a little of glucose's energy; most stays in the two pyruvates, which move from the cytoplasm into the inner compartment of the mitochondrion.
  • Transition reaction. SEE FIGURE 3.23
    • Inside the mitochondrion's inner compartment, pyruvate reacts with coenzyme A (CoA).
    • One carbon is removed from each pyruvate as CO2. The remaining 2-carbon acetyl group binds CoA to form acetyl CoA, a high-energy fuel for the citric acid cycle.
    • One NADH is produced per pyruvate.
  • Citric acid cycle (also called the Krebs cycle, after Hans Krebs). SEE FIGURE 3.24
    • Still in the inner compartment. Acetyl CoA reacts with a 4-carbon compound in the first of a cyclic series of eight reactions; named for its first product, citric acid (citrate).
    • Completes the removal of electrons from glucose. Yields 2 ATP per glucose (one per acetyl CoA) plus several NADH and FADH2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide), which carry high-energy electrons.
    • Releases CO2 as waste.
    • NADH and FADH2 from glycolysis, the transition reaction and the citric acid cycle all feed the electron transport chain.
  • Electron transport chain. SEE FIGURE 3.25
    • NADH and FADH2 pass their electrons to a series of carrier proteins embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane (highly folded, leaving room for thousands of sets of carriers).
    • Energy released as electrons move from protein to protein is used to make ATP.
    • Oxygen is the final electron acceptor; it combines with two hydrogen ions to form water.
    • With no oxygen, electrons back up in the carriers, which halts the citric acid cycle and respiration.
    • Produces 32 ATP per glucose.
  • Health Issue: Mitochondrial Diseases. (condensed under BOXED ESSAYS)
  • Total yield. 36 ATP per glucose: 2 from glycolysis, 2 from the citric acid cycle, 32 from the electron transport chain. ATP powers work such as muscle contraction, the beating of cilia in the respiratory tract, and active transport across membranes. SEE TABLE 3.4 SEE FIGURE 3.26
  • Fermentation. Breaking down glucose without oxygen.
    • Starts with glycolysis in the cytoplasm: 2 pyruvate, 2 NADH, 2 ATP per glucose.
    • The remaining reactions, also in the cytoplasm, pass electrons from NADH to pyruvate (or a pyruvate derivative). This regenerates NAD+, which glycolysis needs to keep making ATP.
    • The final electron acceptor is pyruvate or a derivative, not oxygen. Net yield is only 2 ATP versus 36, so it is very inefficient.
  • Lactic acid fermentation in humans. During strenuous exercise, muscle oxygen runs low and cells increase lactic acid fermentation to keep making ATP. Lactic acid buildup partly causes post-exercise muscle pain. The soreness fades as lactic acid enters the blood and goes to the liver, which converts it back to pyruvate.
  • Looking ahead. Chapter 4 covers how specialized cells form tissues, organs and organ systems.

Stop And Think

1We breathe in oxygen so mitochondria can make ATP. Which of the four phases of cellular respiration make the CO2 we breathe out? (p. 61)

The transition reaction (one CO2 removed from each pyruvate) and the citric acid cycle (releases CO2 as waste). Glycolysis and the electron transport chain produce no CO2. The ETC uses oxygen and makes water.

2(Consider This Case, p. 62) Bethany has Pompe disease, a rare inherited disorder in which glycogen builds up in her cells, especially muscle cells, because she lacks the enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase, which normally breaks glycogen down into glucose. The buildup interferes with cell function and weakens her muscles. Is Pompe disease a mitochondrial disease, or is it tied to another organelle? Which one?

The organelle involved is the lysosome, the site of intracellular digestion (per end-of-chapter Q2); the "acid" in the enzyme's name fits the lysosome's acidic interior. Health Issue

Mitochondrial Diseases (p. 62)

  • Analogy: mitochondrial failure is like a power-grid outage, where services slow and fail.
  • More than 40 illnesses come from deficient energy-metabolism proteins in mitochondria, including electron transport chain proteins. Less energy leads to impaired cell function and possibly cell death, which then affects tissues and organs.
  • Worst damage lands on high-energy organs: brain, heart, kidneys, skeletal muscle.
  • An estimated 1000 to 4000 U.S. babies are born with a mitochondrial disease each year; probably underdiagnosed and misdiagnosed.
  • Cause: DNA mutations, spontaneous or inherited, in genes for mitochondrial energy proteins. Some of those proteins are encoded by mitochondrial DNA; others are encoded by nuclear DNA and imported into the mitochondria.
  • Inheritance: nuclear DNA comes from both parents, mitochondrial DNA only from the mother. Research suggests a maternal age effect: older mothers, and their children, carry more mtDNA mutations. Infections and some drugs can also damage mitochondria.
  • Symptoms: fatigue and failure to gain weight. Brain involvement gives seizures, developmental delay, dementia. Skeletal muscle gives weakness, cramps, exercise intolerance. Multiple systems can be affected.
  • Variable severity: a cell holds hundreds of mitochondria, some normal and some defective, and the proportion sets severity. Eggs differ in their share of defective mitochondria, so siblings with the same inherited mutation can differ sharply.
  • Onset can be in infancy, childhood or adulthood; adult onset may follow an environmental trigger such as a severe illness.
  • Course is hard to predict, from good quality of life to death in childhood. No cure; treatment manages symptoms (dietitian for weight, physical therapy for muscle) and avoids energy stress such as fasting and extreme cold.
  • Questions to Consider (worked answers):
  • Q :: A woman with an inherited, adult-onset disease traced to her mitochondrial DNA was diagnosed after having two sons and a daughter. Which children inherit the mutation?
  • Q :: What determines how severe an affected child's symptoms are? If all three children have families, which will pass the mutation on?

OPENING

Building on Chapter 14 (getting oxygen, removing CO2), this chapter covers how we get nutrients from food: food moves one way through the digestive system, the system is organized to break food molecules down for energy or for raw material for growth and repair, and the chapter closes with the nutrients of a balanced diet (proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, water) and how to combine foods for health and fitness.

The chapter as one chain

  • One tube, four layers — the GI tract is a single hollow tube (mouth to anus) with accessory glands emptying into it; its wall is mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, serosa.
  • Each compartment does one job — mouth starts mechanical digestion and starch digestion, pharynx/esophagus move food by swallowing and peristalsis, stomach stores, liquefies and starts protein digestion.
  • The small intestine finishes the job — pancreatic and intestinal enzymes plus bile complete digestion; folds, villi and microvilli make absorption possible; sugars and amino acids go to blood, fats go to lymph.
  • Accessory organs and the large intestine — pancreas, liver and gallbladder supply the secretions; the liver processes absorbed blood via the hepatic portal system; the large intestine recovers water and houses bacteria.
  • Timing is controlled — nerves (fast: saliva) and hormones (gastrin, VIP, secretin, CCK) release secretions only when food is present.
  • What to eat — MyPlate and DGAC guidance frame a balanced diet; each nutrient class (lipids, carbohydrates, proteins, vitamins, minerals, water) has a job and a healthier form.
  • Using the information — food labels translate nutrients into choices; energy balance (BMR + activity) sets calorie needs.
  • When intake goes wrong — anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa produce starvation-like damage, especially to heart and bone.

15.1The Gastrointestinal Tract

pp. 287–288
Big ideaThe digestive system is an assembly line in reverse — a long hollow tube that breaks complex food molecules into subunits small enough to be absorbed, with a four-layer wall that does the moving, mixing and absorbing.
  • "You are what you eat." The hamburger does not become a hamburger — it becomes you. Like an assembly line in reverse, the digestive system takes food and breaks complex organic molecules into their chemical subunits, small enough to be absorbed into the bloodstream and delivered to body cells, where they supply materials for growth and repair or energy for daily activities.
    • The book's own worked example: a cheeseburger on a bun — the starch in the bun may fuel a jump for joy; the protein in the beef may be used to build muscle; the fat in the cheese may become myelin sheaths that insulate nerve fibers.
    • SEE TABLE 15.1 — the structures of the digestive system and their roles in mechanical and chemical digestion. The text says to refer to it while reading the rest of the section.
  • The gastrointestinal (GI) tract. The digestive system consists of a long hollow tube, the GI tract, into which various accessory glands release their secretions. It begins at the mouth and continues to the pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine. SEE FIGURE 15.1
    • Lumen — the hollow area of the tube through which food and fluids travel.
  • Along most of its length the GI tract wall has four basic layers. SEE FIGURE 15.2
    • Mucosa. The innermost layer — moist, mucus-secreting. The mucus lubricates the lumen so food slides through easily, and protects the cells lining the GI tract from rough substances in food and from digestive enzymes. In some regions cells of the mucosa also secrete digestive enzymes. In some parts of the tract the mucosa is highly folded, which increases the surface area for absorption.
    • Submucosa. Connective tissue containing blood vessels, lymph vessels and nerves. The blood supply maintains the cells of the digestive system and, in some regions, picks up and transports the products of digestion. The nerves are important in coordinating the contractions of the next layer.
    • Muscularis. Responsible for movement of materials through the GI tract and for mixing ingested materials with digestive secretions. In most sections it is a double layer of smooth muscle — "the stomach, as you will read later, is an exception; it has three layers of muscle."
      • Inner "circular" layer — muscle cells encircle the tube, causing a constriction when they contract.
      • Outer longitudinal layer — muscle cells run parallel to the GI tract; they shorten the GI tract when they contract.
      • Together the muscle layers churn the food until it is liquefied, mix the resulting liquid with enzymes, and propel the food along in a process called peristalsis.
      • Segmental contractions — local muscle contractions in random regions, especially in the small intestine. They mix intestinal contents — food and digestive juices — and help the body absorb digested food by moving intestinal contents over the intestinal wall.
    • Serosa. A thin layer of epithelial tissue supported by connective tissue, wrapping around the GI tract. It secretes a fluid that lubricates the outside of the GI tract to reduce friction with the intestine and other abdominal organs it contacts.

15.2Specialized Compartments for Food Processing

pp. 288–298
Big ideaFood passes through a sequence of compartments, each specialized for one stage of processing, with most digestion and absorption in the small intestine and water recovery in the large intestine.
  • Overview. Food passes mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine; salivary glands, liver and pancreas add secretions; most nutrients absorbed in the small intestine, more water in the large intestine; undigested material leaves via the anus.
  • Mouth (oral cavity). Roof is the palate: hard palate (front, bone-reinforced) and soft palate (back, muscle only; keeps food out of the nose when swallowing). Functions: begins mechanical and some chemical digestion, monitors food quality, moistens and manipulates food for swallowing.
  • Teeth and mechanical digestion. Incisors (front, chisel-like) slice; canines tear; premolars and molars grind and crush. SEE FIGURE 15.3
    • Tooth structure: pulp at the center (blood vessels and nerves), then dentin (hard, bonelike), then enamel on the crown (nonliving, hardened with calcium salts). Root covered by cementum (calcified but living). Roots sit in jawbone sockets; vessels and nerves enter via the root canal.
    • Tooth decay — mouth bacteria digest sugar in trapped food and make acid that erodes enamel into a cavity; widened pulp vessels can press on nerves (toothache); untreated, bacteria reach dentin then pulp. Plaque (bacteria, mucus, food) holds acid against enamel; brushing and flossing remove it.
    • Gum disease — affects two of three middle-aged Americans; major cause of adult tooth loss. Gingivitis: plaque at the gum line inflames the gums, leaving pockets that trap more plaque. Periodontitis: bacteria attack bone and soft tissue around the tooth, loosening it.
  • Salivary glands and chemical digestion. Three pairs — sublingual (under tongue), submandibular (below jaw), parotid (in front of ears) — secrete saliva. Water moistens food; mucus binds particles. SEE FIGURE 15.4
    • Salivary amylase begins chemical digestion of starch into shorter sugar chains (bread chewed for minutes tastes sweet).
  • Tongue. Skeletal muscle with taste buds; with olfactory input, monitors food quality (spit out spoiled food). Positions food for chewing, mixes it with saliva, shapes a bolus, and starts swallowing by pushing the bolus back.
  • Pharynx. The throat; shared by respiratory and digestive systems. Swallowing has a voluntary phase (tongue pushes bolus into pharynx) and an involuntary reflex phase once food is in the pharynx: soft palate blocks the nasal cavity, larynx rises, epiglottis covers the glottis, food enters the esophagus. SEE FIGURE 15.5
  • Esophagus. Muscular tube from pharynx to stomach. Peristalsis: stretch from food triggers circular muscle contraction behind the food mass (pinching it forward) while longitudinal muscle ahead of it contracts, shortening and widening the region to receive it. Gravity not required; you can swallow upside down or in space. SEE FIGURE 15.6
  • Stomach. Muscular sac with three functions: store food and regulate release to the small intestine, liquefy food, begin protein digestion. SEE FIGURE 15.7
    • Storage. Empty: small J-shaped sac holding about 50 ml; folds let it expand to several liters. Sphincters (circular muscle bands) guard both openings; contraction closes, relaxation opens.
    • Heartburn — stomach contents overwhelm the sphincter at the lower esophagus. Weak sphincter, or high pressure (large meal, pregnancy, lying down, constipation). Chronic heartburn (at least weekly) raises esophageal cancer risk (eight times normal at one attack a week). Detection by endoscopy or the swallowed-camera PillCam.
    • Liquefaction. Food held 2 to 6 hours; three smooth muscle layers in different directions twist, knead and compress; churned with gland secretions into chyme.
    • Protein digestion. Millions of gastric pits lead to gastric glands. Some cells secrete HCl (kills most swallowed bacteria, breaks down meat connective tissue, activates pepsinogen). Other cells secrete pepsinogen, which HCl converts to pepsin. Pepsin + HCl = gastric juice. Other cells secrete mucus. Gastric glands also secrete intrinsic factor, needed to absorb vitamin B12 in the small intestine.
    • Self-protection. Thick alkaline mucus coat; pepsin made in inactive form; neural and hormonal reflexes limit gastric juice unless food is present; rapid repair (half a million cells replaced per minute; a new lining every 3 days).
    • Little absorption occurs in the stomach; exceptions are alcohol (fast effect, especially on an empty stomach) and aspirin (can cause bleeding; avoid with ulcers).
  • Health Issue: Peptic Ulcers. SEE FIGURE 15.A (condensed under BOXED ESSAYS)
  • Small intestine. Two major functions: chemical digestion and absorption. Three regions: duodenum (receives chyme in squirts plus pancreatic and liver secretions), jejunum, ileum (where most digestion and absorption occur).
    • Chemical digestion. Enzymes complete digestion of carbohydrates, proteins, fats and nucleic acids; most of the work is done by pancreatic enzymes. SEE TABLE 15.2
    • Fat problem. Fats are water-insoluble and clump into globules; lipase is water-soluble and works only at a globule's surface.
    • Bile (water, ions, cholesterol, bile pigments, bile salts) mechanically digests fat: bile salts emulsify fat into small droplets, raising total surface area for lipase. Made by liver, stored in gallbladder, acts in small intestine.
  • Structure of the small intestine. Primary absorption site; long, with three surface-area multipliers. SEE FIGURE 15.8
    • Circular folds — accordion pleats; also make chyme flow in a spiral, improving mixing and contact.
    • Villi — 1 mm projections covering the lining (velvety, like towel pile); increase surface area tenfold.
    • Microvilli — microscopic projections on each absorptive epithelial cell; another 20x increase; form the brush border.
    • Total surface: 300 to 600 m2, bigger than a tennis court.
  • Absorption. Each villus core has a capillary network and a lacteal (lymphatic vessel); absorbed material crosses only two cell layers (villus epithelium, then capillary or lacteal wall). Entry into epithelial cells by active transport, facilitated diffusion or simple diffusion. SEE FIGURE 15.9
    • Monosaccharides, amino acids, water, ions, vitamins, minerals go to capillaries, then the bloodstream.
    • Fat: glycerol and fatty acids join bile salts to form micelles; on contact with a villus cell, the products diffuse in, are reassembled into triglycerides, mixed with cholesterol and phospholipids, coated with protein to form a chylomicron (protein coat makes it water-soluble). Chylomicrons leave by exocytosis, are too large for capillaries, and enter the more porous lacteal, then the lymphatic system, then the bloodstream.
  • Accessory organs: pancreas, liver, gallbladder. Not part of the GI tract; release secretions into the small intestine. SEE TABLE 15.3
  • Pancreas. Lies behind the stomach toward the left. Pancreatic juice goes to the pancreatic duct, which joins the common bile duct just before the duodenum. Contains enzymes, water and ions including bicarbonate, which neutralizes acidic chyme for optimal enzyme activity. Pancreatic and intestinal enzymes yield amino acids, monosaccharides, fatty acids and glycerol. SEE FIGURE 15.10
  • Liver. Largest internal organ. Nutrient-laden blood from villus capillaries travels through the hepatic portal vein to the liver. A portal system links two capillary beds (small intestine to liver). Hepatic veins return blood to circulation. SEE FIGURE 15.11
    • Roles: makes bile (primary digestive role); controls blood glucose (stores excess as glycogen, breaks glycogen down); packages lipids with protein into lipoproteins; stores iron and vitamins A, D, E, K, B12 and folate; removes toxins (lead, mercury, pesticides); converts amino acid breakdown products to urea. About 500 functions.
    • Cirrhosis — liver becomes fatty, deteriorates, replaced by scar tissue; sometimes from prolonged heavy alcohol use.
    • Hepatitis — liver inflammation, most often from one of six viruses (A, B, C, D, E, G). Damaged cells stop removing bilirubin (yellow pigment from red blood cell breakdown), producing jaundice (yellow skin and whites of eyes). About 4 million in the U.S. have hepatitis C, mostly unaware; spread mainly by contaminated blood (shared needles, piercing/tattoo needles, pre-screening transfusions).
  • Gallbladder. Muscular pear-shaped sac that stores, modifies and concentrates bile; a hormone makes it contract when chyme enters the small intestine, squirting bile through the common bile duct into the duodenum. Gallstones: a crystal precipitates from cholesterol-rich bile and cholesterol builds up around it; stones blocking bile flow may require gallbladder removal. SEE FIGURE 15.12
  • Large intestine. Functions: absorb most remaining water (setting feces consistency), store feces, eliminate them. Houses bacteria, some making absorbable vitamins.
    • Regions: cecum, colon, rectum, anal canal. Cecum — pouch below the small/large junction. Appendix — slender pouch off the cecum; no digestive function; possibly immune. SEE FIGURE 15.13
    • Appendicitis — about 1 in 500 per year; blockage (hardened stool, food, tumor) then bacterial infection. Vague bloating and pain near the navel, then severe pain in the lower right abdomen with fever, nausea, vomiting. Treated with antibiotics and removal; rupture spills contents and causes peritonitis (potentially fatal).
    • Colon — ascending (right), transverse (across the top), descending (left). Material entering is still liquid; the colon absorbs 90% of remaining water plus sodium and potassium ions. Feces: undigested food, sloughed epithelial cells, water, bacteria; brown from bile pigments.
    • Colon bacteria — beneficial; make vitamin K and some B vitamins; about 50 species including E. coli; feed on undigested material and release gas (flatus).
    • Lactose intolerance — no lactase, so lactose reaches the colon and bacteria ferment it into CO2 and methane (bloating, gas, discomfort). Not dangerous; managed with lactase tablets or avoiding dairy.
    • Defecation. Slow peristalsis allows water absorption. Feces stretch the rectal wall, triggering the defecation reflex via spinal cord, then rectal contraction. Internal sphincter relaxes automatically; external sphincter is voluntary; abdominal muscles can add pressure.
  • Disorders of the colon. SEE FIGURE 15.14
    • Diarrhea — material moves too fast (microbial toxins, excess food or alcohol), too little water absorbed; risk of dehydration, especially in young children; a major cause of death worldwide.
    • Constipation — too slow, too much water absorbed; hard, infrequent stools. Straining enlarges rectal/anal veins (hemorrhoids) and can push weak spots of the wall outward into diverticula.
    • Diverticulosis (pouches without symptoms; common after 50) vs diverticulitis (infected and inflamed: cramping pain, bowel changes, fever, rectal bleeding; treated by diet change such as more fiber, antispasmodic drugs, or surgery).
    • Colorectal cancer — second leading cause of cancer deaths. Starts as a noncancerous polyp that can take up to 10 years to turn cancerous. Blood in stool may be a sign (usually needs a test to detect), but many polyps don't bleed, so direct viewing is better: sigmoidoscopy (first third of colon) or colonoscopy (entire colon); polyps can be removed and biopsied.

15.3Nerves and Hormones in Digestion

pp. 299
Big ideaNerves and hormones time each digestive secretion to the arrival of food, so enzymes are never released into an empty tract.
  • Why control matters. The body is made of the same substances as food, so enzymes released without food present could digest the tract itself.
  • Saliva is nervously controlled. Food spends little time in the mouth and nerves are faster than hormones. Salivation starts at the thought, sight or smell of food; the main trigger is food in the mouth (flavor, pressure); flow continues after swallowing to rinse the mouth. SEE TABLE 15.4
  • Stomach. Neural reflexes during chewing start gastric juice and mucus secretion. Stomach distention and partially digested protein trigger release of gastrin, which travels through the blood back to the stomach and increases gastric juice.
  • Small intestine. Acidic chyme is the most important stimulus, acting through local nerve reflexes to release intestinal and pancreatic enzymes and bile. It also triggers hormones: SEE TABLE 15.5
    • Vasoactive intestinal peptide — from small intestine, back to small intestine; releases intestinal juices.
    • Secretin — stimulates pancreatic release of sodium bicarbonate to neutralize chyme.
    • Cholecystokinin (CCK) — pancreas releases enzymes; gallbladder contracts and releases bile.

15.5Nutrients

pp. 301–305 (Table 15.7 falls on 306)
Big ideaFood supplies energy, building blocks, regulatory molecules and water; each nutrient class has a job and a healthier and less healthy form.
  • What food provides. Energy for all cellular activity; building blocks for division, maintenance and repair; molecules such as vitamins to coordinate processes; water for the cellular environment and reactions.
    • Calorie — energy to raise 1 g of water 1 degree C. A kilocalorie is 1000 calories; popular usage (and this book) calls kilocalories "calories".
  • Essential nutrients. Cells (especially liver) can rebuild and convert subunits, but some amino acids and fatty acids can't be made in sufficient amounts: essential amino acids and essential fatty acids must come from the diet.
  • Nutrient — a substance in food that provides energy, becomes part of a structure, or functions in growth, maintenance or repair. Fats, carbohydrates, proteins can provide energy (proteins usually build structures and regulatory molecules). Vitamins, minerals, water and fiber provide no energy but are essential.
  • Lipids. Include fats, oils and cholesterol; 95% of food lipids are triglycerides (three fatty acids + glycerol).
    • Saturation: saturated fatty acids hold all the hydrogen possible; monounsaturated could hold two more; polyunsaturated four or more. Saturated fats: solid at room temperature, usually animal. Unsaturated fats (oils): liquid, usually plant.
    • Needs: cholesterol in all cell membranes; myelin sheaths; absorption and transport of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; skin oils; cholesterol is the base of steroid hormones.
    • Need is about one tablespoon a day; average adult eats 6 to 8 tablespoons (78 to 196 g). Linked to obesity, and high fat intake to cancers of colon, prostate, lungs, perhaps breast.
    • Atherosclerosis risk rises with blood cholesterol; recommended under 200 mg/dl; U.S. middle-aged average 215 mg/dl; lowering it can slow or reverse atherosclerosis.
    • Blood cholesterol comes mostly from the liver, not diet; dietary saturated fat raises blood cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol does; genetics matter; the diet-blood link is unclear.
    • Lipoproteins: LDLs ("bad") deliver cholesterol to cells but also deposit it in artery walls; HDLs ("good") carry cholesterol to the liver for elimination. Total cholesterol : HDL should be no more than 4:1; HDL above 60 mg/dl reduces risk.
    • Bad fats: saturated (raises LDL by boosting liver production and slowing clearance; red meat, butter, cheese, whole milk) and trans fats (hydrogen added to oils to stabilize/solidify, e.g., margarine, packaged foods; raise LDL and lower HDL). SEE FIGURE 15.16
    • Good fats: monounsaturated (olive, canola, peanut oils, nuts), polyunsaturated such as omega-3 (mackerel, lake trout, herring, tuna, salmon) and omega-6 (linoleic acid, an essential fatty acid, in corn and safflower oils); lower total cholesterol and LDL.
  • Carbohydrates. Mostly from plants: sugars, starches, fiber. Basic unit is the monosaccharide. SEE FIGURE 15.17
    • Simple carbohydrates (sugars) are mono- or disaccharides. In whole foods (fruit, milk) they come with other nutrients; refined sugars give "empty" calories (candy, cookies, cake, soda).
    • Complex carbohydrates (polysaccharides): starches (plant energy storage; long glucose chains; wheat, rice, oats, corn, potatoes, legumes) and fiber (indigestible plant part).
    • Main job: fuel. All carbohydrates except fiber are broken to simple sugars, mainly glucose.
    • Glycemic response — how fast and how much a food raises blood sugar. Glycemic index — 0 to 100 ranking with glucose = 100. High: sugar, white bread, potatoes, white rice. Low: whole fruit, whole grains, brown rice, barley (usually high in fiber).
    • After a high-glycemic food, insulin rises, lowering blood glucose by converting excess to fat, so hunger returns; in diabetes mellitus high-glycemic foods can cause problems.
    • Dietary fiber — in all plant foods; can't be digested to monosaccharides, so passes to the large intestine where some is digested by bacteria and the rest adds bulk. Lowers LDL but not HDL; helps constipation and hemorrhoids; absorbs water to soften stools.
  • Proteins. Chains of the 20 amino acids. Digested protein forms an amino acid pool; the body makes 11 amino acids; the other 9 are essential amino acids and must come from diet.
    • A protein can't be made if one needed amino acid is missing (NO PARKING sign analogy: only one R means only one sign); production stops or the body breaks down its own proteins.
    • Complete proteins (usually animal) have ample amounts of all essential amino acids; incomplete proteins (usually plant) are low in one or more. SEE FIGURE 15.18
    • Complementary proteins — combinations of incomplete plant proteins that together supply all essential amino acids; vegetarians must eat them. SEE FIGURE 15.19
    • Animal protein usually carries more fat; plant protein carries less fat and adds carbohydrate. Meat-centered U.S. meals drive excess fat intake; choose lean sources, skinless chicken, fish.
  • Vitamins. Organic compounds needed only in milligram or microgram amounts (a day's worth fills an eighth of a teaspoon) because they are not destroyed in use; most act as coenzymes, recycled repeatedly. SEE TABLE 15.6
    • 13 vitamins: 9 water-soluble (C and the B vitamins) and 4 fat-soluble (A, D, E, K).
    • Except vitamin D, cells can't make vitamins. Cooking can improve availability (cooked carrots give more vitamin A), but boiling leaches water-soluble vitamins; steaming preserves them.
    • Daily needs: folic acid (dark leafy greens) prevents neural tube birth defects such as spina bifida; with B6 and B12 may help prevent heart disease. Antioxidants (vitamin C, vitamin E, beta-carotene) protect against oxidative damage and may slow aging and protect against cancer, atherosclerosis, macular degeneration.
  • Minerals. Inorganic substances; fairly large amounts of seven (calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, sodium, chloride, magnesium) plus trace amounts of about a dozen others. Many are water-soluble and can be lost in cooking. SEE TABLE 15.7
    • Sodium — essential but overconsumed; raises blood pressure in some people; limit 2400 mg (slightly over a teaspoon) a day; processed foods are high in salt.
  • Water. Perhaps the most important nutrient: transports materials, lubricates and cushions organs, helps regulate temperature, medium for reactions. Survive about 8 weeks without food but only about 3 days without water. Recommended eight 8 oz glasses (2 qt) a day; fruits and vegetables supply about half; avoid carbonated sweet drinks (carbonation interferes with absorption; sugar adds empty calories), caffeine and alcohol (increase urine loss).

15.7Energy Balance

pp. 307
Big ideaCalories needed = basal metabolic rate plus physical activity; BMR is most of it and falls with age.
  • Uses of energy. Basic body functions, physical activity, processing food.
  • Basal metabolic rate (BMR). Minimum energy to keep an awake, resting body alive; 60% to 75% of energy needs.
    • Males usually higher (more muscle, less fat; muscle uses more energy): a man burns 10% to 20% more than a same-size woman at rest.
    • Muscle mass and metabolic rate drop with age; without lifestyle changes, extra pounds each year after 35.
    • Also influenced by health, food intake, genetics.
  • Physical activity. Second largest use of energy; boosts energy use during and for a while after exercise. Caloric need = BMR + exercise calories.
  • Exercise benefits. Guidelines: adults 18 to 64 should get at least 2 and a half hours of moderate activity a week. Aerobic exercise cuts heart and vessel disease risk and lowers blood pressure; weight-bearing exercise cuts osteoporosis risk; reduces stress, chronic disease (including diabetes) and adult weight gain.

15.8Eating Disorders

pp. 308
Big ideaAnorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa both create severe calorie and nutrient deficits whose effects resemble starvation and can be fatal, most often through the heart.
  • Obesity can be viewed as an overeating disorder; dieting is wise when overweight but can go too far — most anorexia and bulimia cases began with dieting.
  • Anorexia nervosa — deliberate self-starvation; body weight 85% or less of expected for height. Misnamed ("lack of appetite"): refusal stems from intense fear of fatness, with excessive exercise and a distorted body image (sees self as fat even when emaciated). SEE FIGURE 15.21
  • Bulimia nervosa — binge eating (a binge can last up to 8 hours and reach 20,000 calories) followed by purging (self-induced vomiting, enemas, laxatives, diuretics, excessive exercise).
  • Causes. Psychological, social and physiological factors; preoccupation with body size and shape; anxious depression may play a role.
  • Effects of bulimia. Esophageal injury, tooth decay and gum disease from vomiting; dehydration, constipation, electrolyte imbalance.
  • Effects of anorexia. Severe loss of bone health (amenorrhea, malnutrition, low body weight and fat outweigh any bone benefit from exercise). Starvation-like course: early diet is low-energy but protein-rich, which with high activity is nitrogen-sparing, so early loss is mostly fat; once fat is gone, the body breaks down skeletal and heart muscle protein for energy; cell water loss disturbs metabolism and electrolytes.
  • Mortality. Up to 20% die untreated; 2% to 3% even with treatment. Heart problems most common cause in anorexia (bradycardia, hypotension). Hypoglycemia can cause unconsciousness and death in either disorder because the brain runs on glucose.
  • Treatment. Usually involves the family and centers on psychotherapy toward a healthier body image.
  • Looking ahead. Chapter 15a covers causes and dangers of obesity.

15a.1What Is Obesity?

pp. 313
Big ideaOverweight means heavy for your height; obese means too much body fat — and BMI is the practical screen for it, with predictable exceptions at both ends.
  • Overweight and obese are not the same word. An obese person is overweight because of excess fat. An overweight person weighs more than the ideal on a height-and-weight chart.

15a.2Health Risks of Obesity

pp. 314
Big ideaThe reason to care is medical, not cosmetic — obesity drives cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and several cancers, and the three interact.
  • Motivation versus reason. Most dieters slim down for cosmetic reasons; the more important reasons are the health risks, and some of these risks interact with each other. SEE FIGURE 15a.3
  • Cardiovascular disease. Obesity raises total blood cholesterol while lowering HDL, the carrier that removes cholesterol from cells and delivers it to the liver for removal.
    • Those unhealthy values promote lipid deposits in the arteries — atherosclerosis.
    • Deposits partially or completely clog the artery, increasing blood pressure. High blood pressure can cause death from heart attack, stroke or kidney disease.
    • A deposit can rupture and form a blood clot, stopping flow and killing the cells downstream. A heart attack is heart muscle cells dying; a stroke is brain nerve cells dying.
  • Type 2 diabetes. Strongly correlated with obesity. Far more common than type 1; blood glucose is elevated because cells cannot use insulin. SEE FIGURE 15a.4
    • High blood glucose eventually damages small blood vessels in the eyes, kidneys or heart, and can itself cause atherosclerosis and high blood pressure — and so heart attack or stroke.
  • Cancer. Risk is increased for cancers of the colon and rectum, esophagus, kidney and pancreas. Obesity also raises the risk of breast cancer in postmenopausal women and endometrial cancer, probably because fat cells produce estrogen.

15a.3Regulation of Food Intake

pp. 315
Big ideaA hypothalamic circuit runs appetite and metabolism like a thermostat, leptin from fat cells is the signal it reads, and both genes and the environment can shift the setting.
  • The question. Not just how body weight is regulated but how much control over body weight we actually have — and the answer is not simple, because many factors are involved.
  • The hypothalamus coordinates communication among the digestive system, fat cells and the brain.
    • The "hunger circuit" is two sets of neurons with opposing actions — one stimulates appetite and food-seeking behaviour, the other inhibits it.
    • They turn appetite and metabolism up and down much the way a thermostat controls temperature.
  • Leptin is the signal — the "fat stat". A hormone released into the bloodstream by fat cells, so the amount of leptin reflects the body's energy stores.
    • Both sets of neurons have leptin receptors but respond in opposite ways, then release their own peptide hormone to influence appetite and food-seeking.
    • The circuit responds within seconds to the appearance or removal of food.
    • The fat set point: the hypothalamus maintains the amount of body fat needed to keep leptin at an optimal level. Leptin rises with fat gain → appetite decreases and metabolic rate increases. Fat is shed and leptin falls → appetite is stimulated and metabolism slows.
  • Two genes, from mouse studies. SEE FIGURE 15a.5
    • The obese (ob) gene carries the instructions to make leptin. Defective ob → meagre leptin regardless of how much body fat there is. The brain reads low leptin as low fat stores, so it stimulates appetite and slows metabolism.
    • The diabetes (db) gene defect produces defective leptin receptors. Mice and humans with defective db have very high leptin levels, but the brain cannot respond — the weak signal is again read as low fat stores, and again drives weight gain.
  • Epigenetics — DNA is not destiny. A stable alteration in gene expression without changes in DNA sequence; it regulates how genes are expressed without changing the proteins they encode.
    • The FTO (fat mass and obesity) gene is altered by diet. Risk of obesity in people with a genetic variant of FTO is dramatically increased with a high-fat diet, and foods high in folate and B vitamins reduce that risk by turning the FTO gene off.
    • It reaches across generations. "Your lifestyle can influence the health of your great-grandchildren." Maternal nutrition during pregnancy causes epigenetic changes in fetal gene activity that increase susceptibility to obesity, type 2 diabetes, heart disease and cancer.
  • Hunger is often not why we eat. Emotional states — depression, boredom, anxiety, stress — stimulate eating, as does the power of suggestion from advertisements, companions or the social situation.

15a.4Weight Management

pp. 316
Big ideaLosing weight and keeping it off takes three changes at once, and crash dieting actively sabotages the next attempt by lowering the metabolic rate.
  • Three components of a successful weight-loss program. (1) a reduction in calories consumed without departing from recommended nutritional guidelines; (2) an increase in energy expenditure; (3) behaviour modification.
    • Gradual changes in eating habits are most likely to lead to permanent lifestyle changes.
  • Three dietary tips the section gives.
    • Avoid sugar — sweet foods are packed with sugar and therefore calories, and sugar is a high-glycemic carbohydrate that quickly elevates blood glucose.
    • Cut back on saturated (animal) fat — fat contains more than twice as many calories as an equivalent weight of carbohydrate or protein, and unused fat from food is easier for the body to store as body fat than protein or carbohydrate is.
    • Increase fiber — high-fiber foods such as fruits and vegetables are low in calories and fat, high in vitamins and minerals, and bulky, so they are filling.
  • The yo-yo effect. 60% to 90% of dieters who lose weight later regain all of it. SEE FIGURE 15a.6
    • The usual reason: the loss was achieved by drastically cutting calories, which is unhealthy and very hard to sustain. Old habits return and so do the pounds; then the diet begins again.
  • Why the yo-yo effect makes each attempt harder.
    • Severely restricting calories makes the body adopt a calorie-sparing defence that reduces the resting metabolic rate by as much as 45%. This evolved to help our ancestors survive food scarcity.
    • Each diet usually loses both lean and fat tissue, especially without increased exercise. When the weight is regained, most of it is fat.
    • Fat is less metabolically active than lean muscle, so the metabolic rate drops even lower. Repeated crash dieting may be "a no-lose situation."
  • Looking ahead. Chapter 16 covers how the urinary system regulates fluid balance.

Key terms

yo-yo effect
repeated cycles of weight loss and regain
calorie-sparing defence
the drop in resting metabolic rate under severe calorie restriction, up to 45%
high-glycemic carbohydrate
one that quickly elevates blood glucose
Watch out

The mechanism behind the yo-yo effect is a three-step ratchet, and it is the most testable thing in this section. Metabolic rate falls up to 45% → the diet loses lean tissue as well as fat → what is regained is mostly fat, which is less metabolically active → resting rate ends up lower than before. Each cycle leaves you worse placed than the last.

L1.4Mechanical and chemical digestion, defined

L1.6The liver and the hepatic portal system

L1.7Bile: made in the liver, stored in the gallbladder, released by CCK

Big ideaHepatocytes make bile continuously, the gallbladder concentrates and holds it, and a hormone released when fat arrives is what squeezes it out.

L1.8Emulsification is surface area

Big ideaEnzymes only act on what they can touch, so breaking fat into many small droplets multiplies the rate of digestion without changing a single bond.
  • The enzyme only gets what it can reach. Pancreatic lipase cuts triglycerides into smaller pieces — "it's only what they can touch, what they can interact with."
  • Bile turns one big clump into ten thousand little ones, which is "a whole lot more surface area for those enzymes to work upon."
  • The result is a rate change, not a chemistry change — converting fat into something absorbable "at a much more appreciable rate."
Worked example from the text

Lecture The swimming-pool ice. A five-gallon block of ice floats in his pool "like a glacier" for hours. The same 40 lb of ice as cubes is gone in about five minutes. Same mass, same water, same pool — the cubes just have vastly more surface for the warm water to act on. That is emulsification.

L1.11Dentition, and the anatomy of a tooth

L1.12Decay, plaque and gum disease

Big ideaTrapped food feeds mouth bacteria, whose acidic waste dissolves enamel — and the sequence from there is entirely mechanical.
  • At least 100 species of bacteria live in your mouth, whatever you do. "They are a part of you. They are meant to be there."
  • They dine on trapped food and produce an acid and a gas as metabolic waste. The acid eats through enamel — that is a cavity.
  • Plaque is the layer of bacteria, mucus and food on the tooth surface. Brushed away twice a day, two minutes each time.
  • The dentist's pick test: damaged enamel is soft, so the pick sticks — "like a sword in a stone" — which confirms the cavity.
  • The depth decides the treatment. Enamel only → drill and fill with composite. Down to the pulp cavity → root canal: strip the enamel, drill out the rotted dentin, tear out the nerves and vessels, ream the canal with a wire, set a metal post for structural stability, cap it with an artificial crown.
  • Gingivitis — inflamed gums. Can progress to periodontitis, where the soft tissue around the teeth inflames, gums recede and the tooth may loosen. Some of it is reversible with lifestyle change.

L1.15The pharynx and swallowing

Big ideaOne passage serves both breathing and swallowing, so swallowing is mostly a sequence of closing the wrong doors.
  • The pharynx is shared by the respiratory and digestive systems.
  • Swallowing has both a voluntary and an involuntary component.

  • The epiglottis is a flap of fibrocartilage, one of nine cartilages of the larynx.
  • When it fails you cough. Food or drink in the airway triggers the cough reflex, forcing it back up to the throat "where you can give it another chance to go the right way — or get it out."

L1.16The esophagus, peristalsis, and the sphincter at the bottom

Big ideaThe esophagus only transports — and the one-way valve at its lower end is the thing that goes wrong.

L1.18Gastric pits: three cells, and pepsinogen becoming pepsin

Big ideaThe stomach wall is pitted with glands whose three cell types secrete mucus, acid and an inactive enzyme — and the acid is what switches the enzyme on.
  • Gastric pits (gastric glands) are tunnels in the stomach wall. Three cell types line them:
    • Mucous cells — secrete mucus, which protects the lining. "I don't want my stomach digesting itself." They sit mostly nearer the top of the pits.
    • Parietal cells — secrete hydrochloric acid, which kills bacteria and starts breaking down the connective tissue of meat.
    • Chief cells — secrete pepsinogen, the inactive form.
  • Activation: when pepsinogen meets the hydrochloric acid, it becomes pepsin.
  • Pepsin breaks peptide bonds — the covalent bonds between amino acids in a protein. He worked the class back to the name: "From chemistry, we call them peptide bonds."
  • It works indiscriminately, turning big chains of amino acids into smaller chains. "There's still big ass chains of amino acids, though."
  • Pepsin works at about pH 2.
  • Secretion is timed. Hormonal and neural mechanisms time the release of gastric juice to coincide with food arriving, so the food dilutes it before it can damage the lining.

L1.19Intrinsic factor, B12 and pernicious anemia

  • Other kinds he named: hemorrhagic anemia, and iron deficiency anemia, "the most common type."

L1.20Ulcers, and what ibuprofen is really doing

L1.22The small intestine: surface area, three times over

L1.23Neutralization in the duodenum, and pepsin denatured

Big ideaThe acid that made pepsin work has to be neutralized before it reaches the intestine — and that same neutralization destroys pepsin.

  • Protein digestion then ends in the duodenum, for the mirror-image reason.
  • The acidic chyme must be neutralized — pH 2 would dissolve the walls of the small intestine.
  • The base comes from the pancreas: sodium bicarbonate. "You probably know it as baking soda. Your body makes it."
  • Raising the pH denatures pepsin. The protein unfolds, losing its three-dimensional conformation — and "when you take away a protein's structure, what else does that protein lose? Its function. Every time. Because structure determines function."
  • So chemical digestion has to be restarted with new enzymes in the new environment.

L1.25Absorption: two routes out, and why fat needs a coat

Big ideaEverything absorbed leaves by one of two vessels, and lipids take the lymphatic route because they have to be packaged to travel in water.
  • The capillary network in each villus takes the water-soluble products — amino acids and simple sugars — and delivers them first to the liver, then out to the tissues.
  • The lacteal takes the products of fat digestion.
  • Fat has to be modified first. It is packaged into chylomicrons — protein-coated products of fat digestion — which are then soluble in water.
  • Why that works: proteins are hydrophilic, fats are hydrophobic. Coat a hydrophobic fat in a hydrophilic protein and it travels in water without trouble.
  • Where the lymph goes: lymph is mostly water; it is cleaned as it travels back to the cardiovascular system by way of the subclavian veins, where "instantly it becomes plasma once again."
  • Preview of the lymphatic chapter: plasma leaks out of capillaries and becomes interstitial fluid; capillaries are good at leaking out but not back in; the low-pressure lymphatic system reclaims it, and once inside, it is called lymph.

  • The four things, again: "I want you to know who they are, where they come from, where they work, what they work on."

L2.2Surface area, and the two cell types on a villus

Big ideaSame three-level surface-area story as Monday, with the cells of the villus named this time.
  • Plicae circulares — the circular folds of the small intestine.
  • Villi — finger-like projections built on that crinkled wall. "Another order of magnitude."
  • Goblet cells — produce mucus, keeping the tube wet and everything moving along.
  • Simple columnar epithelial cells — on their apical surface, microvilli, increasing surface area again.
  • Why it all matters: absorption needs the particle to be small enough. That is why chewing matters, why mixing with gastric juices matters, and why chemical digestion matters.
  • Enzymes are "nothing more than functional proteins."
  • Pancreatic enzymes never enter cells — they are secreted into the small intestine.

Key terms

Goblet cell
mucus-producing cell of the intestinal lining
Microvilli
the brush border on the apical surface of each columnar cell

L2.3Capillaries are exchange vessels, and fluid leaks out of them

Big ideaCapillaries are where everything is traded, and the reason fluid leaves but does not come back is pressure.
  • Capillaries are where arteries join veins, and they are exchange vessels — "that's where things get exchanged." Nutrients for waste, oxygen for CO2.
  • They leak. Fluid crosses out through slits between the endothelial cells.
  • The reason is pressure. "It's a pressure-driven system" — blood pressure inside, less pressure outside. He answered it "with one word: pressure."
  • What the capillaries absorb: the products of protein and carbohydrate digestion, plus ions and water. Not fat.

Key terms

Capillary
exchange vessel between artery and vein
Endothelial cells
the capillary lining; the slits between them let fluid out
Blood pressure
the reason flow is outward, not inward
Exam tip

Lecture "Why doesn't it go back in?" has a one-word answer and he wanted that word. Pressure. Higher inside the vessel than outside, so the leak is one-directional. The lymphatic system exists to collect what leaks.

L2.4Blood, plasma and interstitial fluid — with numbers

Big ideaThree named fluids that are mostly the same water, and the name changes with the compartment.
  • Plasma is the liquid matrix of blood — blood being a connective tissue.
  • 55% of total blood volume is plasma.
  • 92% of plasma is water. "Your blood is more water than anything."
  • 93% of what leaks out of a capillary is water.
  • Plasma that leaks out becomes interstitial fluid — fluid between the cells, providing the aqueous medium chemical reactions need.
  • Interstitial fluid moves into and out of cells freely, but does not go back into the capillary.

Key terms

Plasma
the liquid matrix of blood — 55% of blood volume, 92% water
Interstitial fluid
plasma once it has leaked out between the cells
Exam tip

Lecture Three numbers worth keeping: 55% of blood is plasma, 92% of plasma is water, 93% of what leaks out is water. He asked for the name of the leaking fluid specifically and would not accept "water."

L2.5The fat route, in full: bile salts, micelles, chylomicrons, lacteal

Big ideaFat takes a completely separate path out of the intestine, and it needs two repackaging steps to get there.
  • Bile salts are the emulsifying agents in bile — they turn big clumps of fat into many small ones, increasing surface area for lipases.
  • Then micelles form. "I'm also going to have to form these things called micelles."
  • Micelles are turned into chylomicrons, which can be absorbed across the cell, through the underlying basement membrane.
  • They reach the lacteal — "the blind-ended lymphatic capillary in the very center of every villus."
  • Chylomicrons are protein-coated products of fat digestion, and that coat is what makes them soluble in water.

Key terms

Bile salt
the emulsifying agent within bile
Micelle
the intermediate package of digested fat, formed before the chylomicron
Chylomicron
protein-coated, water-soluble package of digested fat
Basement membrane
the layer beneath the epithelium the chylomicron crosses
Lacteal
blind-ended lymphatic capillary at the centre of a villus
Exam tip

Lecture The micelle step is new since Monday. Monday's version went fat → chylomicron → lacteal. The full sequence he gave today is emulsify with bile salts → lipase digests → micelles form → micelles become chylomicrons → across the cell and basement membrane → lacteal.

L2.6Why a protein coat makes fat travel in water

Big ideaOil and water do not mix until you change one of them chemically — and coating fat in protein is that change.
  • Oil and water separate no matter how hard you mix them — "until you change one of them chemically."
  • Proteins are hydrophilic. Fats are hydrophobic. Coat the hydrophobic thing in the hydrophilic thing and it travels in water.
  • Not all amino acids are hydrophilic — he flagged the exception and parked it: acidic, basic, hydrophilic, hydrophobic, polar and non-polar amino acids are a biochemistry topic.
  • It closes on the same law as Monday: amino acid chemistry → secondary structure → tertiary structure → the three-dimensional conformation → function. "Structure determines function."
Exam tip

Lecture He made this the argument for understanding rather than memorising. "You've just been trained to do a test without truly understanding why you're even doing it… That's the difference between a worker and a boss."

L2.7Salivary amylase, and what it actually produces

Big ideaSaliva is mostly water carrying one enzyme, and that enzyme's product is a disaccharide — which is why starch starts tasting sweet.
  • Saliva is mainly water, carrying salivary amylase.
  • Salivary amylase breaks starch down into disaccharides, like maltose.
  • That product is what your taste buds register as sweet.

Key terms

Maltose
the disaccharide produced from starch by salivary amylase
Exam tip

Lecture Salivary amylase does not produce glucose. It stops at disaccharides — maltose is the one he named. Brush-border enzymes finish the job in the small intestine.

L2.8The pancreas: 99% exocrine, and its four secretions

Big ideaAlmost the whole organ is a digestive gland, and it supplies one base and three classes of enzyme.
  • 99% of pancreatic mass is exocrine cells, secreting into ducts.
  • Bicarbonate — to neutralize the chyme released by the stomach through the pyloric sphincter into the duodenum.
  • Pancreatic amylase — carbohydrates.
  • Proteases — trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase — proteins.
  • Lipases — fats. He worked the room for the word and then made a point of it.
  • Nucleases also appear in the list he gave of what is added at the duodenum.
Exam tip

Lecture He turned the lipase question into a lesson about confidence. "There's a really good chance you know the answer, you're just afraid of looking wrong in front of your peers… As an instructor, I dream for the day where I hear 60 incorrect answers instead of crickets."

L2.9The plumbing, named in full

Big ideaThe bile and pancreatic ducts merge before they enter the duodenum, and every junction on that route has a name.
  • Bile is made by hepatocytes, emptied into bile canaliculi → bile collecting ducts → out of the liver via the left and right hepatic ducts → "a turn in the road", the cystic duct → the gallbladder, for storage and concentration.
  • CCK — cholecystokinin — is the chemical message that makes the gallbladder contract when fat arrives.
  • On release: bile goes through the cystic duct → down the common bile duct → meets the main pancreatic duct at the hepatopancreatic ampulla → through the sphincter of Oddi → into the duodenum at the major duodenal papilla.
  • Papilla means bump. "Major duodenal papilla — the big ass bump in your duodenum."
  • There is a minor duodenal papilla beside it, with its own sphincter, fed by an accessory duct also from the pancreas.

Key terms

Hepatopancreatic ampulla

Sphincter of Oddi
the valve at that junction
Major duodenal papilla
the bump in the duodenum where the shared duct empties
Minor duodenal papilla
the second, smaller opening, fed by the accessory pancreatic duct
Papilla

L2.10Neutralization, and what it does to pepsin's activity

Big ideaRaising the pH of the chyme is necessary to protect the intestinal wall, and it shuts pepsin down as a side effect.
  • Chyme leaves the stomach through the pyloric sphincter, acidic.
  • Sodium bicarbonate from the pancreas neutralizes it, raising the pH.
  • Pepsin works at roughly pH 2 — "acidic AF is your stomach contents."
  • The question he asked: what happens to pepsin activity when the pH goes from 2 to about 6.5? Activity goes down. He framed it as reading a graph.
Watch out

Lecture He gave the number 6.5 for the duodenum and asked the class to reason across a pH-versus-activity curve from 2 to 6.5. That is a graph question waiting to happen — the answer is that activity falls, and the reason is denaturation, not dilution.

L2.11Denaturation: which levels of structure are lost

Big ideaDenaturation destroys the folding but not the chain — and that distinction is the whole answer.
  • Enzymes are just proteins. Outside their normal environment they "kind of just fall apart."
  • Too much temperature change or pH change makes a protein unfold.
  • What survives: the primary structure — "it stays together in a long ass chain of amino acids."
  • What is lost: secondary structure gone. Tertiary structure gone. Function gone.

Key terms

Primary structure
the amino acid sequence — survives denaturation
Secondary / tertiary structure
the folding — destroyed by denaturation
Denaturation
loss of folding, and therefore of function
Watch out

Lecture This is the precise version of Monday's point, and the precision is the exam answer. Denatured pepsin is not chopped up — the peptide chain is intact. Primary structure stays; secondary and tertiary go; function goes with them.

L2.12The three pancreatic proteases, and how two get switched on

Big ideaTrypsin is the one that arrives active, and it activates the other two.
  • Three, by name: trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase. "There were three of them I wanted you to know."
  • Carboxypeptidase is the one that yields individual amino acids, which are small enough to absorb easily across the intestinal epithelium into the bloodstream.
  • Trypsin activates the other two. It converts chymotrypsinogen → chymotrypsin, and procarboxypeptidase → carboxypeptidase.
  • Why the whole class matters: the stomach's protease has been denatured, so a new protease that works at neutral pH has to be added — from the pancreas.

Key terms

Trypsin
pancreatic protease; also the activator of the other two
Chymotrypsinogen
inactive precursor of chymotrypsin
Procarboxypeptidase
inactive precursor of carboxypeptidase
Exam tip

Lecture Same trick as pepsinogen, one level up. Proteases are secreted inactive so they do not digest the organ that made them — and here the activator is another enzyme rather than acid.

L2.13The liver's jobs, and why everything you swallow goes there first

Big ideaWhatever enters your body reaches the liver before anywhere else, and the liver either processes it or keeps it.
  • Routes in: the mouth, the skin (transdermal), and the non-keratinized stratified squamous lining of the mouth — sublingual absorption, also into the blood.
  • All of it goes to the liver first. "Be careful what you put in your body, because your liver is going to have to deal with it."
  • If the liver cannot process something it stores it, and it accumulates.
  • The jobs he listed: produce bile · regulate blood glucose · detoxify and remove poisonous substances ("my kidneys are gonna do that too — both are gonna take a hit from it").

L2.14Glycogen, and what happens when the stores are full

Big ideaExcess glucose is stored as glycogen in two places, and once those are full it is converted to fat.
  • Short-term storage is glycogen, in the liver and in skeletal muscle.
  • The difference between the two sites: muscle glycogen is "where you're going to use it, like, now"; liver glycogen is easily mobilized for the body.
  • Glycogen storage is very limited.
  • When the stores are full: glucose is converted to glycerol, and three free fatty acids are attached to it, forming a triglyceride.
  • That triglyceride goes to fat storage cells.

Key terms

Glycogen
short-term glucose storage, in liver and skeletal muscle
Glycerol
the three-carbon backbone of a triglyceride
Triglyceride
glycerol plus three fatty acids

L2.15Adipocytes and the caloric deficit

Big ideaFat cells are bags that grow and shrink with their contents, and the only way to empty one is to spend more than you take in.
  • Adipose is the tissue. The cell is the adipocyte. He walked the class from "cell" to "-cyte" to get there.
  • The adipocyte holds a bag of triglycerides, like a laundry bag. The more you put in, the bigger the bag — and the bigger the adipocyte.
  • It is not one-way. Put out more energy than you take in as calories and you create a caloric deficit, at which point "you have no choice but to do some laundry" — the stored fat is broken down and used.

Key terms

Adipose
the tissue
Adipocyte

L2.16Alcohol, hepatitis, cirrhosis and jaundice

Big ideaThe liver detoxifies ethanol, inflames under sustained load, scars, and once it scars it does not come back.
  • Ethanol — ethyl alcohol — is the alcohol you drink, and the only one. "That shit's poison."
  • The liver detoxifies it, and under sustained load becomes inflamed — hepatitis.
  • Sustained inflammation ends in scarring — cirrhosis. He defined it as fat, followed by scar tissue, accumulating in the liver.
  • Scar tissue does not replace itself, so function is permanently reduced.
  • Jaundice is the visible sign. The failing liver stops filtering bilirubin (he also named biliverdin), which accumulates in the skin and the sclera — the whites of the eyes — turning them yellow to orange.
  • At that point the only remedy is a liver transplant, and he was blunt about the allocation question.
  • Hepatitis is a condition, not only a virus. Asked directly whether you can have hepatitis without a virus, he said yes — A, B and C are viruses that lead to the condition, but other things cause the inflammation too.

Key terms

Hepatitis
inflammation of the liver — viral or otherwise
Cirrhosis
fat then scar tissue accumulating in the liver
Bilirubin / biliverdin
pigments the failing liver stops clearing
Jaundice
yellowing of skin and sclera from accumulated bilirubin
Sclera
the white of the eye

L2.17The hepatic portal system and the liver lobule

Big ideaA portal system is two capillary beds in series, and the liver lobule is the hexagonal unit where the second one does its work.
  • A portal system brings together two capillary beds. Here: the bed in the villi of the small intestine, and a second bed in the liver.
  • The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the GI tract and abdominal organs and carries it — nutrient-rich, oxygen-poor — to the liver.
  • The liver also gets a normal arterial supply: the hepatic artery, bringing oxygen-rich blood to sustain the hepatocytes.
  • Liver lobules are hexagonal. At every point of the hexagon sits a triad: a bile collecting duct, a hepatic artery branch, and a branch of the hepatic portal vein.
  • At the centre of the hexagon is the central vein.
  • Between point and centre are the sinusoids — "the leakiest of all capillaries", a very incomplete epithelium letting large things pass both ways between blood and hepatocytes.
  • Out: central veins → left and right hepatic veins → the inferior vena cava → the heart.

Key terms

Portal system
two capillary beds in series
Hepatic portal vein
carries nutrient-rich, oxygen-poor blood from the gut to the liver
Hepatic artery
the liver's oxygen supply
Liver lobule
the hexagonal functional unit
Portal triad
bile duct + hepatic artery + portal vein branch, at each corner
Central vein
at the centre of the lobule
Sinusoid
the lobule's very leaky capillary
Exam tip

Lecture Two blood supplies, and they mix. The portal vein brings nutrients with no oxygen; the hepatic artery brings oxygen. Both drain through the sinusoids to the same central vein. A question asking which vessel brings nutrients has a different answer from one asking which brings oxygen.

L2.18Gallstones, and life without a gallbladder

Big ideaRemoving the gallbladder does not stop bile — it stops storage, and that changes what you can eat.
  • Gallstones are solid particles of cholesterol and other substances built up around smaller particles, caught in the gallbladder. They can grow too large to leave the pear-shaped sac, and rupture is a real problem.
  • The fix is removal. "Cut the cystic duct and tie it off."
  • What is left afterwards: the hepatic ducts still carry bile out of the liver, and the common bile duct still runs to the duodenum. What is gone is the place to store, modify and concentrate it.
  • So bile is released continuously, at a low constant trickle — "you will not have a huge flush of bile to emulsify the fat."
  • The consequence of a high-fat meal afterwards: not enough surface area for lipase, so lipid passes undigested and unabsorbed, causing GI disturbance and greasy stool.
  • You have until the end of the small intestine to absorb it.
Exam tip

Lecture This is the cleanest test of whether you understand emulsification. No gallbladder → no concentrated flush → not enough surface area → lipase cannot keep up → fat leaves in the stool. Every step is the mechanism, not a fact to memorise.

L2.19The large intestine: what it actually absorbs

Big ideaThe colon's job is water recovery, and almost nothing else.
  • The colon gets everything that was not absorbed in the small intestine.
  • It pulls out the water — "damn near all of the water."
  • What is absorbed there: water, ions dissolved in that water, and "maybe some fat-soluble vitamins."
  • What is left is indigestible food residue, stored briefly and pushed out as fecal material.
Watch out

Lecture He said "the only thing you're absorbing in the large intestine is water" and then immediately widened it to ions and possibly fat-soluble vitamins. Water is the headline answer; ions and fat-soluble vitamins are the qualifier. He did not mention microbially produced vitamins here, though the bacteria come up later in the lecture.

L2.20The ileocecal valve, the cecum and the appendix

Big ideaA one-way valve keeps colon contents out of the small intestine, and the pouch just past it carries an organ nobody can assign a job to.
  • The ileocecal valve is named for what it joins: the ileum (the third and last part of the small intestine) and the cecum (the first part of the large intestine).
  • It is a one-way valve, and the direction matters: "things in the large intestine have no business being in the small intestine. They can actually do a lot of damage there."
  • When it fails, contents move back up — he drew the parallel to a failing lower esophageal sphincter and reflux. The result is serious GI disturbance and infections that can become life-threatening.
  • The cecum is a blind pouch, the very beginning of the large intestine.
  • The appendix hangs off the cecum, about the size of a little finger. A vestigial organ — "no longer serves a purpose in our species as it exists today" — though it may serve an immune function.

Key terms

Ileocecal valve
one-way valve from ileum into cecum
Cecum
the blind pouch beginning the large intestine
Vestigial organ
one that no longer serves its original purpose

L2.21Appendicitis, rupture and sepsis

Big ideaAn inflamed appendix is a time-limited emergency, because what it would spill has no way out.
  • Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix. Cause: blockage — by stool, food or a tumour — allowing bacteria to multiply.
  • Treatment is antibiotics, urgently. "You need antibiotics like yesterday."
  • If it is not controlled within 48–72 hours, it comes out — an appendectomy, through the right side.
  • Why the deadline exists: if it ruptures, the contents of the large intestine reach the abdominal cavity, "a place that has no out."
  • If it reaches a blood vessel: sepsis — system-wide infection — and septic shock. That is where the life-threatening part is.

Key terms

Appendicitis

Appendectomy

Sepsis

Septic shock

L2.22The colon: four segments and the haustra

Big ideaUp, across, down and an S — and the pouches along it exist to buy surface area for water recovery.
  • Ascending colon — up from the cecum, on the right.
  • Transverse colon — across the abdominal cavity, right to left.
  • Descending colon — down the left.
  • Sigmoid colon — the S-shape into the rectum.
  • The two bends: the hepatic flexure and the splenic flexure. "You may never need to know that."
  • Haustra — he spelled it, H-A-U-S-T-R-A — the out-pouchings and puckerings along the tube. "Like tiny little rooms, joined to each other with no true sphincters in between them."
  • Their purpose is surface area, to pull out as much water as possible.

L2.23Defecation: two sphincters, two muscle types

Big ideaOne sphincter you control and one you do not, and the difference is entirely the muscle tissue.
  • Peristaltic contractions move material through the large intestine; feces pushes into the rectum, initiating the defecation reflex.
  • Pressure builds in the rectum until the internal anal sphincter relaxes. "That shit's gonna happen whether you want it to or not."
  • The internal anal sphincter is smooth muscle — involuntary.
  • The external anal sphincter is skeletal muscle — voluntary. That is the one you clamp down on.
  • Skeletal muscle is next week's chapter, and he flagged that the reason will make sense then.

Key terms

triggered by rectal pressure

smooth muscle, involuntary
External anal sphincter

L2.24The gut microbiome, and lactose intolerance

Big ideaYour gut bacteria carry genes you do not, and lactose intolerance is what happens when you rely on theirs instead of your own.
  • You have more bacterial cells in your gut than cells in your body — and the reason it is possible is size: a bacterial cell is a tiny fraction of a human cell.
  • "Gut" means the intestines, small and large — not the stomach. He was explicit.
  • Most are harmless, many are beneficial, because their genomes code for enzymes to break down things your genome cannot.
  • Lactase — beta-galactosidase — cuts lactose into glucose and galactose, both small enough to absorb across the small intestine wall.
  • Without the gene you make no lactase. Lactose passes the small intestine unabsorbed into the large intestine.
  • There, bacteria that do produce lactase cut it — but you absorb none of it. They use it themselves.
  • Their metabolic waste is an acid and a gas. The gas builds up; the acid disturbs the colon and reduces how much water is reabsorbed. Result: gas and diarrhea.

Key terms

Lactase (beta-galactosidase)
cuts lactose into glucose and galactose
Lactose
the disaccharide in milk
Galactose
the other monosaccharide lactose yields
Exam tip

Lecture Lactose intolerance is a location problem, not a digestion problem. The lactose does get cut — just in the wrong organ, by the wrong cells, where you cannot absorb the products. The symptoms are entirely the bacteria's waste.

Worked example from the text

Lecture "One third of every turd you've ever made is nothing you ate, but bacteria that call your colon home." He called it one of the things he remembers best from his own undergraduate biology.

L2.25C. difficile and the fecal transplant

Big ideaWhen an infection wins by out-competing your normal flora, the treatment is to reinstall the flora.
  • Irritable bowel syndrome has a number of different causes, one of which is infection by something that should not be there.
  • Clostridium difficile — "C. diff" — is among the most common such infections in the US and the UK. Daily diarrhea until it is fixed.
  • It often does not respond to everyday antibiotics.
  • The treatment is a fecal transplant — donor stool, cleaned and liquefied, instilled high into the rectum.
  • The logic: C. diff wins by out-competing the natural flora, so the fix is to re-establish a healthy gut bacterial population.
  • Donors are screened hard — "it's easier to get invited to attend Harvard than it is to become a fecal donor."

Key terms

Clostridium difficile (C. diff)
a common infectious cause of persistent diarrhea
Fecal transplant
re-establishing gut flora from a screened donor

L2.26Colon disorders: diarrhea, constipation, diverticulitis, polyps

Big ideaMost colon trouble is a failure of water handling or a pouch that traps something.
  • Diarrhea is failure to remove enough water, usually because the gut bacteria are disturbed.
  • Escherichia coli belongs in your colon — a fecal coliform bacterium. Yours is not the same as someone else's; theirs in you is the problem. That is what handwashing is about.
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics cause diarrhea by killing the healthy bacteria along with the target. "It literally says on every antibiotic you take: may cause gastrointestinal disturbances."
  • Opioid painkillers cause constipation — a side effect of all of them. Managed with laxatives, suppositories or a bulk agent.
  • Diverticula are out-pouchings in the haustra; they can trap food — he named nuts — and become inflamed or infected: diverticulitis.
  • Polyps are hypertrophic masses in the colon and can turn cancerous. They can be removed. A ruptured polyp bleeds — visible as bright red blood on the toilet paper.

Key terms

Diverticula
out-pouchings of the colon wall
Diverticulitis
inflammation or infection of those pouches
Polyp
a mass in the colon lining that can become cancerous
Fecal coliform
E. coli — normal colon flora

L2.27Colorectal cancer, and why the screening exists

Big ideaThe colon has no pain receptors for this, so the only early sign is blood — and catching it early is the whole game.
  • Colonoscopy — a camera the width of a little finger, under conscious sedation, after a full bowel prep.
  • How far it goes names the procedure: the rectal wall and sigmoid colon is a sigmoidoscopy; up to the splenic flexure, across the transverse, or down the ascending is a full colonoscopy.
  • You have no pain receptors there. "You're not gonna feel like anything's wrong. The only sign you're going to have is probably red on your tissue."
  • The non-invasive screen is a stool test kit, done at home and mailed in. He does one every year because he is over 40.
  • Caught early the outcome is good — "there's a 90-something percent chance you're gonna survive." Waiting is what kills.
  • His instruction: "Don't keep it to yourself. Bring this shit up to a doctor, like, now."

Key terms

Colonoscopy
full examination of the colon
Sigmoidoscopy
examination limited to the rectum and sigmoid colon
Colorectal cancer
often symptomless early; blood in the stool is the first sign
