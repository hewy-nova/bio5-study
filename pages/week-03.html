  • Reading (21.3). DNA → RNA → protein. Transcription in the nucleus, translation at the ribosome.

21.1Form of DNA

pp. 437
Big ideaDNA is a twisted ladder whose rungs obey one non-negotiable rule — A with T, C with G — so either strand contains enough information to rebuild the other.

Outline

  • What the molecule is. Deoxyribonucleic acid: two strands, ladder-shaped, twisted into a double helix. It is a nucleic acid.
    • About 50-trillionths of an inch across; ~5 feet of DNA per cell if laid end to end.
    • Every organism from bacteria to humans uses the same subunits — only the order differs.
  • The repeating subunit. A nucleotide = sugar + phosphate + nitrogenous base.
    • The sugar in DNA is deoxyribose.
    • Four bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), thymine (T), cytosine (C).
  • How the ladder is built.
    • Sides are alternating sugars and phosphates.
    • Rungs are paired bases held by weak hydrogen bonds.
    • Pairing is specific because of base shape and the number of hydrogen bonds that can form.
  • Consequence. The strands are complementary, so one strand's sequence dictates the other's — which is exactly what makes replication possible.
  • Scale. ~3 billion base pairs per human cell. The pairing rule never varies; the sequence varies enormously, and that sequence is the genetic information.

Because A always pairs with T and C with G, the amount of adenine equals the amount of thymine, and guanine equals cytosine. That is end-of-chapter question 2, worded backwards.

21.2Replication of DNA

pp. 438
Big ideaReplication is semiconservative — unzip the helix, let each old strand act as a template, and each daughter molecule ends up half old and half new.

The steps

  • Why it has to happen. Genetic instructions must pass to the next generation and be present in every cell, so DNA is copied before both mitotic and meiotic division. Complementary base pairing is what guarantees the copies are exact.
  • Unzip. An enzyme breaks the weak hydrogen bonds holding base pairs together; the strands separate and unwind.
  • Expose. Bases on each separated strand are temporarily exposed, so each can serve as a template.
  • Pair. Free nucleotides, always present in the nucleus, attach to their complementary bases on the open strands.
  • Link. DNA polymerases join the sugars and phosphates of the newly attached nucleotides into a continuous new strand.
  • Twist. Each new double-stranded molecule re-forms a double helix.

Key terms

DNA replication
the copying of a DNA molecule before cell division
Semiconservative replication
each new molecule keeps one parental strand and gains one new one
Template
an exposed old strand whose base order dictates the new strand's order
DNA polymerase
enzyme that links the sugars and phosphates of newly attached nucleotides

which enzyme unzips the helix; the section text says only that "an enzyme" breaks the hydrogen bonds and that DNA polymerases link the new nucleotides, while the chapter summary compresses this and credits DNA polymerase with the unzipping too. If a question asks which enzyme builds the new strand, the answer is DNA polymerase either way.

Outline

  • The central chain. DNA → RNA → protein. The base sequence in DNA sets the base sequence in RNA, which sets the amino acid sequence in the protein. That protein is the molecular basis of an inherited trait.
  • RNA differs from DNA. Sugar is ribose not deoxyribose; uracil (U) replaces thymine; mostly single-stranded.
  • The three RNAs.
    • mRNA carries DNA's instructions from nucleus to cytoplasm; its base order specifies the protein's amino acid order.
    • tRNA ferries a specific amino acid to the growing polypeptide and carries an anticodon.
    • rRNA combines with protein to form the ribosomes.
  • Transcription — making RNA from DNA.
    • An enzyme unwinds and unzips the region to be copied. One unwound strand serves as the template.
    • RNA nucleotides pair with their complements: C with G, and U with A.
    • A promoter is the start signal. RNA polymerase binds it, opens the helix ahead of itself, aligns and links RNA nucleotides. The DNA zips back up behind it.
    • Another sequence signals the stop; the finished RNA transcript is released.
    • Processing. Introns (intervening sequences) are snipped out; the remaining exons (expressed sequences) are spliced together before the mRNA leaves the nucleus.
  • The genetic code.
    • A codon is three bases on mRNA, specifying one amino acid, a start, or a stop.
    • Four bases in three-base words gives 64 codons for only 20 amino acids, so several codons can specify the same amino acid.
    • AUG is the start signal; mid-message it codes methionine.
    • UAA, UAG, UGA are stop codons, coding no amino acid.
  • tRNA and the ribosome.
    • The anticodon is tRNA's three-base sequence that binds the mRNA codon. Anticodon AAG carries phenylalanine to codon UUC.
    • A ribosome has a small and large subunit, each rRNA plus protein, joining only during synthesis.
    • Joined, they form a groove for mRNA, two tRNA binding sites, and an enzyme that makes the peptide bond.
  • Translation in three stages.
    • Initiation. The small subunit attaches at the start codon AUG; a matching tRNA pairs; the large subunit joins.
    • Elongation. Codon recognition, then peptide bond formation, then ribosome movement — repeating.
    • Termination. A stop codon enters; no anticodon pairs with it, so synthesis ends and everything separates.
    • Polysome. Many ribosomes read the same mRNA at once, each making its own copy.

21.4Mutations

pp. 444
Big ideaA gene mutation is a change in the order of nucleotides. Swapping one base may or may not matter; adding or removing one shifts every codon after it and almost always does.
  • DNA is remarkably stable and replication, transcription and translation are usually precise — but alterations happen, and they change the message.
  • Two scales of mutation.
    • Chromosomal — whole sections duplicated or deleted.
    • Gene mutation — a change in the order of nucleotides within a gene.
  • Which mutations are inherited. A gene mutation is passed to offspring only if it is in a cell that becomes an egg or sperm. A mutation in a body cell affects that cell and its descendants but is not transmitted to children.
  • Base-pair substitution. One nucleotide pair replaces another.
    • During replication a base may pair incorrectly.
    • Repair enzymes normally swap the wrong base out — but sometimes replace the original base on the old strand instead, locking in a wrong pair.
    • May change one amino acid, and may or may not alter how the protein works.
  • Insertion or deletion. One or more nucleotides added or removed.
    • Generally more serious, because mRNA is read in units of three.
    • Adding or deleting one or two nucleotides shifts every codon downstream, so the protein changes drastically.

Key terms

Mutation
any change in DNA
Gene mutation
a change in the order of nucleotides within a gene
Base-pair substitution
one nucleotide pair replaced by a different one
Insertion / deletion
nucleotides added or removed, shifting all following codons
Exam tip

Test yourself with the reading frame, not memorised labels. Write THE BIG FAT DOG RAN, delete the B, re-split into threes: THE IGF ATD OGR AN. That is a frameshift, and it is why insertions and deletions outrank substitutions in severity.

Exam tip

Lecture His closing checklist asks you to rank point mutations by their impact on protein synthesis — more than either the reading or the lab drills. Be ready to say why a frameshift usually damages a protein more than a single substitution does.

21.5Epigenetics: Regulating Gene Activity

pp. 445
Big ideaEvery nucleated cell except gametes carries the same complete instruction set. Liver cells differ from nerve cells because different genes are switched on, not because they hold different genes.
  • The puzzle. From one zygote, faithful replication puts identical instructions in every nucleated body cell. So why do liver, bone, blood, muscle and nerve cells differ? Because only certain genes are active in a given cell type; most are turned off. Timing matters too.
  • Level 1 — chromosome packing. Tightly coiled DNA is not expressed. When a protein is needed, that region unwinds so transcription enzymes can reach it.
  • Level 2 — regulatory DNA and proteins.
    • Promoter — a DNA sequence next to the gene it regulates. Transcription factors bind it, letting RNA polymerase bind and begin.
    • Enhancers — segments that increase transcription rate, and therefore how much protein is made. They also set timing and responsiveness to external signals.
  • Level 3 — hormones. Steroid hormones bind receptors inside a target cell; the hormone–receptor complex travels to the chromatin and turns on specific genes. Testosterone switches on hair-producing genes in facial follicle cells; women have the genes and receptors but usually not enough hormone.

Key terms

Transcription factor
a regulatory protein that binds a promoter so RNA polymerase can start
Enhancer
a DNA segment that raises transcription rate and helps set timing
Epigenetics
stable changes in gene expression without any change to the DNA sequence
Exam tip

The two named epigenetic mechanisms — DNA methylation (compacts DNA, turns genes off) and histone acetylation (loosens coiling, makes expression easier) — appear in the Environmental Issue essay, not in the main 21.5 text. Still fair game on an exam.

21.6Genetic Engineering

pp. 446–451
Big ideaCut the gene you want and its new home with the same restriction enzyme so their sticky ends match, glue them with ligase, ride a vector into a fast-dividing host cell, then amplify.
  • Making recombinant DNA — five steps.
    • 1. Cut. Source DNA and vector DNA are cut with the same restriction enzyme. Each enzyme recognises a specific sequence and makes a staggered cut, leaving a sticky end.
    • 2. Paste. Sticky ends cut by the same enzyme are complementary and stick together. DNA ligase seals the bond permanently. The result is recombinant DNA.
    • 3. Transfer. A vector carries recombinant DNA into a host cell — commonly a bacterial plasmid, a small circular self-replicating piece of DNA separate from the chromosome.
    • 4. Identify. Each recombinant plasmid goes into one bacterium, which grows into a colony. The colony carrying the gene of interest must be found and isolated.
    • 5. Amplify. By bacterial cloning or by PCR.
  • Two ways to amplify.
    • Cloning — grow the bacteria in huge numbers; each divides into a clone, all carrying the same recombinant DNA. Plasmids can later be moved into plant or animal cells to make transgenic organisms.
    • PCR — heat to separate strands, add primers complementary to each strand, add nucleotides and a heat-resistant DNA polymerase, cool to allow pairing. Each round doubles the copies.
  • Two general uses.
    • Mass-produce a gene product — human growth hormone from engineered bacteria.
    • Move a useful trait between species — salmon given an eel-like fish's gene grow faster.
  • Applications by field.
    • Environmental. Microbes that cut phosphate and nitrate discharge; salt- and cold-tolerant oil-eating microbes for marine spills.
    • Livestock. Engineered vaccines; bovine somatotropin (BST) boosts milk production by nearly 25%.
    • Pharmaceuticals. Engineered vaccines use only a pathogen's surface protein, so they cannot infect. Plantibodies — soybeans carrying human anti-herpes antibodies, corn making a tumour-binding antibody.
    • Gene pharming. Transgenic animals secrete a medicinal protein in milk, eggs or blood. AAT from sheep for inherited emphysema; ATryn, the first drug from a transgenic goat's milk.
    • Agriculture. Most common engineered traits are pest resistance and herbicide resistance. Golden rice makes beta-carotene for populations where vitamin A deficiency blinds 500,000 children a year.
  • Gene therapy.
    • Goal. Put normal functional genes into body cells affected by a mutant gene.
    • Delivery by virus. Splice a healthy gene into a harmless virus; each virus type targets one cell type. Typical route: isolate a bone-marrow stem cell, infect it, return it to the patient.
    • Retroviruses store information as RNA, rewrite it as DNA inside the target cell and insert it into a chromosome.
    • Results so far. Over 4,000 human diseases trace to single-gene defects; the FDA has approved no gene therapy yet, though hundreds of trials run.
    • SCID, the first trial. A mutant gene blocks adenosine deaminase (ADA), so white blood cells die before maturing. In 1990 Ashanthi DeSilva's cells were engineered to carry ADA. White blood cells live only weeks, so treatment must be repeated.

21.7Genomics

pp. 452–455
Big ideaOnce you can sequence a whole genome, the interesting questions shift — which genes are switched on, how do people differ in that 0.1%, and what do we share with other species?
  • Definitions to get straight first.
    • Genome — the entire set of genes carried by one member of a species.
    • Genomics — the study of entire genomes and how genes interact with each other and the environment.
    • Epigenome — the chemical tags that bind specific genes and turn them on or off.
  • Human Genome Project.
    • Worldwide effort completed in 2003, sequencing ~3 billion base pairs across all 23 pairs.
    • Gene count came in at 20,000–25,000, not the 100,000 expected — gene families share genes, and many genes code for parts of more than one protein.
    • Genes for more than 1,400 genetic diseases mapped; roughly 4,000 human diseases have a known genetic basis.
    • Humans are identical across 99.9% of gene sequences. The remaining 0.1% is where the interesting questions live.
  • The epigenome.
    • The book's analogy: the genome is the hardware, the epigenome is the software.
    • Genes are silenced when their DNA wraps tightly around histones; uncoiled regions can be expressed.
    • The genome is relatively stable; the epigenome is flexible, varying across life, cells, tissues and people, and responding to diet and stress.
  • Microarray analysis.
    • More than 95% of human DNA does not code for protein, but some noncoding sequences are regulatory.
    • A microarray is thousands of DNA sequences stamped on a glass slide — a DNA chip — revealing which genes are active under different conditions.
    • Classic use: find genes active in cancerous but not healthy cells.
    • SNPs (single-nucleotide polymorphisms) differ by one nucleotide between people. SNPs near each other are inherited together as a haplotype; the International HapMap Project compares haplotype frequencies to find disease-associated genes.
  • Comparing genomes across species. We share about 50% of our genes with the fruit fly and 90% with the mouse. The closer the evolutionary relationship, the more DNA in common.

Key terms

Don't mix these up. Genome = the full gene set. Genomics = the field that studies it. Microarray = the tool that shows which genes are active. Questions 8 and 9 are built entirely out of swapping those three definitions, and share the same four answer choices — read both stems before committing.

Stop And Think

1The last codon on the transcript in Figure 21.3 is GUA. Which amino acid? (p. 441)

Valine. All four GU_ codons specify valine — a good illustration of why 64 codons are enough for 20 amino acids.

2Streptomycin binds bacterial ribosomes and prevents accurate reading of mRNA. Why does that slow bacterial growth? (p. 443)

If mRNA cannot be read accurately the bacterium cannot build correct proteins, including the enzymes and structural proteins it needs to grow and divide. Growth slows, buying the body's defences time.

3Why do female athletes who inject testosterone sometimes grow more facial hair? (p. 445)

They already have the hair-producing genes and the testosterone receptors in their facial follicle cells; what they normally lack is enough hormone. Injecting it supplies the missing piece, and the hormone–receptor complex switches those genes on.

1If the bases after a start signal were AACUCAGCC, which amino acids are specified? (Figure 21.5, p. 442)

Split into codons first: AAC · UCA · GCC gives asparagine – serine – alanine. Splitting into threes before looking anything up is the whole trick.

Boxed essays

Health Issue

Genetically Modified Food (p. 448)

  • Over 70% of US processed foods contain GM ingredients; people have eaten them since the mid-1990s.
  • A National Academy of Sciences panel found GM crops pose no risks conventional breeding cannot also cause, while recommending pre-market scrutiny. Regulated by USDA, FDA and EPA.
  • Allergens — a protein may be modified differently in a GM plant and become an allergen. Known allergens are small, protein, and resist heat, acid and digestion.
  • Antibiotic resistance — marker resistance genes could in principle move to gut bacteria. Being phased out for alternatives like green fluorescent protein.
  • Environment — pest-resistant cotton cut pesticide use, corn largely didn't; herbicide use is killing the milkweed monarchs depend on; GM canola in North Dakota has already become doubly herbicide-resistant "superweeds".
  • Social — proponents cite hunger relief; critics say hunger is a distribution problem, and GM seeds don't self-seed so poor farmers must rebuy them yearly.
Environmental Issue

Environment and Epigenetics (p. 450)

  • Epigenetics is stable alteration in gene expression without changes to the DNA sequence.
  • DNA methylation adds a methyl group, recruits proteins that compact DNA, and turns a gene off.
  • Histone acetylation loosens the coiling and makes expression easier.
  • Methylation patterns shift over time, respond to diet and chemicals, can cause disease, and can pass through generations.
  • Maternal nutrition during pregnancy can alter fetal gene activity, affecting later susceptibility to obesity, type 2 diabetes, heart disease and cancer.
  • Linked to autism spectrum disorders, Rett syndrome and Fragile X; altered patterns appear in cervical, prostate, breast, stomach and colon cancers.
Ethical Issue

Forensic Science, DNA, and Personal Privacy (p. 454)

  • DNA fingerprinting identifies individuals from unique features of their DNA.
  • It works because of short tandem repeats (STRs) — repeated units of one to five bases whose repeat number varies between people.
  • Method: extract DNA from any tissue, amplify the repeat regions with PCR, then count repeats.
  • The FBI uses 13 STRs as its core forensic set.
  • It is easier to prove two fingerprints don't match than that they do. Over 200 US convicts have been exonerated by DNA testing in a decade.
  • Privacy — about 30 states collect DNA from people accused of misdemeanours, and samples often stay in a national database even after acquittal.

"They are necessary for translation"

14Why is deleting one nucleotide usually worse than substituting one?

Because mRNA is read in three-base codons

A substitution changes at most one codon, and because several codons specify the same amino acid the protein may not change at all. A deletion shifts the reading frame, so every codon downstream is misread.

15Every cell in your body has the same genes. Why doesn't a nerve cell look like a liver cell?

Because only certain genes are active in each cell type

Cut, paste, transfer, identify, amplify, harvest

Did you know

  • Nearly 15 million cancer survivors live in the United States, projected to reach 19 million by 2024.
  • There are more than 200 types of cancer.
  • One of every three people in the US will develop cancer at some point.

The chapter as one chain

  • Uncontrolled division (21a.1). The trait every cancer shares — but it is metastasis that kills, not the original tumour.
  • Each safeguard has a specific defeat (21a.2). Checkpoints, apoptosis, division limits, blood-vessel restriction, adhesion. p53 is implicated in four of them.
  • So it takes many hits (21a.3). At least two genes, usually more than six, accumulated over years. That is why inheritance gives predisposition, not disease.
  • Sequence change isn't the only route (21a.4). The epigenome can silence a tumour suppressor without touching a nucleotide — and it is reversible, so it is also a drug target.
  • Not every tumour cell may be equal (21a.5). If only cancer stem cells self-renew, shrinking a tumour is not curing it.
  • The mutations come from somewhere (21a.6). Viruses, chemical carcinogens, radiation.
  • Most of that exposure is behavioural (21a.7). Tobacco and diet account for two-thirds of US cancer deaths.
  • Detection, then treatment (21a.8, 21a.9). Every conventional treatment attacks rapidly dividing cells, which explains both the side effects and why p53-mutant cancers resist them.

21a.1Uncontrolled Cell Division

pp. 459–460
Big ideaAll cancers share uncontrolled cell division, but it is the spread of cells away from the original tumour — not the original tumour — that kills.
  • Tumour vs. cancer.
    • Tumour (neoplasm) — an abnormal growth of cells forming a mass of tissue. Not all tumours are cancerous.
    • Benign tumour — surrounded by a connective tissue capsule, stays where it forms, neither invades nor spreads. Usually not life-threatening because surgery can remove it completely. Still harmful if it presses on nearby tissue, and life-threatening if inoperable, as in the brain.
    • Malignant tumour — invades surrounding tissue and spreads. Only malignant tumours are properly called cancerous.
    • Metastasis — the spread of cancer cells from one part of the body to another.
  • Stages of development.
    • Dysplasia — changes in shape, nuclei and tissue organisation of precancerous cells. Ragged edges, unusually large and atypically shaped nuclei that may hold increased DNA.
    • Karyotype changes — extra copies of some chromosomes, missing parts of others, extra parts of still others.
    • Disorganised clump — an unusually high percentage of cells in the process of dividing.
    • Critical mass of about a million cells — still only a millimetre or two across, but interior cells cannot get nutrients and are poisoned by their own waste. At this stage it is a carcinoma in situ, "cancer in place".
  • The ominous transition.
    • Attracting a blood supply. Tumour cells secrete chemicals that cause blood vessels to invade. The new supply brings nutrients and removes waste — and is equally an escape route into blood or lymphatic vessels.
    • Why it changes treatment. A tumour that stays put can grow large and still be surgically removable. Once cells leave, they usually spread to too many sites for surgery.
    • Where the deaths come from. The original tumour is rarely a cause of death. Tumours at distant sites are responsible for 90% of the deaths of people with cancer.
  • How cancer kills. It interferes with normal cell function — cancer cells are greedy and deprive normal cells of nutrients, prevent healthy cells carrying out their usual functions, and can block blood vessels or air passageways or press on vital nerve pathways.

21a.2Development of Cancer

pp. 461–462
Big ideaCancer is the sequential defeat of five normal control systems — cell-cycle checkpoints, programmed cell death, division limits, restriction of blood-vessel formation and cell adhesion — plus escape from the body's defence cells.
  • Genetic controls on division.
    • Cell cycle checkpoints. A healthy cell that detects damage stops the cycle, assesses and repairs. Successful repair resumes the cycle; damage judged too severe initiates cell death. Incomplete repair lets damage accumulate.
    • Tumour-suppressor genes inhibit or stop division — their products are the brakes. Some detect damaged DNA; others manage the repair shop and can activate death genes.
    • p53 produces a protein regulating another gene whose product holds cells in a nondividing state. Mutant p53 is a factor in more than half of all cancers.
    • BRCA1 and BRCA2 products initiate DNA repair and play a role in breast cancer.
    • Proto-oncogenes stimulate division by producing growth factors, affecting their function, or affecting the activity of certain genes.
    • Oncogene — a mutated proto-oncogene; a stuck accelerator. Mutation often yields too much protein, or a more active one.
    • ras — its product normally signals a growth factor's presence; the oncogene protein is hyperactive and stimulates division even without growth factors.
    • Contact inhibition. Normal cells divide until they form a single layer, then stop on contact. Cancer cells show none and pile up.
  • DNA damage and cell destruction.
    • Apoptosis — programmed cell death; "death genes" prompt manufacture of proteins that kill the cell.
    • Blebs — membrane bulges pinched off during apoptosis; the cell breaks into fragments that other cells engulf.
    • Cancer cells often fail to trigger apoptosis, and faulty p53 is often partly responsible.
    • Why that matters for treatment. Tumours with damaged p53 grow aggressively, spread quickly and resist radiation and chemotherapy — because those work by damaging DNA to trigger a self-destruction mutant p53 cells cannot perform.
  • Unlimited division.
    • Most human cells divide only about 50 or 60 times before senescence.
    • Telomeres protect chromosome tips like the plastic on shoelaces; telomerase builds them.
    • Most cells stop producing telomerase after embryonic development, so each copy shaves a piece off every telomere. When gone, tips fuse and the cell dies.
    • The cancer cell's fountain of youth is unceasing telomerase production. Telomerase is present in nearly 90% of biopsies of human cancerous tumours.
  • Blood supply and adhesion.
    • At roughly a million cells, growth stops unless the tumour attracts a blood supply. Cancer cells release growth factors that cause capillaries to invade.
    • Normally cells produce a protein preventing new vessels forming — and the gene that makes it is p53.
    • Cellular adhesion molecules (CAMs) glue normal cells in place; unglued normal cells stop dividing and self-destruct.
    • Cancer cells break loose by secreting enzymes that degrade CAMs, and keep dividing because their oncogenes send a false message that the cell is still attached.
  • Body defence cells. Cancer cells arise daily and natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells usually kill them. Some cancer cells inhibit the defence cells; some multiply too fast to be destroyed.

Keep the metaphor straight. Tumour-suppressor genes are the brakes; proto-oncogenes are the accelerator. A tumour-suppressor mutation removes a brake; a proto-oncogene mutation jams the accelerator.

21a.3Multiple Mutations

pp. 463
Big ideaBecause so many independent safeguards have to fail, cancer is a multistep process requiring mutations accumulated over years — which is why an inherited mutation confers predisposition, not the disease.
  • The safeguards, stacked. Tumour-suppressor genes and proto-oncogenes restrict division to growth and repair; a mutated cell normally self-destructs; lacking telomerase a cell is limited to 50–60 divisions; without a blood supply the tumour starves at about a million cells; without breaking its anchoring molecules it cannot metastasise.
  • Accumulation over time. The first mutation passes to all descendant cells. Usually many years later a second occurs in one descendant; both are then passed on. Many years later a third may occur in a cell already carrying two.
  • How many genes. Damage must occur in at least two genes, most commonly more than six, before cancer occurs.
  • The colon example. Colon cells must accumulate damage in at least one proto-oncogene and three tumour-suppressor genes.
  • A single tumour may contain different cell lines carrying mutations in different sets of genes.
  • Inherited predisposition. Someone inheriting one mutant gene may be predisposed, but a second event is required before uncontrolled division is unleashed.

Key terms

Multistep process

Predisposition

Researchers found 50,000 mutations in the lung tumour of a single heavy smoker — the scale of damage a lifetime of carcinogen exposure produces.

"At least two, most commonly more than six" is the general rule. "One proto-oncogene and three tumour-suppressor genes" is specifically the colon example. Don't swap them.

21a.4Cancer and Epigenomics

pp. 464
Big ideaGene activity can be switched on or off without any change to the nucleotide sequence, so cancer can develop epigenetically — and because epigenetic changes are reversible, they are also a potential cure route.
  • Mutation vs. epigenome. A mutation changes the sequence of nucleotides. The epigenome is a network of chemical tags that bind DNA and turn genes on or off without altering the sequence.
  • Both directions cause trouble. Tumour-suppressor genes turned off allow mutations to accumulate. Oncogenes not turned off, or switched on, speed division despite inappropriate conditions.
  • The observed pattern. The gene activity patterns of most cancers reveal fewer silenced genes than those of healthy cells.
  • Heritable across generations. Some epigenetic modifications pass through generations, underscoring the importance of the mother's diet and lifestyle during pregnancy.
  • Reversibility is the opening. Because epigenetic processes are reversible, a drug that cures cancer by turning key genes on or off is conceivable in a way that reversing a mutation is not.

Key terms

Epigenome
network of chemical tags binding DNA that turns genes on or off without changing the sequence
Mutation
a change in the sequence of nucleotides in the genome
Exam tip

The distinguishing test is sequence change. Mutation = sequence altered. Epigenetic change = activity altered, sequence intact.

Watch out

Cancers show fewer silenced genes than healthy cells — not more. Check the direction of that comparison before answering.

21a.5Cancer Stem Cell Hypothesis

pp. 465
Big ideaIf only a subpopulation of tumour cells can renew itself indefinitely, then a treatment that shrinks a tumour by killing rapidly dividing cells can leave the source of recurrence untouched.
  • The question. Do all cells of a tumour have the ability to divide without restraint, metastasise and form new tumours? An increasing number of scientists suggest only some do.
  • Cancer stem cells. The subpopulation within the tumour with the capacity for unlimited self-renewal, which gives rise to the tumour.
  • What it explains. Radiation and chemotherapy target rapidly dividing cells, so they shrink tumours — but lingering cancer stem cells replenish the population, which is why tumours often return.
  • Research direction. Finding ways to cure cancer by killing the cancer stem cells specifically.

Key terms

Cancer stem cells
the tumour subpopulation with unlimited self-renewal capacity that gives rise to the tumour
Cancer stem cell hypothesis
the proposal that only this subpopulation sustains the tumour
Exam tip

Short section, high yield. This is the book's whole explanation for tumour recurrence after apparently successful treatment — a short-answer prompt if ever there was one.

21a.6Known Causes of Cancer

pp. 466
Big ideaThe genetic changes that produce cancer are usually caused by three classes of agent — viruses, chemical carcinogens and radiation.
  • Viruses — about 5% of US cancers.
    • Viral oncogenes. Some cancer-causing viruses carry oncogenes; inside the host cell a viral oncogene behaves as a host oncogene would.
    • Reverse transcriptase. If the viral information is RNA, this enzyme synthesises viral DNA for insertion into a host chromosome. Proteins from that DNA may drive a proto-oncogene to be expressed abnormally.
    • Kidnapping a proto-oncogene. RNA viruses can pick one up from one host cell and carry it into a new one.
    • Insertional disruption. Viral DNA can land at a site that disrupts a gene influencing division, breaking the switch that turns a proto-oncogene off.
    • Immune interference. Some viruses lessen the body's ability to find and destroy cancer cells.
    • Named associations: HPV with cervical, penile and other anogenital cancers; hepatitis B and C with liver cancer; Epstein-Barr with B-cell lymphomas and nasopharyngeal carcinoma; HTLV-1 with adult T-cell leukaemia; CMV with lymphomas and leukaemias; Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus with Kaposi's sarcoma.
  • Chemicals.
    • Carcinogen — an environmental agent that fosters the development of cancer.
    • A mutation in as few as one nucleotide can alter a gene's message.
    • Tobacco smoke contains a host of carcinogens, among them one that specifically mutates p53 and another that specifically mutates one of the ras proto-oncogenes.
    • Tobacco smoke is responsible for 30% of all US cancer deaths and may contribute to as many as 60%.
    • Harder to avoid: benzene, formaldehyde, hydrocarbons, certain pesticides, chemicals in some dyes and preservatives.
    • Promotion rather than mutation. Some chemicals contribute by stimulating division, raising the chance of further mutations. Certain hormones act this way.
    • Estrogen stimulates division in breast tissue and endometrium. Sustained high levels are linked with breast cancer; endometrial cancer incidence is elevated in postmenopausal women on estrogen-only HRT.
  • Radiation. Also causes cancer by producing mutations. Natural exposure cannot be avoided entirely — ultraviolet light, cosmic rays, radon, uranium. Sunlight's UV rays cause skin cancer.

21a.7Reducing the Risk of Cancer

pp. 467
Big ideaCancer is a family of more than 200 diseases named for the tissue it arises in, and two modifiable behaviours — tobacco use and diet — account for two-thirds of US cancer deaths.
  • Named by tissue of origin.
    • Carcinomas — epithelial tissues.
    • Adenocarcinomas — glandular epithelia.
    • Leukaemias — bone marrow.
    • Sarcomas — muscle, bone, cartilage or connective tissue.
    • Lymphomas — lymphatic tissues.
  • Scale. Cancer is the second leading cause of death in industrialised countries. Tobacco use and unhealthy diet are responsible for two-thirds of all US cancer deaths; tobacco smoke is the leading carcinogen.
  • Diet.
    • High-fat diet is linked to colon and breast cancers, especially saturated fat from red meat.
    • Smoked, salt-cured and nitrite-cured foods in large quantities increase risk of oesophageal and stomach cancers.
    • Fibre dilutes intestinal contents, binds carcinogens and speeds passage so carcinogens spend less time in the intestine.
    • Antioxidants interfere with oxidation, which can form free radicals that damage DNA. The three major ones: beta-carotene, vitamin E and vitamin C.
  • Reducing your risk. Don't use tobacco and avoid secondhand smoke; cut saturated fat; minimise salt-cured, pickled and smoked foods; five servings of fruit and vegetables daily; at most one or two drinks a day; maintain a healthy weight; limit sun exposure; avoid unnecessary medical x-rays; get the recommended screenings.

21a.8Diagnosing Cancer

pp. 468
Big ideaBecause treatment succeeds far more often before spread, diagnosis runs from self-awareness of warning signs through screening, imaging, biopsy, tumour markers and genetic tests.
  • The seven warning signs — CAUTION.
    • C — Change in bowel or bladder habit or function
    • A — A sore that does not heal
    • U — Unusual bleeding or bloody discharge
    • T — Thickening or lump in breast or elsewhere
    • I — Indigestion or difficulty swallowing

21a.9Treating Cancer

pp. 469
Big ideaAll three conventional treatments work by damaging rapidly dividing cells — which explains both their side effects and their failure against p53-mutant cancers — so newer approaches target the specific molecular causes instead.
  • Surgery. Used when a tumour is accessible and removable without damaging vital tissue. Complete cure is possible if every cancer cell is removed, as with carcinoma in situ. More than half of all tumours have already metastasised by the time of diagnosis.
  • Radiation. Usually follows surgery when cancer has spread but is still localised.
    • Radiation damages DNA, and extensive damage triggers programmed cell death. The greatest harm falls on rapidly dividing cells.
    • Renewal tissues — tissues whose cells keep dividing throughout life: reproductive system, skin, stomach lining, blood-forming cells, hair. Radiation cannot distinguish them from cancer, so side effects are temporary sterility, nausea, anaemia and hair loss.
    • Intensity-modulated radiation therapy delivers precise doses matched to the tumour's three-dimensional shape.
  • Chemotherapy. Used when cancer is thought to have spread by diagnosis. Drugs reach all parts of the body and kill all rapidly dividing cells — some block DNA synthesis, others damage DNA.
    • Targeting technique 1. Magnetic fields pull drug-coated metallic beads into the tumour.
    • Targeting technique 2. A light-activated drug lingers longer in cancer cells; light of a particular wavelength then produces an active form of oxygen that kills them.
  • Targeted treatment. Sequence the genome and map the epigenome to find the faulty genes, then aim drugs at the molecular causes. Gleevec blocks the abnormal protein driving an aggressive leukaemia.
  • Immunotherapy.
    • Lymphocyte factors. Interleukin-2 stimulates lymphocytes that attack cancer cells; interferons stimulate the immune system and directly affect tumour cells; tumour necrosis factor directly causes cancer cells to self-destruct.
    • Vaccine type 1 — preventive, against a virus. A vaccine against four HPVs is available. HPV16 and HPV18 cause 70% of cervical cancer cases. Recommended for all girls 9 to 12 and boys 11 to 12.
    • Vaccine type 2 — therapeutic. In clinical trials; stimulates T cells to attack cancer cells. Unlike most vaccines these cannot prevent cancer, only treat it.
  • Starving the tumour, and gene therapy.
    • Inhibiting blood vessel formation. Many drugs cut off these lifelines; some are FDA-approved.
    • Gene therapy. More than 400 clinical trials currently target cancer, but the FDA has approved none of them.
    • Strategy 1. Insert normal tumour-suppressor genes — a healthy p53 — hoping to trigger cell death.
    • Strategy 2, most promising. Insert a viral gene for thymidine kinase, whose enzyme sensitises cells to ganciclovir, inactive except inside cells producing that enzyme — so only tumour cells die.

Exam tip

Every side effect, one cause. Renewal tissues divide rapidly too. If asked why hair loss, anaemia, nausea or sterility occurs, name the renewal tissue — that is where the points are.

Exam tip

Two vaccine types, easily conflated. The HPV vaccine prevents — it targets a virus, before exposure. The trial vaccines treat — they target cancer cells that already exist, and cannot prevent anything.

Stop And Think

1Why would telomerase activity work as a tissue marker for cancerous cells? (p. 462)

Because the enzyme's distribution separates the two populations almost cleanly. Most mature human cells stop producing telomerase after embryonic development, so normal tissue has little or none, while the gene is switched back on in most cancer cells — telomerase appears in nearly 90% of biopsies of human cancerous tumours. The honest limit: 21a.8 says a telomerase test is still experimental, and high sensitivity is not a validated diagnostic.

2Investigating a cancer cluster caused by a common exposure — which risks would you look for? (p. 466)

The shared-exposure risks, not the individual ones. First chemical carcinogens — contaminated air, water or soil, plus any local industrial or occupational source. Then radiation — radon, uranium, medical x-rays, sunlight for skin cancers. Then oncogenic viruses, if the cluster is of a virus-linked cancer. Inherited predisposition explains a geographic cluster poorly unless the population is closely related; tobacco and diet matter mainly as confounders to rule out. The cancer type narrows the search — a lung cluster points at inhaled carcinogens or radon, a liver cluster at hepatitis B or C.

    • A 5' cap is added — assists translation and protects from degradation.
    • A poly-A tail is added — assists in leaving the nucleus and protects from degradation.
  • Only then does the mRNA leave through a nuclear pore.

L1.4Transcription, step by step

Big ideaA promoter marks the start, RNA polymerase opens the region and builds a complementary RNA copy, and the DNA zips back up behind it.
  • 1. The DNA unzips. The strands are held by hydrogen bonds — the weakest of all chemical bonds. Two between A and T, three between C and G.
  • 2. A promoter says "start here." Just a sequence of bases marking where transcription begins.
  • 3. RNA polymerase binds the promoter and opens the region. It then recruits helicase to unwind the helix and break the hydrogen bonds.
  • 4. RNA nucleotides are added complementary to the template and linked together. Where the DNA reads T the RNA gets A; where the DNA reads A the RNA gets U.
  • 5. The DNA zips back up behind the polymerase. Exposed bases can't be left hanging.
  • 6. A stop signal — again just a sequence of bases — ends the process and the transcript dissociates.
  • The sequence carries everything. All the information, all the code, all the instruction is in the order of the bases.
Conflict — recorded, not resolved

Lecture beats the textbook here, so give his version — but the exam-relevant fact either way is that RNA polymerase is the enzyme that links the RNA nucleotides.

Worked example from the text

A student asked whether one base "takes more of the hydrogen" when a C–G pair with three bonds breaks. Like magnets: pull them apart and they simply stop attracting. Nothing is taken, given, or exchanged.

L1.5Splicing, and why one gene isn't one protein

Big ideaAlternative splicing means a single gene can yield several different proteins, so one gene does not equal one protein.
  • The fresh transcript isn't ready to ship — it carries material that won't make it into the final message.
  • Introns — intervening sequences between the promoter and stop signal that carry no code to be translated. Enzymes cut them out and they are discarded.
  • Exons — the sequences that will be expressed. They are glued back together in order to form the final mRNA.
  • Alternative splicing. You don't have to use every exon. A transcript with exons 1, 2 and 3 can be spliced as 1+2+3, or 1+2, or 1+3.
    • Those are not the same protein. Protein 1+2 is structured differently from 1+2+3 — and if it is structured differently it functions differently, because structure determines function.
    • So one gene does not equal one protein.
  • One mRNA does not equal one protein either. Transcribing a gene doesn't guarantee a protein — if an amino acid the recipe calls for isn't available, the protein can't be built.
  • The amino acid arithmetic. There are 20 amino acids in nature. Your body can make 11. That leaves 9 you cannot make — the essential amino acids, so called because it is essential you get them from the environment by eating.

L1.6The genetic code

Big ideamRNA is read in three-base codons; one start, three stops, and heavy redundancy.
  • A set of three nucleotides is a codon — codons are the words of the code, and each translates to one amino acid.
  • One start codon only: AUG. It always codes methionine.
  • Three stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA. They code no amino acid and have no matching tRNA.
  • Redundancy is everywhere. Leucine has six codons; serine has six.
  • The eight properties he read out.
    • Written in linear form, using mRNA bases as letters.
    • Each word is three letters — a codon.
    • Unambiguous — one codon specifies only a single amino acid.
    • Degenerate — one amino acid may be coded for by more than one codon.
    • Has start and stop codons.
    • Commaless — bases are read one after another with no punctuation.
    • Non-overlapping — each base is read in order, with no overlap.
    • Nearly universal — the same dictionary is used by all living things, with minor exceptions.
Worked example from the text

How the first codon was cracked. Nirenberg and Matthaei set up 20 tubes, each containing all 20 amino acids but with only one radioactively labelled per tube. To each they added synthetic mRNA made solely of UUU plus everything needed for translation. In the tube where phenylalanine was labelled they got a radioactive chain of phenylalanines — identifying UUU = phenylalanine and opening the door to the rest of the code.

Exam tip

"Unambiguous" and "degenerate" sound contradictory but are not. One codon → one amino acid (unambiguous). One amino acid ← many codons (degenerate). The asymmetry is the point.

L1.9Mutations

3 slides
Big ideaTypos happen at replication, repair usually fixes them, and the repair that fixes the wrong strand locks the error in.
  • Every time DNA is replicated there is a chance of a typo. Repair proteins scan, cut them out, insert the correct base and seal it back up. That happens during DNA replication, which is also where the typos are introduced.
  • Inheritance. A mutation is passed to offspring only if it occurs in a gamete — sperm or egg. A mutation in a body cell affects that cell and its descendants but goes no further.
  • Chromosomal mutations. A whole section is duplicated or deleted. Every gene sits at a specific address called a locus (plural loci). Deletion is harsher: lose a piece and you lose every gene located there, with no replacement.
  • Frameshift. The most common and often most damaging. Insert or delete a single base and everything downstream is misread — not just the site of the change.
  • Outcomes, in order of frequency: silent (no meaningful change), harmful, and occasionally beneficial.

Key terms

Locus
the specific chromosomal address of a gene
Frameshift
an insertion or deletion that misreads every codon downstream
Watch out

The repair that backfires. Suppose a C is inserted where a T belonged. Repair enzymes notice the mismatch — but if they "correct" the original base instead of the new wrong one, the pair now matches and looks fine. The error is locked in.

L1.10Gene regulation

3 slides
Big ideaOnly certain genes are switched on in a given cell, and that is what gives cells their specialisation.

L1.11Genetic engineering

18 slides
Big ideaCut with a restriction enzyme, paste with ligase, carry on a plasmid, select with a reporter gene, amplify and express.

L2.9The cell cycle

Big ideaInterphase is G1, S and G2 together and takes almost all of a cell's life; M phase is the sliver at the end.
  • G1 — first gap / growth. The new cell grows to adult size and does its job. "A baby cell grows up to be a big boy cell" — where most of your life has been.

L2.10Mitosis: P-MAT

Big ideaFour stages, each with a characteristic event he told you outright to memorise.
  • Prophase. Loose DNA condenses into individually identifiable chromosomes; the nuclear membrane starts to dissolve; the mitotic spindle forms — centrosomes with microtubules — and migrates toward opposite poles.
  • Metaphase. All 46 chromosomes line up at the metaphase plate, the equator of the cell. Alignment is what guarantees each daughter gets one chromatid from each chromosome.
  • Anaphase. The link at the centromere breaks; sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles. Once separated they are called chromosomes again, not chromatids.
  • Telophase. Nuclear envelope reforms around each set; cleavage furrow appears; spindle disintegrates. Cytokinesis begins here.
  • The machinery he named.
    • Kinetochore protein sits at the centromere. When a microtubule attaches to it, that fibre is renamed a kinetochore fibre.
    • Fibres pull from both sides — his tug-of-war — first aligning the chromosome at the middle, then tearing it at the centromere.
    • The cleavage furrow is a ring of filamentous actin ratcheted down by a motor protein, non-muscle myosin II. His analogy: a string tied around a balloon and pulled tight. He noted the myosin II detail is beyond what is required here.

The week, told once

Week 3 is one argument in four moves: DNA is information, the information gets copied, the information gets read, and the information can be edited — by mutation, by regulation, or deliberately by us. Ch. 21 walks that chain, the lab builds it in plastic, and Ch. 21a is what happens when the controls on the last step fail.

Everything rests on one rule. A pairs with T, C pairs with G. Because pairing is specific, either strand contains enough information to rebuild the other — which is simultaneously why replication is accurate (21.2), why transcription works (21.3), and why the lab could hand you one strand and ask you to derive the rest.

The chain, as all three sources tell it:

  • Information. DNA is a nucleotide ladder: sugar-phosphate sides, paired bases as rungs, hydrogen bonds holding them — two for A–T, three for C–G. The sequence is the message.
  • Copying. Helicase unzips, each old strand becomes a template, DNA polymerase links new nucleotides. Semiconservative — every daughter keeps one parental strand. The lab makes this concrete: leading strand continuous, lagging strand in Okazaki fragments sealed by ligase.
  • Reading. DNA stays in the nucleus, so a portable copy goes out. Transcription makes mRNA from the template strand; processing cuts introns, splices exons, adds a 5' cap and poly-A tail; translation reads codons three at a time at the ribosome, tRNA delivering one amino acid each. Start at AUG, stop at UAA/UAG/UGA.
  • Editing, accidentally. A substitution changes at most one codon and may change nothing. An insertion or deletion shifts the reading frame and garbles everything downstream — worse, and worse the earlier it falls.
  • Editing, deliberately. Cut gene and vector with the same restriction enzyme so the sticky ends match, seal with ligase, carry on a plasmid, select, amplify by cloning or PCR, express and harvest.
  • When editing goes wrong. Ch. 21a is Ch. 21's last section read as pathology — mutations in the genes that regulate division defeat one safeguard after another until cells divide without restraint.

What only lecture gives you. Three things sit on the Lecture 1 tab and nowhere else. Alternative splicing — you don't have to use every exon, so one gene does not equal one protein — which he spent more time on than almost anything. The amino acid arithmetic: 20 in nature, 11 your body makes, 9 essential. And the reporter gene step in cloning: the plasmid carries antibiotic resistance, so growing on that antibiotic selects the bacteria that took your plasmid up. The textbook says "identify the right colony" without saying how.

What the lab gives you that reading cannot. The lab's whole point is that the derivation is mechanical. Given 5'-ATGGGCTCATAA-3' you can write the template, the mRNA, and the peptide without looking anything up beyond the codon chart — and the chart is supplied on the exam. Practise the derivation, not the chart.

Where Ch. 21a changes the emphasis. It has no end-of-chapter question set — none, confirmed against the printed page. So there is nothing to practise against, and lecture plus lab carry more weight for it than for any other chapter this term.
