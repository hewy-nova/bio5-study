Did you know

  • The prevalence of nearsightedness (myopia) in young adults in the United States has almost doubled in the past 30 years, probably because the amount of time spent on close work, such as using computers, has increased.
  • A human has about 12 million olfactory receptors and can distinguish about 10,000 odors.

OPENING

Chapter 8 covered how the brain integrates sensory information to direct appropriate responses, and Chapter 8a how psychoactive drugs alter that integration. This chapter goes one step back up the chain, to the receptors themselves: the general senses (touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, pain) and the special senses (vision, hearing, balance, smell, taste). Vision, hearing and balance get the close look — how light, sound and body position are reported by the structures of the eye and ear — and smell and taste are taken together, because they work together. (p. 152)

The chapter as one chain

  • A receptor is a transducer. Sensory receptors detect a change in the environment (a stimulus) and turn it into an electrochemical message; if that message reaches threshold it becomes an action potential. Nothing else in the chapter makes sense until this is in place.
  • The brain, not the receptor, decides what you experience. Sensation is awareness of a stimulus; perception is the cortex's interpretation of it. Which part of the cortex receives the impulse determines whether you see, hear or feel — which is why pressing on the eyelid produces light.
  • Receptors are classified by the stimulus they answer to — mechano-, thermo-, photo-, chemo- and pain receptors. Every sense in the rest of the chapter is one of these five in a particular package.
  • The general senses are the five classes scattered loose through the body — touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, body position, pain — mostly free or encapsulated nerve endings in skin, muscle, joints and viscera.
  • The special senses are the same classes concentrated into organs in the head. Vision and hearing get the most space because the organ does most of the work: the eye focuses light onto photoreceptors, the ear converts air pressure waves into fluid waves and then into hair-cell bending.
  • Hearing and balance share a receptor and an organ. Both are hair cells in the inner ear; the cochlea bends them with sound, the vestibular apparatus bends them with head movement and gravity.
  • Smell and taste are both chemoreceptors, and they are not separable in practice — about 80% of what we call flavour is smell.

9.1Sensory Receptors

pp. 153
Big ideaSensory receptors convert environmental change into electrochemical messages that, if strong enough to reach threshold, become nerve impulses the brain interprets.

Outline

  • Sensory receptors. Structures specialised to detect and respond to changes in the environment, known as stimuli. Information about both the external world and the internal world of the body arrives this way. SEE FIGURE 9.1
    • If a stimulus is strong enough, the message eventually becomes a nerve impulse (action potential) conducted to the spinal cord or brain by sensory neurons.
  • Sensation is an awareness of a stimulus. Whether it is experienced as sight, sound or something else depends on which part of the brain receives the nerve impulses — not on the receptor and not on the stimulus.
    • Photoreceptors respond best to light but can also respond to pressure. Either way their impulses go to the visual cortex, so we see light. Pressing gently on closed eyelids produces the sensation of spots of light.
  • Perception is the brain's interpretation of sensory input — understanding, not just awareness. Perception occurs when the cerebral cortex integrates sensory input.
    • Light reflected from a banana strikes the eye and stimulates photoreceptors; the brain interprets the pattern of input and we perceive a banana.
  • The response is graded, then all-or-none. A receptor's response is a change in the charge difference across its plasma membrane, and it varies in magnitude with the strength of the stimulus — the louder the sound, the larger the change, up to a point.
    • When the change reaches a critical level, called threshold level, an action potential is generated.
  • Sensory adaptation. Most receptor types gradually stop responding when stimulated continuously, and we become less aware of the stimulus.
    • The musty smell of an antique store is obvious to someone walking in but not to the salesclerk who works there.
    • Pressure and touch receptors adapt quickly — which is why we stop noticing clothing against the skin.
    • Adaptation can also occur in the central nervous system: the reticular activating system filters stimuli (Chapter 8).
    • Some receptors adapt slowly or not at all. The receptors in muscles and joints that report body-part position never adapt; their continuous input is essential for coordinated movement and balance.

Key terms

sensory receptor
structure specialised to detect and respond to a change in the environment
stimulus (pl. stimuli)
a change in the environment that a receptor can detect
sensation
awareness of a stimulus
perception
the brain's interpretation or understanding of sensory input
threshold level
the critical level of membrane charge change at which an action potential is generated
sensory adaptation
the gradual decline in a receptor's response to a continuous stimulus
Exam tip

The exam-sized distinction is sensation vs. perception. Sensation = awareness, happens because impulses arrive; perception = interpretation, happens when the cortex integrates. The eyelid-pressing example is the book's own test of whether you have it: the stimulus is pressure, the sensation is light, because the destination decides.

Exam tip

"Never adapt" is a short list and it is examinable — the muscle and joint receptors that report body position. Everything else on the list adapts, touch and pressure fastest.

9.2Classes of Receptors

pp. 154
Big ideaReceptors are classified by the kind of stimulus they respond to, and there are five classes.

Outline

  • Mechanoreceptors. Responsible for touch, pressure, hearing and equilibrium. Also detect changes in blood pressure, and others report the body's position.
    • They respond to distortions in the receptor itself or in nearby cells.
  • Thermoreceptors. Detect changes in temperature.
  • Photoreceptors. Detect changes in light intensity.
  • Chemoreceptors. Respond to chemicals.
    • Input from chemoreceptors of the mouth is taste (gustation); from the nose, smell (olfaction).
    • Other chemoreceptors monitor levels of specific substances — carbon dioxide, oxygen, glucose — in body fluids.
  • Pain receptors (nociceptors). Respond to very strong stimuli that usually result from physical or chemical damage to tissues.
    • Sometimes classed with chemoreceptors, because they often respond to chemicals liberated by damaged tissue.
    • Occasionally classed with mechanoreceptors, because they are stimulated by physical changes such as swelling in the damaged tissue.
  • External vs. internal receptors. Receptors near the body surface respond to environmental stimuli and we are usually aware of them. Receptors inside the body monitor internal conditions and we often are not.
    • Internal receptors are key components of the feedback loops that regulate blood pressure, blood chemistry and breathing rate — vital to homeostasis.
    • They also cause pain, hunger and thirst, prompting us to attend to the body's needs.
  • General senses vs. special senses. The organising split for the rest of the chapter.
    • General senses — touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, body and limb position, and pain — arise from receptors in skin, muscles, joints, bones and internal organs. We are not usually aware of them, but they report body position and help hold internal conditions within healthy limits.
    • Special senses — vision, hearing, balance (equilibrium), smell and taste. The receptors are located in the head, most of them within specific structures.

Key terms

mechanoreceptor
responds to distortion of the receptor or nearby cells — touch, pressure, hearing, equilibrium, blood pressure, body position
thermoreceptor
detects changes in temperature
photoreceptor
detects changes in light intensity
chemoreceptor
responds to chemicals — taste, smell, and internal levels of CO2, O2, glucose
pain receptor (nociceptor)
responds to very strong stimuli that damage tissue
general senses
touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, body and limb position, pain — receptors distributed through the body
special senses
vision, hearing, balance, smell, taste — receptors concentrated in the head
Exam tip

Pain receptors are the one class the book itself will not file cleanly — it says they are "sometimes classed with" chemoreceptors and "occasionally classed with" mechanoreceptors. If a question asks for five classes, name them as five; if it asks why nociceptors are awkward, the answer is that they respond both to damage chemicals and to physical change.

Exam tip

Hearing and equilibrium are mechanoreceptor senses, not a class of their own. That is the single most commonly dropped item from this list, and §9.5 and §9.6 both depend on it.

9.3The General Senses

pp. 155
Big ideaThe general senses are free or encapsulated nerve endings distributed through skin, muscle, joints and viscera, each type tuned to touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, position or pain.

Outline

  • Free vs. encapsulated nerve endings. Receptors for general senses are distributed throughout the body; some monitor internal conditions, others report the outside world. SEE FIGURE 9.2
    • Free nerve endings are the tips of dendrites of sensory neurons, not protected by an accessory structure.
    • An encapsulated nerve ending is one in which a connective tissue capsule encloses and protects the tips of the dendrites.
  • Touch, pressure and vibration. Mechanoreceptors respond to any stimulus that stretches, compresses or twists the receptor membrane.
    • Touch is used throughout life to learn about the world and to communicate. Pressure messages matter too — some tell us to loosen a belt after a big meal; others monitor internal conditions including blood pressure.
    • Free nerve endings wrapped around hair bases detect bending of the fine hairs on the skin (light touch).
    • Merkel disks — free nerve endings together with the special cells they end on (Merkel cells). When compressed, Merkel cells stimulate the free nerve endings to tell us something has touched us. Found on both hairy and hairless skin.
    • Meissner's corpuscles — encapsulated endings that tell us exactly where we have been touched. Common on hairless, very sensitive skin: lips, nipples, fingertips.
    • Pacinian corpuscles — onionlike layers of tissue surrounding a nerve ending. Respond when pressure is first applied and adapt quickly, so they are important in sensing vibration. Scattered in the deeper layers of skin and underlying tissue.
    • Ruffini corpuscles — encapsulated endings that respond to continuous pressure.
    • Pressure as a sensation generally lasts longer than touch and is felt over a larger area.
  • Temperature change. Thermoreceptors in humans are specialised free nerve endings just below the skin surface. SEE FIGURE 9.2
    • One kind responds to cold, another to warmth.
    • Widely distributed but especially numerous around the lips and mouth.
    • They are very active while temperature is changing and adapt rapidly when it is stable — which is why hot-tub water feels scalding at first and comfortably warm soon after.
  • Body and limb position. The brain continuously scans signals from muscles and joints to check alignment and coordinate balance and movement. SEE FIGURE 9.3
    • Muscle spindles — specialised muscle fibres wrapped in sensory nerve endings; monitor the length of a skeletal muscle and respond when the muscle is stretched.
    • Tendon organs — highly branched nerve fibres located in tendons (the connective tissue bands connecting muscle to bone); measure the degree of muscle tension. When a muscle contracts it increases tension on the tendon organs, which respond.
    • The brain combines muscle spindle and tendon organ information with input from the inner ear to coordinate movement.
  • Pain. Pain receptors are free nerve endings found in almost every tissue of the body.
    • When tissue is damaged, cells release chemicals that alert the free nerve endings; the stimulated sensory neurons carry the message to the brain, where it is interpreted as pain.
    • Aspirin and ibuprofen reduce pain by interfering with the production of one of the released chemicals.
    • Any stimulus strong enough to damage tissue — heat, cold, touch, pressure — can cause pain.
    • Referred pain. Pain originating in an internal organ is sometimes perceived as pain in an uninjured region of the skin. SEE FIGURE 9.4
      • The pain of a heart attack is often experienced as pain in the left arm.
      • Probable mechanism: sensory neurons from the internal organ and from a particular skin region communicate with the same neurons in the spinal cord, so the brain interprets the input as coming from the skin.
    • Pain warns and protects — it usually stops a person with a broken leg from causing further damage. But pain that persists long after the warning is needed can be debilitating.

Key terms

free nerve ending
tip of a sensory neuron's dendrite, unprotected by an accessory structure
encapsulated nerve ending
dendrite tips enclosed and protected by a connective tissue capsule
Merkel disk
free nerve ending plus its Merkel cell; light touch, hairy and hairless skin
Meissner's corpuscle
encapsulated ending for precise location of light touch; lips, nipples, fingertips
Pacinian corpuscle
onionlike encapsulated ending in deep skin; strong pressure and vibration, adapts quickly
Ruffini corpuscle
encapsulated ending responding to continuous pressure
muscle spindle
specialised muscle fibre with sensory nerve endings; monitors muscle length / stretch
tendon organ
branched nerve fibre in a tendon; measures muscle tension
referred pain
pain from an internal organ perceived as coming from a region of skin
Exam tip

The four named skin corpuscles are the classic matching question, and each has exactly one discriminator: Merkel = light touch; Meissner = where you were touched; Pacinian = vibration / fast-adapting strong pressure; Ruffini = continuous pressure. End-of-chapter Q1 tests Pacinian.

Exam tip

Spindle vs. tendon organ is length vs. tension. Spindles sit in the muscle and fire when it is stretched; tendon organs sit in the tendon and fire when the muscle contracts.

Exam tip

Referred pain is explained by convergence in the spinal cord, not by anything happening in the skin. Figure 9.4's map is the one to know for the left-arm/heart example.

9.4Vision

pp. 156–159
Big ideaThe eye is a three-layered ball that focuses light with cornea and lens onto the retina, where rods and cones convert light into neural messages the brain assembles into an image.

Outline

  • Wall of the eyeball — three layers. The human eyeball is an irregular sphere about 25 mm (1 in.) in diameter. SEE FIGURE 9.5 · SEE TABLE 9.1
    • Outer layer — a tough, fibrous covering with two regions.
      • Sclera — the white of the eye; protects and shapes the eyeball and is the attachment site for the muscles that move the eye.
      • Cornea — transparent, bulges slightly outward at the front and centre; the window through which light enters.
    • Middle layer — three regions.
      • Choroid — contains many blood vessels supplying nutrients and oxygen to the tissues of the eye. Also contains the brown pigment melanin, which absorbs light reflected from the retina; this sharpens vision by preventing excessive reflection of light inside the eye.
      • Ciliary body — toward the front the choroid becomes a ring of tissue, primarily muscle, encircling the lens. It holds the lens in place and controls its shape.
      • Iris — the part of the choroid in front of the ciliary body; the coloured portion seen through the cornea. Regulates the amount of light entering the eye. Shaped like a flat doughnut.
        • The pupil is the doughnut hole — the opening in the centre of the iris through which light enters.
        • The iris contains smooth muscle fibres that automatically adjust pupil size: the pupil dilates in dim light and constricts in bright light.
        • Pupil size is also affected by emotion — dilating when frightened or very interested, constricting when bored.
    • Inner layer — the retina, containing almost a quarter-billion photoreceptors, the structures that respond to light by generating electrical signals.
      • Two types of photoreceptor: rods and cones.
      • Fovea — a small region in the centre of the retina where photoreceptors are most concentrated. To see fine detail, light must be focused on the fovea. The fovea is only the size of a pinhead, so at any moment only about a thousandth of the visual field is in sharp focus; eye movements bring different parts of the field to it.
      • Optic nerve — carries the message from the eye to the brain.
      • Blind spot — the region where the optic nerve leaves the retina has no photoreceptors, so an image striking it cannot be seen. We are not usually aware of it because involuntary eye movements constantly shift the image on the retina and the brain "fills in" the missing parts.
  • Fluid-filled chambers. The ciliary body and lens divide the interior of the eyeball into two cavities. SEE FIGURE 9.5
    • Posterior chamber — at the back, between lens and retina; filled with a jellylike fluid, vitreous humor. Keeps the eyeball from collapsing and holds the thin retina against the wall of the eye. Produced during embryonic development and never replaced.
    • Anterior chamber — at the front, between cornea and lens; filled with aqueous humor. Supplies nutrients and oxygen to cornea and lens and carries away their metabolic wastes; creates pressure within the eye that helps maintain its shape.
      • Aqueous humor is replaced bit by bit about every 90 minutes — continuously produced from the capillaries of the ciliary body, circulating through the anterior chamber and draining into the blood through channels surrounding the eye.
    • Glaucoma. If drainage of aqueous humor is blocked, pressure within the eye may rise to dangerous levels. It is the second most common cause of blindness, after cataracts.
      • Accumulating aqueous humor pushes the lens partially into the posterior cavity, raising pressure there and compressing the retina and optic nerve. This collapses the tiny blood vessels that nourish the photoreceptors and optic nerve fibres; deprived of nutrients and oxygen they begin to die and vision fades.
      • Glaucoma is progressive but painless, making detection difficult. Late signs: blurred vision, headaches, halos around objects.
  • Focusing and sharp vision. Sharp vision requires entering light rays to converge so their focal point falls on the retina. SEE FIGURE 9.6
    • Most bending of light occurs at the curved surface of the cornea. Because of the way the curved cornea bends light, the image on the retina is upside down and backward.
    • The cornea has a fixed shape, so it always bends light to the same degree and cannot adjust for distance.
    • The lens is elastic and changes shape. A rounder, thicker lens bends light to a greater degree, letting the eye focus on near objects. Changing lens shape to alter light bending is accommodation.
    • Ciliary muscle control — and it is counter-intuitive. The ciliary muscle is circular and attached to the lens by ligaments.
      • Ciliary muscle contracts → its diameter becomes smaller (like pursed lips) → tension on the ligaments is relaxed → the lens is freed to assume a rounded shape → focus on nearby objects.
      • Ciliary muscle relaxes → diameter increases → tension on ligaments and lens increases → lens flattens → focus on distant objects.
    • With age the lens becomes less elastic and does not round up as easily, which is why we hold the newspaper farther away as we get older.
  • Cataracts. A cataract is a cloudiness or opaqueness in the lens, usually a result of aging. SEE FIGURE 9.7
    • The lens typically takes on a yellowish hue that blocks light on its way to the retina. Early on, a haze that can limit activities and cause car accidents, early retirement and dangerous falls; as the lens becomes more opaque the fog thickens.
    • Cataracts are the leading cause of blindness worldwide. Treated by surgically removing the clouded lens and replacing it with an artificial lens.
    • Most common eye problem affecting men and women older than 50. Risk reduction: wear sunglasses in bright sunlight (UV and bright light cause cataracts); minimise exposure to cigarette smoke, which also causes them.
  • Focusing problems. The three most common visual problems at all ages are focusing problems; corrective lenses usually restore normal vision. SEE FIGURE 9.8 · SEE TABLE 9.2
    • Farsightedness (hyperopia) — distant objects seen more clearly than nearby ones. Roughly 5% to 10% of Americans. The eyeball is too short or the lens too thin, focusing images of nearer objects behind the retina. The lens cannot become round enough to bend light sufficiently for close objects.
      • Corrected with convex lenses — thicker in the middle than at the edges — which converge the light a bit before it enters the eye.
    • Nearsightedness (myopia) — nearby objects seen more clearly than distant ones. The eyeball is elongated or the lens too thick, focusing the image in front of the retina. The lens cannot flatten enough to bring distant objects to the retina.
      • Corrected with concave lenses — thinner in the middle than at the edges — which diverge the light slightly before it enters the eye.
      • Genetics plays a role, but frequent close work is also a cause: frequent ciliary muscle contractions raise pressure within the eye, which can stretch and elongate it. Eye specialists recommend looking up from page or screen at frequent intervals, particularly if nearsightedness runs in the family.
    • Astigmatism — irregularities in the curvature of the cornea or lens cause light rays to converge unevenly, distorting the image. Almost always corrected by lenses that compensate for the asymmetrical bending.
    • Surgical reshaping of the cornea.
      • LASIK (laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis) — permanently changes corneal shape: a flap is cut in the cornea, a computer-controlled laser reshapes the middle layer, the flap is replaced. Corrects nearsightedness, farsightedness and astigmatism.
      • LASEK (laser-assisted subepithelial keratomileusis) — flap in the epithelium only rather than the entire cornea, eliminating some complications of deeper corneal flaps. Mostly for people who are poor LASIK candidates because their corneas are thin or flat.
      • PRK (photorefractive keratectomy) — short bursts of laser shave a microscopic layer of cells off the corneal surface and flatten it; a computer calculates and controls the exposure.
  • Light and pigment molecules. The eye contains 70% of all the sensory receptors in the body. The function of rods and cones is to respond to light by sending neural messages to the brain. SEE FIGURE 9.9
    • The sequence: light waves in the visible spectrum strike an object → some wavelengths are reflected to the eye → focused by cornea and lens onto the retina → absorbed by photopigment molecules in the photoreceptors.
    • When light strikes a photopigment, the molecule's components split apart. This produces a series of reactions that reduce the photoreceptor's release of inhibitory neurotransmitter.
      • The neurotransmitter normally inhibits the cells that process the information, so reducing it increases the activity of those processing cells.
    • The optic nerve carries the signals from the retina to the thalamus, where some processing occurs; the information is then sent to the visual cortex at the back of the brain, where a perception takes shape.
    • Processing begins in the retina, before the signals leave it.
      • Messages go first to bipolar cells — rare examples of neurons with only two processes, an axon and a single dendrite, extending from opposite sides of the cell body.
      • Bipolar neurons direct the information to interneurons called ganglion cells.
      • Together, bipolar and ganglion cells convert retinal input into patterns such as edges and spots.
  • Rods: vision in dim light. SEE FIGURE 9.10
    • Rods are the photoreceptors responsible for black-and-white vision. Much more numerous than cones.
    • Exceedingly sensitive — capable of responding to light measuring one 10-billionth of a watt, the equivalent of a match burning 50 mi (80.5 km) away on a clear, pitch-dark night. (The ideal conditions to actually see this are impossible to attain.)
    • Rods let us see in dimly lit rooms and pale moonlight, and by detecting changes in light intensity across the visual field they contribute to the perception of movement.
    • Rhodopsin is the pigment in rods, packaged in membrane-bound disks stacked like coins in the rod's outer segment.
      • Light strikes a rod → splits rhodopsin → triggers events that reduce the permeability of the plasma membrane to sodium ions → changes in bipolar and ganglion cell activity.
      • In the dark, rhodopsin is resynthesised. Difficulty seeing on first walking into a dark room from bright light is because rhodopsin has been split; as it is resynthesised, vision sharpens.
  • Cones: colour vision. SEE FIGURE 9.11
    • Cones are responsible for colour vision. Unlike rods they produce sharp images, because each cone usually sends information to only one bipolar cell.
    • Colour is determined by wavelength. White light consists of all wavelengths. (Rainbows are created when the wavelengths of white light are separated passing through water droplets.) When light strikes an object some wavelengths are absorbed and others reflected; we see the reflected ones — an apple looks red because it reflects mostly red light.
    • Three types of cone — blue, green and red — named for the wavelengths they absorb best, not for their colour.
      • Each type absorbs a range of wavelengths and the ranges overlap considerably, so coloured light stimulates each type to a different extent.
      • Red apple → stimulates red cones. Ripe yellow banana → stimulates both red and green cones. Blueberries → stimulate both blue and green cones.
      • The brain interprets colour according to how strongly each type of cone is stimulated.
    • Colour blindness — certain colours cannot be distinguished from each other. Most colour-blind people see some colours but confuse certain ones.
      • The standard test shows a colour plate with dots of two colours; dots of one colour indicate a number that a colour-blind person cannot distinguish.
      • Cause is a lack, or reduced number, of one type of cone.
      • A person who lacks red cones sees deep reds as black. A person who lacks green cones sees deep reds but cannot distinguish between reds, oranges and yellows. Absence of blue cones is extremely rare.
      • People who are colour blind generally function normally, compensating with cues such as intensity, shape or position. Ironically red and green — the universal traffic colours — are the most commonly confused; but the brightest light can usually be picked out, and all signals are arranged the same way, red on top and green on the bottom.
      • Schoolchildren are regularly tested so that students and teachers are aware; the condition can cause difficulty with class work such as reading maps or graphs presented in colour.

9.5Hearing

pp. 160–164, 166
Big ideaThe ear receives sound at the outer ear, amplifies it through the middle ear bones, and converts fluid pressure waves into neural messages by bending hair cells on the basilar membrane of the cochlea.

Outline

  • Sound is vibration. From a social perspective hearing is perhaps the most vital sense, because speech binds society; it also reports things we cannot see (a car approaching from behind), adds to events we can see (a mother reading a baby's cry), and enriches life (music, waves). SEE FIGURE 9.12
    • Vibrating objects — guitar strings, a speaker surface, vocal cords — move rapidly back and forth, pushing repeatedly against the surrounding air and creating sound waves.
    • Loudness is determined by amplitude — the distance between the top of the peaks and the bottom of the troughs.
    • Pitch is determined by frequency — the number of cycles (repetitions of the wave) per second. More cycles, higher pitch.
  • Form and function of the ear — three parts. SEE FIGURE 9.13 · SEE TABLE 9.3
    • Outer ear — the receiver.
      • Pinna — the fleshy funnel visible on the head. Gathers sound and channels it into the external auditory canal. Also accentuates the frequencies of the most important speech sounds, making speech easier to pick out from background noise; its shape and placement on either side of the head help us determine the direction a sound comes from.
      • External auditory canal — leads from the pinna to the eardrum.
    • Eardrum (tympanic membrane) — the tissue separating outer from middle ear. As thin as a sheet of paper and as taut as the head of a tambourine. Vibrates at the same frequency as the sound waves and transfers the vibrations to the middle ear.
    • Middle ear — the amplifier. An air-filled cavity within a bone of the skull, spanned by the three smallest bones of the body.
      • Malleus (hammer), incus (anvil), stapes (stirrup) — named for their shapes; together a system of levers conveying airborne sound waves from the eardrum to the oval window.
      • The malleus is attached to the inner surface of the eardrum, so eardrum vibration rocks the malleus; that rocking moves the incus and then the stapes. The base of the stapes fits into the oval window, a sheet of tissue forming the threshold of the inner ear.
      • The force of the eardrum's vibrations is amplified 22 times in the middle ear. The magnification is necessary to transfer vibrations into the fluid of the inner ear. It happens because the eardrum is larger than the oval window, concentrating the pressure.
      • Auditory tube (Eustachian tube) — connects the middle ear cavity with the upper throat. Air pressure must be nearly equal on both sides of the eardrum for it to vibrate properly; unequal pressure makes the eardrum bulge inward or outward, causing pain and difficulty hearing.
        • Usually closed and flattened; swallowing or yawning opens it briefly to equalise pressure — the ear "popping". Happens in elevators, aircraft takeoff and landing, and scuba diving.
    • Inner ear — the transmitter. Generates neural messages in response to pressure waves and sends them to the brain. Contains two sensory organs: the cochlea (hearing) and the vestibular apparatus (position and movement, §9.6).
  • The cochlea. About the size of a pea; the true seat of hearing. SEE FIGURE 9.14
    • A bony tube about 35 mm (1.4 in.) long, coiled about two-and-a-half times, looking like a snail's shell. (Cochlea is from the Latin for "snail.")
    • The wider end has two membrane-covered openings: the upper is the oval window, into which the stapes fits; the lower is the round window, which relieves the pressure created by the movements of the oval window.
    • Uncoiled, the cochlea is three fluid-filled longitudinal compartments divided by two membranes.
      • The central compartment (the cochlear duct) ends blindly, like the finger of a glove, and does not extend to the end of the tube — so the upper (vestibular canal) and lower (tympanic canal) compartments are connected at the end nearest the tip of the coil.
      • The basilar membrane is the floor of the central compartment.
    • Spiral organ (of Corti) — supported on the basilar membrane; the portion of the cochlea most directly responsible for hearing. Consists of the hair cells and the overhanging tectorial membrane.
      • Each hair cell has about 100 "hairs", slender projections from its upper surface that project into the tectorial membrane. The hair cells are arranged on the basilar membrane in rows resembling miniature picket fences.
    • The transduction chain. Stapes moves to and fro against the oval window → pressure waves in the inner ear fluid → transmitted from the upper compartment's fluid to the lower compartment's (they are continuous) → the basilar membrane swings up and down → the projections on the hair cells are pressed against the tectorial membrane → bending of the hairs alters the rate of nerve impulses in the auditory (cochlear) nerve, which arises within the cochlea and carries sound information to the brain.
  • Loudness and pitch, as the ear encodes them.
    • Loudness. The louder the sound, the greater the pressure changes in the inner ear fluid and the stronger the bending of the basilar membrane. Because hair cells have different thresholds of stimulation, more vigorous vibration stimulates more hair cells; each hair cell also bends more, which increases the number of nerve impulses. The brain interprets the increased number of impulses as louder sound.
    • Pitch. Different regions of the basilar membrane vibrate in response to different pitches — the cochlea is like a spiral piano keyboard.
      • Near the oval window the basilar membrane is narrow and stiff; like the shorter strings on a piano, this region vibrates maximally to high-frequency sound such as a whistle.
      • At the tip of the cochlear tube the basilar membrane is wider and floppier; low-frequency sounds such as a bass drum cause maximum vibration here.
      • The brain interprets input from hair cells in different regions as sounds of different pitch.
  • Hearing loss. An estimated 28 million Americans have some degree of hearing loss and 2 million are completely deaf. Hearing loss severe enough to interfere with social and job-related communication is among the most common chronic neural impairments in the United States.
    • Conductive loss — an obstruction anywhere along the route prevents sound being conducted through the external auditory canal to the eardrum and over the middle ear bones to the inner ear.
      • External auditory canal clogged with wax or other foreign matter. Inserting cotton-tipped swabs usually aggravates this by clogging the canal further and should be avoided.
      • Thickening of the eardrum (chronic infection), or perforation of the eardrum (trauma).
      • Excess fluid in the middle ear, common with middle ear infection, prevents eardrum movement and causes temporary conductive loss.
    • Sensorineural deafness — damage to the hair cells or to the nerve supply of the inner ear.
      • A common cause is gradual loss of hair cells throughout life, some simply from aging: after age 20, the loss of hair cells causes us to lose about 1 Hz of our total perceptive range of 20,000 Hz every day.
      • Much of the damage is from exposure to loud noise (condensed under BOXED ESSAYS).
      • Neural damage can also be caused by infections — mumps, rubella (German measles), syphilis, meningitis — and by drugs such as those used for tuberculosis and certain cancers.
      • One of the first signs is the inability to hear high-frequency sounds. Because consonants, which are needed to decipher most words, have higher frequencies than vowels, speech becomes difficult to understand — so beware if you find yourself asking "Could you please repeat that?"
  • Ear infections.
    • External ear infection — an infection in the ear canal. Swimmer's ear, the most common, is precipitated by water trapped in the canal creating an environment favourable to bacterial growth.
      • First symptom is itching, usually followed by pain that can become intense and constant; chewing food or touching the earlobe sharpens the pain.
      • Usual treatment: antibiotic ear drops, heat, pain medication. Prevention: drain remaining water from the ear canal after swimming or bathing, shaking the head if necessary.
    • Middle ear infection (otitis media) — usually results from an infection of the nose and throat that works its way through the auditory tubes.
      • At least half of all children experience one; some have four or five a year. More common in children because the tubes are nearly horizontal, letting infectious organisms travel more easily; in adults the auditory tubes curve and tilt downward.
      • Signs: a stabbing earache, impaired hearing, a feeling of fullness in the ear, often with fever. Usually treated with an antibiotic.

9.6Balance and the Vestibular Apparatus of the Inner Ear

pp. 165
Big ideaThe vestibular apparatus monitors head position and movement with the same hair cells used for hearing — semicircular canals for dynamic equilibrium, utricle and saccule for static equilibrium.

Outline

  • The vestibular apparatus. A fluid-filled maze of chambers and canals within the inner ear, responsible for monitoring the position and movement of the head. SEE FIGURE 9.15
    • Its receptors are hair cells similar to those in the cochlea. Head movements or changes in velocity bend the hairs, sending messages to the brain, which uses the input to maintain balance.
    • Two parts: the semicircular canals and the vestibule.
  • Semicircular canals — dynamic equilibrium. Three canals in each ear that contain sensory receptors and help us stay balanced as we move. They monitor sudden movements of the head, including acceleration and deceleration, maintaining equilibrium when the body or head is moving.
    • The three canals are oriented at right angles to one another. Head movement causes the fluid in the canals to move in the direction opposite the direction of movement — in the same way that sudden acceleration slams passengers back against the car seat.
      • Nodding "yes" swirls fluid in the canals parallel to the sides of the head.
      • Shaking "no" moves fluid in the canals parallel to the horizon.
      • Tilting the head to look under the bed moves fluid in the canals parallel to the face.
      • Even the most complex movement can be analysed as motion in three planes.
    • Ampulla — an enlarged region at the base of each semicircular canal, containing a tuft of hair cells.
    • Cupula — a pointed "cap" of stiff, pliable, gelatinous material in which the hairlike projections of those hair cells are embedded.
      • When you move your head, fluid in the canal lags a little behind, causing the cupula to bend the hair cells and stimulate them. The brain receives the signals and interprets them to maintain balance while moving.
  • Vestibule — static equilibrium. The maintenance of balance when we are not moving. SEE FIGURE 9.15
    • Consists of the utricle and the saccule, two fluid-filled cavities that "let us know which end is up". They tell the brain the position of the head with respect to gravity when the body is not moving. They also respond to acceleration and deceleration, but not to rotational changes as the semicircular canals do.
    • Both contain hair cells overlaid with a gelatinous material to which small chalklike granules of calcium carbonate are bound.
      • Otoliths — those granules; they make the gelatin heavier than the surrounding material so it slides over the hair cells whenever the head moves. The movement of the gelatin stimulates the hair cells, which signal the brain, and the brain determines the position of the head relative to gravity.
    • Utricle vs. saccule — the orientation is the whole distinction.
      • Utricle — hair cells on the floor of the chamber, oriented vertically when the head is upright. Senses forward tilting of the head and forward motion.
      • Saccule — hair cells on the wall of the chamber, oriented horizontally when the head is upright. Responds to vertical movement, as when you jump up and down.
  • Motion sickness. Dizziness and nausea, sometimes with vomiting, thought to result from a mismatch between sensory input from the vestibular apparatus and sensory input from the eyes.
    • Reading in a car: the eyes tell the brain the body is stationary while the vestibular system detects the car changing speed, turning and hitting bumps.
    • Seasickness: the vestibular apparatus reports the head rocking back and forth while the deck underfoot looks level.
    • The brain is confused by the conflicting information. Staring at the horizon, so you can see that you are moving, can sometimes relieve it. Over-the-counter drugs such as Dramamine work by inhibiting the messages from the vestibular apparatus.

9.7Smell and Taste

pp. 167–168
Big ideaSmell and taste are both chemoreceptor senses — odour molecules dissolved in nasal mucus and food molecules dissolved in saliva — and smell supplies most of what we call flavour.

Outline

  • Smell (olfaction). Perhaps the least appreciated sense. You could get along without it, but about 80% of what we usually think of as the flavour of a food is really detected by smell — which is why food tastes bland when the nose is congested. SEE FIGURE 9.16
    • Olfactory receptors are located not in the nostrils but in a small patch of tissue the size of a postage stamp in the roof of each nasal cavity.
    • They are sensory neurons that branch into long olfactory hairs projecting outward from the lining of the nasal cavity, covered by a coat of mucus which keeps them moist and acts as a solvent for odorous molecules. The hairs move, gently swirling the mucus.
    • Olfactory receptors are one of the few kinds of neuron known to be replaced during life — about every 60 days.
    • The pathway. Odour molecules are carried into the nasal cavity in air → dissolve in the mucus → bind to receptor sites on the hairs of the olfactory receptor cells → stimulate the cells → if threshold is reached, the message is carried to the two olfactory bulbs of the brain.
      • The olfactory bulbs process the information and pass it to the limbic system and the cerebral cortex (Chapter 8), where it is interpreted.
      • The limbic system is a centre for emotions and memory — which is why we rarely have neutral responses to odours. The perfume industry makes a fortune from the association between scents and sexuality, and odours can trigger a flood of long-forgotten memories.
    • About 1000 types of olfactory receptor, with which we can distinguish about 10,000 odours. Each receptor responds to several odours, so the brain relies on input from more than one type of receptor to identify an odour.
  • Taste (gustation) — the five primary tastes. Sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami (savoury).
    • These five are enough to answer the important question about food or drink: should we swallow it or spit it out?
      • Sweet — prompts swallowing; implies a rich source of calories and thus energy.
      • Salty — prompts swallowing; salts replace those lost in perspiration.
      • Sour — a dilemma. Sour is the taste of unripe fruit that would have more food value later; as fruit ripens, starches break down into sugars that create sweetness and mask the sourness, so it is often better to reject sour fruit and wait. But some sour fruits — oranges, lemons, tomatoes — are rich in vitamin C, an essential vitamin.
      • Bitter — easy; we generally reject it, because bitterness usually indicates food that is poisonous or spoiled.
      • Umami — prompts swallowing; indicates food high in protein.
    • Umami is savoury or brothy, derived from a Japanese word meaning "delicious". It occurs when amino acids — especially glutamic acid — small proteins and nucleotides (DNA and RNA) stimulate umami receptors. To experience it: boil a cup of water with a tablespoon of dried shitake mushrooms, cool it, take a sip. Other umami foods: chicken broth, beef broth, parmesan cheese.
  • Taste buds. We have about 10,000 taste buds, the structures responsible for the sense of taste. SEE FIGURE 9.17
    • Most are on the tongue, but some are scattered on the inner surface of the cheeks, on the roof of the mouth and in the throat.
    • Most taste buds on the tongue are located in papillae — the small bumps that give the tongue a slightly rough feeling. Each papilla contains 100 to 200 taste buds.
    • The cells of a taste bud are completely replaced about every 10 days, so you need not worry about permanently losing your sense of taste when you burn your mouth on hot pizza.
    • Structure. A taste bud is the meeting point between chemicals dissolved in saliva and the sensory neurons that convey information about them to the brain.
      • A lemon-shaped structure containing about 40 modified epithelial cells — some are taste cells that respond to chemicals, others are supporting cells.
      • Taste cells have long taste hairs projecting into a pore at the tip of the taste bud; the hairs bear the receptors for certain chemicals found in food.
      • Dissolved in saliva, food molecules enter the pore and stimulate the taste hairs.
      • Taste cells are not neurons, but they generate electrical signals that are sent to sensory nerve cells wrapped around the taste cell.
    • Each taste bud responds to all five basic tastes, but a single taste cell responds to only one. Because of the way taste buds are distributed, different regions of the tongue have slightly different sensitivity to these tastes.
  • Looking ahead. The previous chapters showed how neurons and the nervous system create quick responses to stimuli; the next chapter considers the endocrine system, which produces hormones causing slower, more prolonged responses.

Stop And Think

1Most bipolar cells receive input from several rods but from only one cone in the fovea. How might this difference explain why rods permit us to see at lower light intensities than do cones? How might it explain why the sharpest images are formed when the object is focused on the fovea? (§9.4)

Convergence trades acuity for sensitivity, and the retina makes that trade differently in the two systems. Because many rods feed one bipolar cell, their individually weak responses summate — a quantity of light too small to make any single rod fire a message strong enough on its own can, pooled across many rods, push the shared bipolar cell to respond. That is sensitivity bought cheaply. The cost is that the brain can no longer tell which of those rods was hit, so everything the pooled signal reports is spatially smeared. In the fovea the wiring is one cone to one bipolar cell, so there is no pooling: each cone's message arrives separately and the brain can resolve which individual photoreceptor was stimulated. No summation means poor performance in dim light, but it means two points close together stay two points — which is exactly what visual acuity is. Hence sharpest images when the image falls on the fovea.

2As we age, the tissues of the eardrum thicken and become less flexible. How do these changes partially explain why sensitivity to high-frequency sounds is usually lost as we grow older? (§9.5)

The eardrum works by vibrating synchronously with the sound wave that strikes it. High-frequency sound means the pressure wave reverses direction many more times a second, so following it demands that the membrane change direction very rapidly. A thickened, stiffer membrane has more mass and less flexibility, so it responds sluggishly — it can still keep up with the slow reversals of a low-frequency wave but increasingly cannot keep up with fast ones. The high-frequency vibrations are therefore transmitted poorly to the ossicles and on to the oval window, and less of that energy reaches the hair cells. Note the question says partially: stiffening of the eardrum is only one contribution, since loss of hair cells at the base of the cochlea — the region tuned to high frequencies — is the other, and noise damage acts there first.

3When you stop moving, there is a time lag before the fluid in the semicircular canals stops swirling. How does this time lag account for the sensation of dizziness after you have been twirling around for a while? (§9.6)

The semicircular canals do not report rotation directly; they report fluid movement relative to the canal. While you spin, the fluid lags at first, then catches up and turns with you. When you suddenly stop, the canal stops but the fluid's momentum carries it on swirling, so it now moves relative to the stationary canal — in the direction you had been turning. That continued flow keeps pushing the cupula and keeps the hair cells firing, so the canals go on reporting rotation. Meanwhile your eyes and your vestibule (which senses gravity) both report that you are still. The brain receives two contradictory accounts of the same body at the same moment, and the mismatch is experienced as dizziness, lasting until the fluid finally comes to rest.

Environmental Issue

Noise Pollution (§9.5, pp. 164, 166)

  • The damage is mechanical and it is permanent. Loud noise wears down the hairs on the hair cells of the inner ear; they lose their flexibility and can fuse together (Figure 9.A). There is no repair — the book's line is that you cannot grow spare parts for your ears.
  • Scale of the problem: excessive noise is to blame for the hearing loss of one-third of all hearing-impaired people.
  • The decibel scale is logarithmic, and an increase of 10 dB generally makes a sound twice as loud.
  • The thresholds, which are in the prose and not in Table 9.a: most people judge sounds over 60 dB intrusive, over 80 dB annoying, over 100 dB extremely bothersome. OSHA's safety limit is 85 dB for 8 hours of exposure. The threshold for physical pain is 140 dB.
  • Young people are affected, and the likely cause is music.
  • Prevention, the only available remedy: keep music low enough that you can still hear other sounds; with earbuds, nobody else should be able to hear them; wear earplugs for unavoidable loud noise — mowing, vacuuming, concerts.
  • The first sign of sensorineural damage is loss of high-frequency hearing. Because consonants carry higher frequencies than vowels and consonants are what make words decipherable, speech becomes hard to follow before anything else is noticed — hence "Could you please repeat that?" as an early warning.
  • Questions to Consider (printed): Should local governments have more regulations to control noise pollution? Should there be regulations on how loudly concert music can be played?

10A ______ can detect changes in hydrogen ion concentration.

a) thermoreceptor b) chemoreceptor c) proprioceptor d) pain receptor

b

Hydrogen ion concentration is a chemical quantity, and chemoreceptors "respond to chemicals," including those monitoring "levels of specific substances such as carbon dioxide, oxygen, or glucose in our body fluids" — the same internal-monitoring role. (a) thermoreceptors detect temperature. (c) proprioceptors detect body and limb position. (d) pain receptors respond to tissue-damaging stimuli; they are sometimes classed with chemoreceptors because they answer to chemicals released by damaged tissue, which makes (d) the strongest distractor — but detecting a change in pH is monitoring, not injury.

a

(b) limb position is the proprioceptors, Figure 9.3 — the same trap as Q8 run in the opposite direction. (c) and (d) are the wrong sense organs entirely. Applying the Concepts — short answer, p. 170.

12Molly needs glasses for driving but not for reading. What is the name for her visual problem? Describe the shape of the lens she needs to correct her vision.

Nearsightedness (myopia).

Reading is close work and driving is distance work, so she sees near objects clearly and distant ones blurred — that is myopia by Table 9.2's definition. The cause is an eyeball too long or a lens too thick, so the image focuses in front of the retina. The correction is a concave lens — thinner in the middle than at the edges — which diverges the rays slightly before they enter the eye so the image falls back onto the retina (Figure 9.8c).

13Sarah has a viral infection that is causing vertigo (dizziness). In which part of the ear is the infection?

The inner ear — specifically the vestibular apparatus.

Balance is controlled by the vestibular apparatus, the semicircular canals and vestibule, which sit in the inner ear (Table 9.3, Figure 9.13). Disturbance of the hair cells there makes the canals report rotation that is not happening, and the mismatch with vision is felt as vertigo. The outer and middle ear are concerned with conducting and amplifying sound and have no role in equilibrium.

14You are at a baseball game. After watching the ball fly into the stands for a home run, you turn to a friend sitting beside you to ask for some peanuts. What changes occur in your eyes for your friend's face to come into focus?

Accommodation — the lens rounds up — together with pupillary constriction.

Your eyes were set for a distant object, so the ciliary muscle was relaxed, its diameter wide, the ligaments taut and the lens flattened. Shifting to a face an arm's length away reverses all three: the ciliary muscle contracts, which reduces the ring's diameter the way pursed lips do, this loosens the tension on the ligaments, and the freed lens becomes rounder — the greater curvature bends the strongly divergent rays from the near object enough to focus them on the retina (Figure 9.6). The pupils also constrict, which increases depth of field. Finding and Evaluating Information — p. 170. Based on University of Chicago News coverage of a study reporting that, in older adults, failure of a simple five-odour identification test (peppermint, fish, orange, rose, leather, in order of increasing difficulty) predicted death within five years: 39% of those who failed died in that period, against 19% with moderate smell loss and 10% with a healthy sense of smell; the association held after adjustment for age, gender, socioeconomic status, overall health and race. Lead author Jayant M. Pinto describes smell loss as "like the canary in the coal mine" — not a cause of death but a harbinger.

15Develop some hypotheses to explain the possible causes for a relationship between loss of smell and death within 5 years.

Several are consistent with the chapter and with the quoted article. (i) Olfactory receptors are neurons that are replaced roughly every 60 days — one of the few nerve cell populations that regenerate throughout life — so a failure of smell may be an early readout of a general collapse in the body's capacity to regenerate tissue. (ii) Because the olfactory nerve is a direct route from the environment into the brain, smell loss may be an early sign of neurodegenerative disease that is not yet otherwise detectable. (iii) Smell loss may be cumulative damage from toxic or environmental exposure, so it marks a life history of exposure rather than a disease. (iv) It may act causally but weakly, through appetite: taste depends heavily on smell, so losing it can reduce food intake and lead to malnutrition. The article's own framing favours the harbinger readings over the causal one.

16Does the loss of smell predict death within 5 years? Should a 60-year-old man who can identify only one of the scents assume that he will die soon?

No, he should not. The finding is a population-level association, not an individual prognosis, and three things limit what it can say about one person. First, it is a correlation, and the author explicitly denies causation — smell loss "doesn't directly cause death." Second, the base rates cut against the inference: 39% of those who failed the test died, which means 61% did not — failing is not even a majority-odds predictor. Third, the study was of older adults, and a 60-year-old may sit outside the age range from which the statistic was derived; his smell loss may equally have a mundane, treatable cause such as sinus disease, nasal polyps or a past infection. The sound reading is that the finding identifies a group worth investigating further — which is how Pinto proposes using it, as a quick and inexpensive clinical test to flag patients at risk — not a verdict on any individual.

17Other studies have implied that dogs can detect a scent associated with cancer. Use reliable sources to find research on this subject. What were the methods, results, and conclusions? Were the conclusions valid given the methods and results?

An open research exercise — no answer is printed in the book, and none is supplied here. What the question is testing is the evaluation, so the things to check in whatever study is found: whether the dogs' handlers were blinded to which sample was which (unblinded handlers can cue a dog unconsciously — the Clever Hans problem, and it is the single commonest flaw in this literature); how many samples and how many dogs, since small numbers inflate apparent accuracy; whether sensitivity and specificity are both reported, rather than only the hit rate; whether the positive and negative samples differed in any way besides the cancer — storage, handling, collection site — that the dog could be keying on instead; and whether the result was tested on a fresh sample set rather than the one the dogs were trained on.

Write

What counts as a general sense

  • Everything that is not a special sense — i.e. not vision, hearing, vestibular sensation, gustation or olfaction. Includes both somatic and visceral senses.
  • Sensation = detection of environmental stimuli. Many stimuli → many receptor types, classified by the stimulus they detect.

Three receptor cell types

  • Free nerve endings — dendrites embedded in tissue.
  • Encapsulated nerve endings — wrapped in connective tissue that enhances sensitivity to the stimulus.
  • Specialized receptor cell — a separate cell (e.g. a rod) that releases neurotransmitter onto a bipolar cell.
  • The split that matters: the special senses — except smell — use a separate receptor cell; most general senses use a modified axon terminal of a sensory neuron.

Transduction

  • Transduction = stimulus → action potential. In the general senses it happens at the receptor.
  • Stimulus → ion channels open → membrane potential changes → AP propagated along the sensory neuron = transmission. The transmission pathway is the neurons and synapses involved.

Touch receptors of the skin — all mechanoreceptors

  • Merkel cell — light touch, fine spatial resolution; in the stratum basale, sensory neurons (Merkel fibers) adjacent. Abundant in the hands and feet — the Braille receptor.
  • Meissner's corpuscle — dermal papillae, encapsulated. Light touch, but less differentiation of detail than Merkel.
  • Pacinian corpuscle — deep dermis, encapsulated, capsule in layers → lamellated corpuscle; an onion in cross-section.
  • Ruffini corpuscle — deep dermis + hypodermis, encapsulated stretch receptor → proprioceptive (body position) info.
  • Free nerve endings around hair follicles → hair movement. Nociceptors and thermoreceptors = free nerve endings in the skin → pain and temperature.

Write

Three layers (tunics)

  • Fibrous tunic (outer) — sclera (white of the eye) + cornea (clear, curved, anterior).
  • Vascular tunic (middle) — the iris, ciliary body and choroid.
    • Iris — two smooth muscles → regulate pupil size, and so the amount of light entering.
    • Ciliary muscle attaches to the fibres supporting the lens → regulates lens shape for near or far focus.
    • Choroid — blood vessels + pigment that absorbs light, preventing scatter.
  • Nervous tunic (inner) — the retina.

Retina

  • Inner-most → outer-most: ganglion → bipolar → photoreceptors, the photoreceptors against the pigmented epithelium. So light passes the ganglion and bipolar layers before reaching the rods and cones.
  • Macula — retina directly opposite the cornea; holds the fovea — highest concentration of cones, best visual detail. Only cones in the fovea.
  • Optic disc — no photoreceptors → no signal to the cortex → the blind spot. Where ganglion axons exit as the optic nerve and blood vessels enter and leave.

Path of light

  • Cornea — curved shape refracts light, directing it toward the fovea.
  • Anterior chamber / aqueous humor — clear fluid; supplies nutrition, carries away wastes, circulating the anterior cavity.
  • Pupil → lens. The lens refracts further to aim light at the fovea.
  • Close object → light enters at a more severe angle → lens more convex, more refraction. Distant object → rays nearly parallel, little refraction → ciliary muscle relaxes, pulling the lens flatter.
  • Posterior cavity / vitreous humor — clear, jelly-like: passes light, shapes the eye, keeps the retina pressed to the back.

Transduction

  • Light hits the photoreceptor → pigment changes conformation → cascade → hyperpolarization → stops release of the inhibitory neurotransmitter.
  • Bipolar cells, no longer inhibited, fire and signal the ganglion cells → AP in the ganglion cell → primary visual cortex, occipital lobe.

Rods vs cones

  • Rods — detect low amounts of light and do not respond to specific colours. Outer segment is long and columnar, with stacks of membrane-bound discs containing rhodopsin.
  • Cones — respond to specific wavelengths of light. Outer segment is short and tapered, with folds of membrane in place of the discs.
  • Both share the same plan: an inner segment holding the nucleus and organelles, and an outer segment of membrane arrays carrying the photosensitive opsin.

Outer ear — the collector

  • Pinna (auricle) — shaped somewhat like a satellite dish; funnels sound waves into the auditory canal toward the tympanic membrane.
  • Tympanic membrane — separates the outer ear from the middle ear and vibrates when sound waves reach it.

Middle ear — the amplifier

  • Three auditory ossicles: malleus (hammer) → incus (anvil) → stapes (stirrup). The malleus is attached to the tympanic membrane and to the incus, so when the membrane moves it moves the malleus, which moves the incus, which moves the stapes.
  • The stapes sits against the oval window, the membrane covering the opening into a fluid-filled chamber of the cochlea. Stapes vibrates → oval window moves → fluid wave inside the cochlea.
  • Most of the middle ear is surrounded by the petrous portion of the temporal bone. The exceptions are the tympanic membrane, the oval window, the round window (a smaller membrane over another opening into the inner ear) and the Eustachian tube.
  • Eustachian (auditory) tube — connects the pharynx to the middle ear chamber. Very important for equalizing pressure between the middle ear and the atmosphere.

Inner ear — the transducer

  • The fluid wave begins in the scala vestibuli and moves the perilymph, making the basilar membrane — the floor of the cochlear duct — undulate.
  • Hair cells sit on top of the basilar membrane. The undulation pushes them up into the tectorial membrane, bending them → neurotransmitter released → sensory neurons fire.
  • The AP is carried along the cochlear nerve (VIII) → primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. The fluid wave itself continues on to the round window.

How pitch and loudness are coded

  • The basilar membrane is thicker and narrower at the base (near the middle ear) and thinner and wider at the apex. That variation is what allows different frequencies to be detected.
  • High frequencies vibrate the region near the base; low frequencies vibrate toward the apex — so only hair cells in one specific region respond to a given frequency. The figure prints 20,000 Hz at the base · 1500 Hz in the middle · 20 Hz at the apex.
  • Frequency → pitch. Higher frequency is perceived as higher pitch.
  • Amplitude (height of the wave) → loudness. A larger amplitude bends more hair cells, sending more action potentials to the cerebral cortex.

Write

What it is

  • Vestibular apparatus = the semicircular canals + the vestibule.

Semicircular canals — rotation

  • Three fluid-filled canals in different orientations. At the base of each, the ampulla — an enlarged space holding the cupula, a gelatinous, cone-shaped structure with hair cells at its base.
  • Head rotates → fluid moves → cupula bends → hair cells bend.
  • The direction of the bend decides whether neurotransmitter release — and so APs along the vestibular nerve (CN VIII) — increases or decreases.
  • As a canal swings with the head, the fluid inside moves the opposite way — that lag bends the cupula and stereocilia.
  • Two canals in a plane give direction; all six give head movement in three dimensions.
  • The canals report rotational movement — a type of dynamic equilibrium.

Vestibule — head position and linear acceleration

  • Utricle and saccule, both fluid-filled — but here the fluid does not move the hair cells. That is the difference from the canals.
  • Within each is the macula: a gelatinous membrane with hair cells embedded at its base and crystals (otoliths) on its surface.
  • Maculae face different directions — upright on the floor of the utricle, on the sides of the saccule.
  • Head shifts → the weight of the otoliths pulls the gel and hair cells in the direction of gravity, bending them. As in the canals, the direction of the bend sets the neurotransmitter released and the APs sent on for integration.
  • The vestibule detects head position when stationary — and it also detects linear acceleration. The mechanism is inertia: the stereocilia and the otolithic membrane they are embedded in move differently, and the resulting shearing force bends the stereocilia in the direction of the acceleration.

What it is

  • Olfaction = the sensation of smell, and like gustation it relies on chemoreceptors.
  • Inhaled chemicals — odorants — bind to chemoreceptors present on the dendrites of the olfactory neurons themselves.
  • This is unique to olfaction. All other special senses have separate receptor cells for transduction — this is the single cleanest contrast in the lab, and it is worth stating in exactly those terms.

Pathway

  • The AP is transmitted along the olfactory neuron through the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone, into the cranial vault.
  • The olfactory neuron synapses in the olfactory bulb, which transmits the AP to the primary olfactory cortex in the temporal lobe.

Smell and taste together

  • Olfaction is associated with taste. The same chemical in the food may become aerosolized and bind chemoreceptors in the nasal epithelium.
  • Like gustation, olfactory neurons are replaced throughout life — but replacement decreases later in life, and sensitivity to smell decreases as well.
