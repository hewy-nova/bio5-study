L1.2Managing diabetes: lifestyle, monitoring, medication

4 slides
Big ideaType 2 is prevented or delayed by lifestyle: diet, exercise, weight loss, tight control of glucose and blood pressure. Type 1 cannot be prevented or cured yet; research aims to replace the destroyed beta cells.
  • Diet: whole, high-nutrition, less processed food; fruit as the treat.
    • Portion size matters — the same calories buy far more of whole foods than of a half-cup serving of cereal.
    • To lose weight you need a calorie deficit; to gain, more calories in than you use.
  • Exercise raises the body's calorie demand, so it offsets some of what you eat.
  • Monitoring:
    • a glucometer, or a continuous glucose monitor worn in the arm — no finger-pricks, real-time readings of how food and exercise change blood glucose;
    • hemoglobin A1c — shows the hemoglobin has been exposed to high glucose over a long period. Checked every few months: change a treatment, then reassess in 3 or 6 months. "At least twice a year, 4 times a year is even better."
  • Antidiabetic medication for type 2 and prediabetics — e.g. metformin (Glucophage) — to minimise long-term complications such as cardiovascular disease, when diet and exercise advice has not worked.
  • Type 1: "Nothing we can do to cure that."
    • Autoimmune; people develop it and "we don't know why."
    • Research is trying stem-cell transplants to restore the beta cells of the pancreatic islets, which make and secrete insulin.
    • Strict glucose control — diet, exercise, medication — can delay or prevent long-term complications.
  • Type 2: lifestyle intervention — weight loss and exercise, with strict control of blood glucose and blood pressure. Small changes become habits and are easier to build on.
    • Repeating a parent's lifestyle repeats their outcome — heart attack, stroke, diabetes.

Key terms

Hemoglobin A1c
glucose-exposed hemoglobin; tracks long-term blood glucose, checked every few months.
Continuous glucose monitor
Lecture a sensor worn in the arm giving real-time glucose readings without finger-pricks.
Metformin (Glucophage)
Lecture antidiabetic drug for type 2 and prediabetes.

L1.3Gestational diabetes and big babies

4 slides
Big ideaPlacental hormones make the mother's cells insulin resistant. Her glucose crosses the placenta but her insulin does not, so from about three months the fetal pancreas overproduces insulin and the fetus overgrows — macrosomia, over 9 pounds.
  • About 7% of pregnancies.
  • Cause: hormones from the placenta make the mother's cells more resistant to insulin, and her insulin production is insufficient to overcome it.
  • It typically goes away after delivery.
  • Consequences for the mother:
    • possible preeclampsia — life-threatening high blood pressure and fluid retention;
    • difficult deliveries from large babies — vaginal or by cesarean.
  • Consequences for the baby:
    • high maternal glucose passes to the fetus through the placenta; maternal insulin cannot cross the placenta, so the baby has to make its own;
    • at about 3 months' gestation the fetal pancreas begins producing insulin, responds to the high glucose and produces excess insulin;
    • glucose moves into fetal cells → excessive growth of the fetus — macrosomia, birth weight over 9 pounds.
    • His relative-by-marriage had four babies, the smallest 9 lb and the others about 11 and 13 lb — probably uncontrolled gestational diabetes.
  • In passing: placenta previa — the placenta forms too low, too close to the cervical opening; it can mean bed rest, reclined, so the baby is not pressing on its own lifeline.

Key terms

Gestational diabetes
diabetes in pregnancy from placental hormones causing insulin resistance; usually resolves after delivery.
Preeclampsia
life-threatening high blood pressure and fluid retention in pregnancy.
Macrosomia
excessive fetal growth — birth weight over 9 pounds.
Placenta previa
Lecture a placenta that forms too close to the cervical opening.
Exam tip

The chain is the answer: maternal glucose crosses, maternal insulin does not. The fetal pancreas makes the extra insulin, and the insulin drives the growth.

L1.4The thymus: T-cell university

1 slides
Big ideaThe thymus secretes thymopoietin and thymosin, which promote the maturation of T lymphocytes. It is large in childhood and shrinks with age.
  • Location: "just above and anterior to your heart… it looks like it's straddling your heart." The deck says superior to the heart; the textbook says behind the breastbone, on top of the heart — the same place.
  • Hormones: thymopoietin and thymosin — promote the maturation of T lymphocytes.
  • Size: very large in childhood, much smaller by middle age, a fraction of its size by your 70s or 80s — by then it has done its job.
  • "T-cell university" — T cells go there to learn to tell self from not-self.
    • Only about 5% graduate; the other 95% are killed by other immune cells if they fail even one test.
    • The stakes: a T cell that gets out without passing can attack the body it is meant to protect — that is how we get autoimmune diseases.
  • More on the thymus with the immune system later in the course.

Key terms

Thymopoietin / thymosin
thymus hormones that promote T-lymphocyte maturation.

L1.5The pineal gland: melatonin, SAD and circadian rhythm

2 slides
Big ideaThe pineal gland makes melatonin from serotonin. Light inhibits melatonin, so it rises at night. Too much melatonin — in winter's short days — is linked to seasonal affective disorder, treated with bright light.
  • Melatonin is made from serotonin — the "happy chemical" from the nervous system. Making more melatonin means less serotonin.
  • Light inhibits melatonin secretion. The pineal gland gets a lot of input from the visual pathways; as light falls at night, melatonin rises.
  • Melatonin's effects: promotes sleep, reduces jet lag, and is "thought to possibly help slow aging."
  • Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) — from too much melatonin, so less serotonin. Associated with winter and short days; overcast northern cities are notoriously more depressed.
  • Treatment: repeated exposure to very bright light, which inhibits melatonin secretion — though it may disturb sleep.
  • Circadian rhythm — the pineal gland helps control when you should be awake and when asleep. With no alarms on the weekend, the time you wake is probably your own rhythm.

Key terms

Melatonin
pineal hormone made from serotonin; inhibited by light, higher at night; promotes sleep.
Seasonal affective disorder (SAD)
winter depression from too much melatonin; treated with bright light.
Circadian rhythm
the daily sleep–wake cycle the pineal gland helps set.
Exam tip

The serotonin link is his, not the book's. The textbook and deck say SAD comes from too much melatonin. He adds why it lowers mood: melatonin is made from serotonin, so more melatonin leaves less serotonin. The two agree; his is one step further.

L1.6Local signalling molecules: growth factors, nitric oxide, prostaglandins

4 slides
Big ideaLocal signalling molecules act very quickly because they only go next door. Growth factors make cells grow and divide; nitric oxide is a gas that dilates vessels; prostaglandins are lipids with very diverse effects.
  • They act very quickly on adjacent cells — "they ain't going very far." Neurotransmitters are one kind.
  • Growth factors — stimulate cells to grow, develop and multiply.
  • Nitric oxide (NO) — a gas that:
    • dilates blood vessels;
    • may aid peristalsis;
    • acts as a neurotransmitter.
    • His example: a drug on this vessel-dilating pathway, given for the lungs, dilated blood vessels throughout the body. It was pulled, then remarketed as Viagra — a side effect turned into the main use.
  • Prostaglandins — lipid molecules continually released by plasma membranes; about 16 different ones.
  • Their effects are very diverse:
    • reproductive system — e.g. menstrual cramps;
    • the inflammatory response — the four signs: redness, swelling, heat and pain;
    • blood clotting;
    • body temperature.

Key terms

Local signalling molecule
acts quickly on adjacent cells — neurotransmitters, growth factors, NO, prostaglandins.
Nitric oxide (NO)
a gas; dilates vessels, may aid peristalsis, acts as a neurotransmitter.
Prostaglandins
lipids continually released by plasma membranes; ~16 kinds; reproduction, inflammation, clotting, body temperature.
Exam tip

Prostaglandins in brief. He named them and their four areas and said the detail comes in Bio 22. Know lipid, ~16 kinds, local and fast, and the four areas.
