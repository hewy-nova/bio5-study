1Lab 12Activity 1 · Hematocrit
  • lab manual
  • textbook

You spin a capillary tube of blood and see a thin whitish band between the two main layers. What is that band?

Answer: b

Buffy coat — white blood cells plus platelets, about 1 mm, under 1% of the column. Plasma (a) is the straw-coloured top layer, about 55%; the packed RBCs (c) sit at the bottom, about 45%. The anticoagulant is there to stop clotting, so there is no clot (d).

2Lab 12Activity 1 · Hematocrit
  • lab manual

A blood column measures 50 mm in total and its red layer measures 21 mm. Using the lab's formula, what is the hematocrit?

Answer: c

HCT = (RBC volume ÷ total blood volume) × 100% = 21 ÷ 50 × 100 = 42%. Option a reads the RBC height as if it were a percentage; 29 mm (b) is the plasma height, not a percentage; 58% (d) is the plasma share. 42% sits in both the lab's female (37–47%) and male (42–52%) ranges.

3Lab 12Activity 1 · Hematocrit
  • lab manual
  • textbook

In a normal spun sample, what share of the total blood volume is plasma?

Answer: b

Plasma about 55%, RBCs about 45% (the hematocrit), buffy coat under 1%. 93% (d) is the share of plasma itself that is water, per Whalen's Ch. 11 deck — a different number.

4Lab 12Activity 1 · Blood componentsselect all that apply
  • lab manual

Which statements about the components of blood are true? Select all that apply.

Answer: a, c, d, e

The lab's own summary question. Plasma carries electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, gases and wastes (a). The buffy coat is WBCs + platelets, and it — not the RBCs — is the smallest layer, under 1% (so b is false: RBCs are about 45%). Low HCT = anemia (d); high HCT = polycythemia, but dehydration falsely raises it and is the most common cause of a high reading (e).

5Lab 12Activity 1 · Hematocrit
  • lab manual

After a day of vomiting with no fluids, a patient's hematocrit reads 56%. What does the lab say is the most likely explanation?

Answer: b

Plasma is the difference between RBC volume and total volume, so losing plasma water raises the HCT without adding a single red cell — the lab calls dehydration the most common cause of a high HCT. True polycythemia (a) is a real excess of RBCs. Anemia (c) lowers HCT; fluid overload (d) also lowers it.

The largest white cell on the smear has a kidney-shaped nucleus and no visible granules. What does it become once it leaves the blood?

Answer: b

That is a monocyte, an agranulocyte; in tissue it becomes a macrophage — a phagocyte that secretes cytokines and acts as an APC. Plasma cells (a) come from B lymphocytes. Mast cells (c) are tissue cells related to basophils. Megakaryocytes (d) are marrow cells that fragment into platelets.

12Lab 12Activity 2 · Blood cells
  • lab manual
  • textbook

The lab asks which blood cells are phagocytic. Which set does it accept?

Answer: b

The lab's phagocytes are neutrophils, eosinophils and macrophages (monocytes become macrophages; the glossary lists monocytes as phagocytes). Basophils, like mast cells, are not phagocytic — they release histamine, which is why the lab's own select-all item "basophils, neutrophils and macrophages are all phagocytic" is false (a). Sources differ on eosinophils: the lab and Whalen's deck count them as phagocytes; the Ch. 11 table has them engulf antibody–antigen complexes; but the §13.2 text says they attack pathogens "too big to engulf" and Table 13.1 lists them apart from the phagocytes. For the lab exam, include them.

  • lab manual

Which statements about blood cells are true? Select all that apply.

Answer: a, b, e

The lab's own summary question. RBCs carry O2 and about 23% of the CO2 (a). Neutrophils are the most numerous WBC (b). Basophils are not phagocytic (c). 14Lab 12Activity 2 · Cell definitions

In the lab's cell-definition table, which cell "displays antigens to activate T cells"?

Answer: d

Dendritic cell — a phagocyte that displays antigen on its MHC proteins; the glossary calls dendritic cells the most common APC. Macrophages and B cells can present too. Mast cells release histamine; NK cells kill infected and abnormal cells; plasma cells make antibodies.

15Lab 12Activity 2 · Cell definitions
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Both cells release histamine. Which is the one that sits in the tissues, rather than circulating in the blood?

Answer: b

The lab's table pairs them: basophil — releases histamine, circulates in the bloodstream; mast cell — releases histamine, located in the tissue. The textbook calls mast cells small, mobile connective-tissue cells. Eosinophils target parasites; neutrophils are the first-responding phagocytes.

16Lab 12Activity 2 · Cell definitions
  • lab manual
  • textbook

A virus-infected cell is killed within hours of infection, before any antigen-specific lymphocyte has been activated. Which cell most likely killed it?

Answer: c

NK cells are innate and non-specific — they look for any suspicious cell (infected, cancerous) and kill it with perforins + granzymes, triggering apoptosis. Cytotoxic T cells (a) kill the same targets, but only after antigen-specific activation, which takes days. Helper T cells activate; plasma cells make antibodies.

17Lab 12Activity 2 · Adaptive cells
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Which adaptive-immunity cell activates both B cells and cytotoxic T cells?

Answer: d

Helper T — the switch for both branches. In the lab's terms, effector helper T cells release IL-2 (activating cytotoxic T) and IL-2, IL-4, IL-5 (fully activating a B cell). Memory B cells wait for a repeat exposure; plasma cells make antibodies; NK cells are innate.

18Lab 12Activity 2 · Adaptive cells
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Why is the response to a second exposure to the same pathogen faster and bigger than the first?

Answer: a

Memory B and T cells remain after the first infection; they are far more numerous than the original naive cells and each clones into new effector and memory cells. Effector cells live only days (c is backwards), so plasma cells (b) and their antibodies (d) fall once the invader is gone.

A typing card shows agglutination in the anti-B well only — none in the anti-A or anti-D wells. What is the blood type?

Clumping = that antigen is present. Anti-B clumps → B antigen; anti-A clear → no A; anti-D clear → no Rh, so Rh−. B+ (a) would also clump in anti-D; AB− (c) would clump in anti-A too.

Where are mucous membranes found, and how do they stop pathogens?

Which statement correctly distinguishes an antigen from an antibody?

True or false, from the lab: "Antigens are found on pathogens and are not on human body cells."

35Lab 12Activity 4 · Cell-mediated vs humoral

A bacterial toxin is circulating free in the plasma. Which branch of the adaptive response mainly deals with it?

1Lab 12short answerActivity 1 · Hematocrit
  • lab manual

Describe the hematocrit procedure, label the spun tube, and calculate the HCT for a 48 mm column with a 20 mm red layer. Is the result normal?

Model answer

Blood is drawn into a capillary tube with anticoagulant, capped, and centrifuged; the heaviest parts are pushed to the bottom. Tube, top to bottom: plasma (straw-coloured, about 55%) · buffy coat (WBCs + platelets, under 1%) · packed RBCs (about 45%). HCT = (RBC volume ÷ total blood volume) × 100% = 20 ÷ 48 × 100 = 41.7%. Normal is female 37–47%, male 42–52%: normal for a woman, just under the male range. Low HCT suggests anemia; high suggests polycythemia, or dehydration, which falsely raises it.

Marks for the three layers in order, the formula, the arithmetic, and reading the result against the ranges. The lab's calculation question; the reason males run higher is asked by the lab but given by no course source.

2Lab 12short answerActivity 1 · Blood components
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Complete the lab's table: for plasma, the buffy coat and the erythrocytes, give the components and the functions.

Model answer

Plasma — water carrying electrolytes, glucose and amino acids, hormones, dissolved gases, wastes (urea), and plasma proteins: albumin (fluid balance), globulins (α, β transport; γ antibodies), fibrinogen (clotting). Function: transport. Buffy coat — leukocytes (defense against pathogens and abnormal cells) and platelets (fragments of megakaryocytes; clotting, hemostasis). Erythrocytes — biconcave, no nucleus, full of hemoglobin (4 O2 per molecule); carry O2 and CO2, live about 120 days.

One mark per portion for components and one for function. The plasma proteins with their three jobs are where most points are lost.

3Lab 12short answerActivity 2 · Adaptive cells
  • lab manual
  • textbook

List and describe the functions of the cell types involved in adaptive immunity.

Model answer

B lymphocytes — receptors for one antigen; when activated, clone into plasma cells (make antibodies at once — humoral) and memory B cells. They can also act as APCs. Helper T (CD4) — recognize antigen on MHC II of an APC, then release cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, IL-5) that activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells. Cytotoxic T (CD8) — recognize antigen on MHC I of infected or abnormal cells and kill them with perforins and granzymes (apoptosis) — cell-mediated. Memory cells (B and T) — long-lived; give a faster, bigger secondary response.

A mark per cell type with its job. The lab's Activity 2 question; the MHC and CD details are the lab's own.

4Lab 12short answerActivity 3 · Rh vs ABO antibodies
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Explain how Rh antibody formation differs from A or B antibody formation, and why it matters in pregnancy.

Model answer

Anti-A and anti-B are present without exposure — the lab says formed before birth, as large IgM; a person has antibodies to whichever ABO antigen they lack. Anti-Rh forms only after an Rh− person is exposed to Rh antigen (a transfusion, or fetal Rh+ cells entering at delivery or miscarriage), and it is a small IgG that crosses the placenta. So a first exposure does no harm, but in a later Rh+ pregnancy the mother's anti-Rh crosses and destroys the fetus's red cells — hemolytic disease of the newborn. RhoGAM prevents it.

The lab objective, verbatim. Marks for: no exposure vs exposure, IgM vs IgG, the placenta. Note the lab's own table lists anti-Rh for every Rh− type, which contradicts its text and Ch. 11; the explanation above is the text's.

Explain why a patient with O− blood can donate whole blood to all blood types but can receive blood only from O−.

  • textbook

What causes inflammation? Explain why it is characterized by redness, swelling, pain and warmth.

Any cell injury or infection: damaged cells and mast cells (and macrophages) release histamine, with serotonin and bradykinins. Redness — histamine causes vasodilation, so more blood flows in. Sketch an ECG, label the P wave, QRS complex and T wave, and say what is happening during each.

4Lab 13short answerActivity 3 · Cardiac cycle
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Describe the cardiac cycle. What is occurring during atrial diastole and ventricular systole?

Model answer

The cardiac cycle is the contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole) of the chambers in one heartbeat; both sides go through each phase together, and pressure differences open and close every valve (blood flows high → low). (1) Atrial and ventricular diastole — atrial pressure is higher, so the AV valves are open and both fill; the SL valves are closed. (2) Atrial systole, ventricular diastole — the atria contract and top up the ventricles. (3) Atrial diastole, ventricular systole — ventricular pressure closes the AV valves (first sound, "lub"); all valves are shut briefly (isovolumetric contraction); then ventricular pressure exceeds the great vessels', the SL valves open and blood is ejected into the pulmonary trunk and aorta, while the relaxed atria refill. (4) Back to diastole — ventricular pressure drops, back-flowing blood closes the SL valves (second sound, "dup"); all valves shut (isovolumetric relaxation) until the AV valves open again.

The two printed Activity 3 questions. Full marks need the phase names, the valve states with the pressure reason, and both heart sounds. The textbook starts the cycle at early diastole and does not name the isovolumetric phases.

5Lab 13short answerActivity 5 · Blood pressure
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Define blood pressure. Explain why systolic is higher than diastolic, and why diastolic pressure is never 0 mm Hg.

Model answer

Blood pressure is the force blood exerts against the walls of the vessels — in a reading, the systemic arterial pressure, written systolic/diastolic. Systolic is the peak, during ventricular systole, when the ventricles force blood into the arteries; diastolic is the lowest, during ventricular diastole, when they relax. It never reaches 0 because the arteries are full of blood and their elastic walls, stretched by each beat, recoil between beats, keeping pressure and flow steady until the next contraction. Normal (lab): systolic 120 or less, diastolic 80 or less.

The Activity 4 objective (define BP and normal values) and two printed Activity 5 questions. Marks for the definition, the systole/diastole link and elastic recoil.

You exercise for a minute. Predict what happens to heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output and blood pressure, and explain why, using the lab's account.

Model answer

Exercise raises the energy need, so the sympathetic nervous system is stimulated. Describe the three functions of the nephron and where each takes place.

4Lab 15short answerActivity 3 · Water balance hormones
  • lab manual
  • textbook

Explain how excessive sweating and water loss would affect urine output. Be specific about the stimuli, the hormones involved and their actions.

Model answer

Sweating loses water and salt, so the blood becomes more concentrated and blood volume and pressure fall. (1) Concentrated blood → the posterior pituitary releases ADH (and thirst rises) → aquaporins in the DCT and collecting duct → more water reabsorbed. (2) Low pressure at the afferent arteriole → the JGA releases renin → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → (ACE) angiotensin II, which constricts vessels and triggers aldosterone, ADH and thirst. (3) Aldosterone makes the DCT and collecting duct reabsorb Na⁺, and water follows. (4) ANP is not released (it answers high volume). Result: less urine, concentrated and darker, with a higher specific gravity; blood volume and pressure rise back toward normal.
