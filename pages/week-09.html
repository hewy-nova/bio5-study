Did you know

  • There are more neurons in the human brain than stars in the Milky Way.
  • It is possible to fit 30,000 neurons on the head of a pin.
  • A "typical" neuron has 1000 to 10,000 synapses that communicate with other neurons.

OPENING

Chapter 6 covered how muscles contract when they receive messages from nerve cells. This chapter is about the nerve cells themselves — how they communicate with muscle cells and with one another. Chapter 8 then covers the parts and functions of the brain and spinal cord. So Ch. 7 is deliberately the cell-level chapter: one neuron, one impulse, one synapse. The anatomy of the system is held back for next week. (p. 115)

The chapter as one chain

  • The nervous system is built from exactly two cell types — neurons, which are excitable and carry messages, and neuroglial cells, which outnumber them 10 to 1 and support them. Everything in the chapter is a property of one or the other.
  • Neurons come in three functional kinds in a fixed order — sensory in, interneuron decides, motor out. That three-step chain is the skeleton the whole nervous system hangs on, and it is why ALS (motor neurons only) spares awareness.
  • One neuron's shape is its job — dendrites and cell body receive, axon sends. The receiving end is many and short; the sending end is one and long.
  • Wrapping the axon in myelin makes it fast — the sheath forces the impulse to jump node to node (saltatory conduction, up to 100× faster). This one structural fact explains both why long axons are myelinated and why multiple sclerosis is so destructive.
  • The impulse is ions crossing a membrane, not electricity in a wire — a resting potential maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, then Na⁺ in (depolarization), then K⁺ out (repolarization). Everything about the action potential follows from which gate is open.
  • The impulse is all-or-nothing and one-way — the refractory period is what makes stimulus strength encode as impulse frequency rather than impulse size, and what keeps the wave from running backward.
  • At the end of the axon the signal changes medium — electrochemical becomes chemical. A neurotransmitter crosses the cleft, binds a receptor, and opens an ion channel. The receptor, not the transmitter, decides whether the effect is excitatory or inhibitory.
  • Every neuron is summing thousands of these at once, and the net of excitatory and inhibitory input decides whether it fires — which is also why small changes in neurotransmitter level produce Alzheimer's, depression and Parkinson's.

7.1Cells of the Nervous System

pp. 116
Big ideaThe nervous system is made of just two cell types — neurons, which generate and transmit messages, and neuroglial cells, which support them — and neurons sort into three functional categories that form a sensory → interneuron → motor chain.

Outline

  • Function of the nervous system. To integrate and coordinate body activities. Lecture The deck opens with this; the chapter text never states the function outright, and it is the study guide's first objective.
  • The two primary divisions (named here, deferred to Ch. 8).
    • Central nervous system (CNS) — brain and spinal cord.
    • Peripheral nervous system (PNS) — all nervous tissue in the body outside the brain and spinal cord.
    • Both divisions are composed of the same two specialized cell types.
  • Neurons (nerve cells). Excitable cells that generate and transmit messages.
  • Neuroglial cells (glial cells). Support and protect neurons. They outnumber neurons by about 10 to 1.
    • Several types, each with a different job.
    • Some provide structural support for neurons of the brain and spinal cord.
    • Some supply nerve growth factors — chemicals that stimulate nerve growth. Without nerve growth factors, neurons die.
    • Some form insulating sheaths (myelin sheaths) around axons — the functions of the sheath are held until §7.2.
    • Glial cells can communicate with one another and with neurons — a comparatively recent finding, stated plainly here.
  • The three categories of neuron. The chapter's organizing chain.
    • Sensory (afferent) neurons conduct information toward the brain and spinal cord. They generally extend from sensory receptors — structures specialized to gather information about conditions within and around the body.
    • Motor (efferent) neurons carry information away from the brain and spinal cord to an effector — either a muscle (which contracts) or a gland (which secretes) — as a response to information from a sensory neuron or interneuron.
    • Association neurons (interneurons) sit between sensory and motor neurons. Found only within the brain and spinal cord, where they integrate and interpret sensory signals and thereby "decide" the appropriate response.
      • Interneurons are by far the most numerous nerve cells in the body — more than 99% of the body's neurons.
  • ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis / Lou Gehrig's disease) — the book's worked illustration of why the three categories matter.
    • Motor neurons throughout the brain and spinal cord die and stop sending messages to skeletal muscles.
    • Without stimulation, muscles gradually weaken; the person loses control over arms, legs and body.
    • Cause of death is respiratory failure, because the muscles that control breathing (diaphragm and rib muscles) eventually die.
    • Sensory neurons and interneurons are not affected, so awareness and reasoning do not deteriorate. That is the point of the example: the disease is category-specific.

Neuroglial cell (glial cell)

Sensory (afferent) neuron

Sensory receptor

Motor (efferent) neuron

7.2Structure of Neurons

pp. 117–118
Big ideaA neuron's shape is its function — many short dendrites and a cell body receive, one long axon sends — and wrapping that axon in a myelin sheath is what makes conduction fast.

Outline

  • The shape of a typical neuron is specialized for communicating with other cells.
  • Dendrites. Many short, branching projections that provide a huge surface for receiving signals from other cells, and carry information toward the cell body.
  • Cell body. The enlarged central region signals travel toward. Holds all the normal organelles, including a nucleus, for maintaining the cell. The deck adds that the cell body both controls the cell's metabolic activities and integrates input from other neurons.
  • Axon. A single long extension that carries messages away from the cell body, either to another neuron or to an effector (muscle or gland).
    • The axon can span very long distances. The book's example: a motor neuron letting you wiggle your big toe has its cell body in the spinal cord and its axon running all the way to the muscles of the toe.
    • The end of the axon has many branches specialized to release a neurotransmitter, which alters the activity of the effector.
    • The axon is the sending portion; the dendrites and cell body are the receiving portions.
  • The scale analogy — the book's own, and a likely lecture hook.
    • If the cell body of a typical motor neuron were the size of a tennis ball, its axon would be about 1 mile (1.6 km) long and only about one-half inch (1.3 cm) in diameter.
    • At the same scale the dendrites would fill an average-sized living room.
  • Nerve vs. neuron — a distinction the exam can trade on.
    • A nerve is a bundle of parallel axons, dendrites, or both, arising from many neurons. A neuron is one cell; a nerve is a bundle.
    • Each nerve is covered with tough connective tissue.
    • Classified by the neurons it contains: sensory, motor, or mixed (both sensory and motor neurons).
  • Myelin sheath. An insulating outer layer on most axons outside the brain and spinal cord, and some within.
    • Two stated functions: it increases the rate of conduction of a nerve impulse, and it helps to repair it. The chapter summary sharpens the second to "plays a role in the regeneration of cut axons in the peripheral nervous system" — see CALLOUTS.
    • Composed of the plasma membranes of glial cells. Outside the brain and spinal cord these are Schwann cells; Lecture the deck adds that inside the CNS the sheath is formed by oligodendrocytes, a name the chapter never uses.
    • A Schwann cell's plasma membrane wraps around the axon many times, giving a covering that looks "somewhat like a jelly roll."
    • Functions as living electrical tape — insulating individual axons and preventing messages from short-circuiting between neurons.
    • The sheath is kept alive by the Schwann cell's nucleus and cytoplasm, which are squeezed to the periphery as the sheath forms.
  • Nodes of Ranvier.
    • A single Schwann cell encloses only a small portion of an axon — about 1 mm long.
    • The gaps between adjacent Schwann cells, where the axon is exposed to the extracellular environment, are the nodes of Ranvier.
    • Their presence is crucial to how rapidly a neuron transmits messages.
  • Saltatory conduction (saltare, to jump).
    • With the myelin sheath in place, the impulse "jumps" successively from one node of Ranvier to the next.
    • Up to 100 times faster than conduction on an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter.
    • Therefore axons conducting signals over long distances are typically myelinated.
    • The book's analogy: moving a basketball down the court — dribbling the whole length (unmyelinated) is slower than passing through a series of players (node to node).
  • Multiple sclerosis — the disease that proves the sheath matters.
    • Myelin sheaths in the brain and spinal cord are progressively destroyed.
    • Damaged regions become hardened scars called scleroses (hence the name).
    • These interfere with impulse transmission; short-circuiting between normally unconnected conduction paths delays or blocks signals going from one brain region to another.
    • Depending on the part of the nervous system affected, the result can be paralysis or loss of sensation, including loss of vision.

Exam tip

"Up to 100 times faster" carries a condition: of the same diameter. Diameter is the other variable that sets conduction speed, and the comparison is only meaningful held constant. Students routinely quote the 100× without it.

Exam tip

Neuron ≠ nerve. A neuron is one cell; a nerve is a bundle of many neurons' fibers wrapped in connective tissue. End-of-chapter Q5 trades on an adjacent confusion — synaptic cleft vs. node gap.

Exam tip

The two gaps in this chapter are different things and are drawn only one page apart. A node of Ranvier is a gap between two Schwann cells on one axon; a synaptic cleft (§7.4) is a gap between two neurons. Q5's distractor is exactly this swap.

Since the lecture deck outranks the textbook, learn the pair — and note the sheath's two locations matter for MS, which is a CNS demyelination and therefore an oligodendrocyte disease.

7.3Nerve Impulses

pp. 119–120
Big ideaA nerve impulse is not electricity in a wire — it is Na⁺ rushing in and then K⁺ rushing out through gated channels, reversing and then restoring a charge difference that the sodium-potassium pump set up in advance.

Outline

  • The impulse defined. A nerve's message — a nerve impulse or action potential — is an electrochemical signal caused by sodium ions (Na⁺) and potassium ions (K⁺) crossing the neuron's membrane to enter and leave the cell.
  • Plasma membrane of a neuron. Selectively permeable — it allows some substances through but not others.
    • Ion channels — pores through which ions pass without using cellular energy, down their concentration gradient (high to low).
      • Each channel permits only certain ions: sodium channels pass only sodium ions, potassium channels only potassium ions. They "function as molecular sieves."
      • Some channels are permanently open. Others are regulated by a "gate" — a protein that alters shape in response to changing conditions, opening the channel (ions pass) or closing it (ions cannot cross).
    • Sodium-potassium pumps — proteins that actively transport sodium and potassium against their concentration gradients, using ATP.
      • Each pump ejects three sodium ions (Na⁺) out while bringing in two potassium ions (K⁺). The 3:2 ratio is printed and is the most quotable number in the section.
      • Lecture The deck adds what the 3:2 exchange buys: a concentration gradient (high Na⁺ outside, high K⁺ inside) and an electrical gradient (more positive outside, more negative inside). The chapter states the two facts separately and never names them as two gradients.
  • Resting potential. The state of a neuron that is not transmitting — and, as the book says, "resting is hardly the word to describe what is going on."
    • The membrane maintains a difference in electrical charge across it, keeping the inside surface more negative than the outside.
    • Ion distribution at rest: more sodium outside than inside; more potassium inside than outside.
    • Leaks run both ways and are unequal: potassium leaks out (it is more concentrated inside); to a lesser extent sodium leaks in.
    • The pump maintains the resting potential by pumping sodium out while moving potassium back in.
    • The cell also contains negatively charged ions too large to pass through the membrane — the chapter summary calls these negatively charged proteins.
    • The number: the inner surface is typically about 70 mV (millivolts) more negative than the outer surface. The book's scale check — that is about 5% of the strength of a size AA flashlight battery.
    • Why spend the ATP: the resting potential lets the neuron respond more quickly than it could if the membrane were electrically neutral at rest. The book's analogy: keeping a car's battery charged so it starts as soon as you turn the key.
  • Action potential. A sudden reversal of the charge difference across the membrane, followed by restoration of the original difference.
    • Step 1 — sodium ions (Na⁺) enter the axon.
      • An excitatory stimulus causes the gates on sodium channels to open.
      • Sodium enters; its positive charge begins to reduce the negative charge within. That reduction of the charge difference is depolarization.
      • The action potential begins when depolarization reaches the threshold. At threshold, the gates on more sodium channels open — the positive-feedback step.
      • Enough sodium enters to create a net positive charge in that region, about +30 mV.
    • Step 2 — potassium ions (K⁺) leave the axon.
      • About halfway through the action potential, the gates on potassium channels open.
      • Potassium leaves; the exodus of positive charge makes the interior negative relative to the outside again.
      • That returns the membrane potential close to its resting value — repolarization.
    • After the fact — the pump cleans up. At the end there are slightly more sodium ions and slightly fewer potassium ions inside than before, and the sodium-potassium pump restores the original distribution. The pump's action is slow, so it does not contribute directly to the events of the action potential. The book makes this point explicitly and students routinely get it backwards.
  • Propagation — why it is a wave.
    • The events do not occur simultaneously along the whole axon. Sodium entering at one location makes the inside less negative there, and that change opens the sodium channel gates in an adjacent part of the membrane.
    • The change in charge therefore travels down the length of the axon.
    • Once started, action potentials do not diminish — the book's image: the last domino in a falling row falls with the same energy as the first.
  • All-or-nothing.
    • The intensity of the impulse does not vary with the strength of the stimulus that triggered it. If an action potential occurs at all, it is always of the same intensity as any other.
    • The book's analogy: firing a gun — the force of the bullet is not changed by how hard you pull the trigger.
  • Refractory period.
    • Immediately after an action potential the neuron cannot be stimulated again for a brief instant.
    • During it the sodium channels are closed and cannot be reopened, so a new action potential cannot yet be generated.
    • Consequence 1 — frequency coding. A prolonged above-threshold stimulus produces a series of discrete impulses, not one larger sustained impulse. So increasing stimulus strength increases the frequency of impulses, not their size. The book's example: impulse frequency increases with the heat of an object touched — but only up to a point.
    • Consequence 2 — one-way travel. The inability of the sodium gates to open during the refractory period is also the reason impulses cannot reverse and go backward toward the cell body. This is the whole answer to the study guide's "why does the action potential only travel one way."

Ion channel

Depolarization

Exam tip

The pump does not run the action potential. The book states it outright — the pump is slow and "does not contribute directly to the events of the action potential." It sets the stage beforehand and cleans up afterward. End-of-chapter Q8 and Q11 both separate pump from channel, and Q10's wrong option attributes repolarization to the pump.

Exam tip

Three numbers worth holding exactly: resting ≈ −70 mV, peak ≈ +30 mV, pump ratio 3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in.

Exam tip

All-or-nothing is about intensity, not about whether it fires. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger impulse; it makes more impulses per second. That substitution — amplitude for frequency — is the classic exam trap, and the refractory period is the mechanism behind it.

Exam tip

Depolarization is loss of the charge difference; repolarization is its return. The words sound like they should mean "making polar" and "re-making polar," which inverts the first one.

Exam tip

The one-way answer is the refractory period. The study guide asks it as its own objective. The sodium gates behind the wave cannot reopen, so there is nothing for the impulse to travel back through — it is the same mechanism as frequency coding, read in the other direction.

Worked example from the text

Threshold crossing is positive feedback — a little Na⁺ in opens more sodium gates, which lets more Na⁺ in. That is why the response is all-or-nothing once threshold is reached.

7.4Synaptic Transmission

pp. 121–124
Big ideaAt the end of the axon the signal changes medium — electrochemical to chemical — and the receptor on the receiving cell, not the neurotransmitter itself, determines whether the effect is excitatory or inhibitory.

Outline

  • Why a chemical step exists at all. When an impulse reaches the end of an axon, in almost all cases the message must cross a small gap the impulse cannot jump. Transmitting it requires a brief change in the medium of communication from an electrochemical signal to a chemical signal. A chemical is released from the axon tip, diffuses across the gap, and conveys the message.
  • The anatomy of a synapse.
    • Synapse — the junction between a neuron and another cell.
    • Synaptic cleft — the gap between the cells.
    • Synaptic knob — the small bulblike swelling ending each branch of the axon, on the presynaptic neuron.
    • Presynaptic neuron — the neuron sending the message ("before the synapse").
    • Postsynaptic neuron — the neuron receiving the message ("after the synapse").
    • Synaptic vesicles — tiny sacs inside the synaptic knob holding the neurotransmitter.
  • Release of the neurotransmitter and the opening of ion channels — the sequence.
    • The nerve impulse reaches the axon ending of the presynaptic neuron.
    • Synaptic knobs release packets of neurotransmitter. The impulse's arrival opens the gates of calcium ion channels in the knob membrane — Lecture the deck specifies voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels. Calcium ions move into the knob, causing the synaptic vesicle membranes to fuse with the plasma membrane and dump the neurotransmitter into the cleft.
    • Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft.
    • Neurotransmitter binds with receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. A receptor is a protein that recognizes a particular neurotransmitter, "much as a lock recognizes a key."
      • The only cells a neurotransmitter can stimulate are cells that have receptors specific for it. So only certain neurons can be affected by a given neurotransmitter.
    • Binding opens an ion channel in the postsynaptic neuron.
      • The response depends on the type of ion channel the receptor opens. It is the receptor that determines which ion channels open and what the effect of a given neurotransmitter will be. This is the section's central claim.
  • Excitatory vs. inhibitory synapses.
    • Excitatory synapse — binding opens sodium channels; sodium enters; increases the likelihood that an action potential will begin in the postsynaptic cell.
    • Inhibitory synapse — binding opens different ion channels (the chapter does not name them; Lecture the deck names K⁺ or Cl⁻), which decreases the likelihood of an action potential. The cell's interior becomes more negatively charged than usual, so it requires larger than usual amounts of an excitatory neurotransmitter to reach threshold.
  • Summation.
    • A neuron may have as many as 10,000 synapses with other neurons at the same time — some excitatory, some inhibitory.
    • Summation is the combined effect of excitatory and inhibitory input at any given moment, and it determines whether an action potential is generated.
    • Integrating input from large numbers of different synapses gives the nervous system fine control — the book's analogy: having both an accelerator and a brake gives finer control of a car than an accelerator alone.
  • Removal of neurotransmitter. Neurotransmitters are quickly removed so their effects are temporary; otherwise they would excite or inhibit the postsynaptic membrane indefinitely.
    • Enzymatic deactivation — e.g. acetylcholinesterase (AChE) breaks down acetylcholine in the synapse.
    • Reuptake — the neurotransmitter is actively pumped back into the presynaptic knob.
    • Lecture Diffusion away from the cleft — on the deck (slide 7.49) as a third route, and in the chapter nowhere. See the CONFLICT below; the study guide asks for this list by name.
  • Roles of different neurotransmitters.
    • There are dozens of neurotransmitters carrying messages among neurons and between neurons and muscles or glands. Their activities "produce our thoughts and feelings."
    • Some neurotransmitters produce different effects on different types of cells.
    • Acetylcholine and norepinephrine act in both the peripheral and central nervous systems. Both have either excitatory or inhibitory effects depending on where they are released.
      • Most internal organs receive input from neurons releasing acetylcholine and from neurons releasing norepinephrine (Ch. 8).
      • Norepinephrine stimulates most organs but inhibits certain others. Whatever norepinephrine's effect on a particular organ, acetylcholine will have the opposite effect.
    • Acetylcholine is the neurotransmitter released at every neuromuscular junction (junction of a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle cell), where it triggers contraction of voluntary (skeletal) muscles. This is the direct hand-off from Ch. 6.
    • Myasthenia gravis — the worked case for the neuromuscular junction.
      • An autoimmune disease: the body's defense mechanisms attack the acetylcholine receptors at neuromuscular junctions.
      • With any repeated movement the acetylcholine released per impulse normally declines after the neurons have fired a few times in succession.
      • The low number of receptors makes these patients extremely sensitive to even the slightest decline in acetylcholine availability.
      • Result: little muscle strength, and repeated movements become feeble quite rapidly.
      • Treatment: drugs that inhibit acetylcholinesterase, preventing acetylcholine breakdown and elevating its level in the junction.
    • About 50 neurotransmitters are used by the central nervous system for communication between neurons in the brain. Different neurotransmitters are involved with different behavioral systems.
      • Norepinephrine — regulation of mood, the brain's pleasure system, arousal; thought to produce an energizing "good" feeling; thought to be essential in hunger, thirst and sex drive.
      • Serotonin — thought to promote a generalized feeling of well-being.
      • Dopamine — helps regulate emotions; also used in pathways that control complex movements.
      • A change in the level of a neurotransmitter affects the behaviors controlled by neurons that use it — the premise the whole Health Issue essay rests on.
  • Looking Ahead. This chapter considered the structure and function of neurons; the next shows how neurons are organized to form a functional nervous system. (p. 124)

Exam tip

Acetylcholine and norepinephrine are the book's own proof — each is excitatory in some places and inhibitory in others.

Exam tip

Stop And Think

1("Stop and Think", p. 120) "Red tides" in the ocean are caused by proliferation of dinoflagellates — single-celled marine algae containing a chemical called saxitoxin (STX), extremely small concentrations of which prevent sodium channels in mammalian neurons from opening. The clams, scallops and mussels that consume the dinoflagellates are insensitive to the toxin, but STX accumulates in their tissues. What effect would you expect STX to have on nerve transmission in humans who accidentally eat tainted shellfish?

Nerve transmission would be blocked. The action potential begins with sodium entering the axon through gated sodium channels; if those channels cannot open, depolarization cannot reach threshold, and no action potential is generated at all — not a weakened impulse but an absent one, because conduction is all-or-nothing. Affected neurons fall silent. The clinically important consequence is the one the chapter gives for ALS: motor neurons supplying the diaphragm and rib muscles stop firing, so the muscles of breathing are not stimulated, and respiratory failure is the danger. Sensory loss — numbness and tingling, classically around the mouth — follows from the same block in sensory neurons. The shellfish are unaffected because their own sodium channels do not bind STX; the toxin is species-specific in its target, not universally inert.

2("Consider This Case", p. 124) It is Cassidy's 30th birthday. She tells her friend Olivia that her vision in her right eye is blurry and cannot be corrected with glasses, her left leg seems weak, and she feels depressed because she is tired all the time. After tests, a doctor diagnoses multiple sclerosis — a condition in which body defence cells mistakenly attack the myelin sheath on her nerve cells. Which of Cassidy's symptoms led the doctor to suspect MS?

The blurry vision in one eye that glasses cannot correct, together with the weakness in one leg. Both are the giveaway, and for the same reason: they are neurological, not optical or muscular. Blurring that no lens can fix is not a focusing problem in the eye — it points to the signal from the eye failing on its way to the brain. Leg weakness with no muscle injury points to motor signals failing on the way out. The decisive feature is that the two deficits are in unrelated parts of the body, which a single local injury cannot explain but a disease attacking myelin everywhere can. The fatigue and low mood are real symptoms but far too non-specific to point anywhere on their own.

3("Consider This Case", continued, p. 124) Given what you know about the function of the myelin sheath, why are the problems so widespread?

Because myelin is not a feature of one nerve or one region — it sheaths axons throughout the central nervous system and most axons outside it, wherever signals must travel fast or far. An autoimmune attack on myelin therefore has targets everywhere at once, and the symptoms appear wherever patches of sheath happen to be destroyed. The mechanism is §7.2's: without the sheath the impulse can no longer jump node to node, so saltatory conduction is lost and transmission slows or fails; and the damaged regions harden into scleroses that let signals short-circuit between normally unconnected conduction paths, so messages are not merely delayed but misrouted. Since the sheath's job is identical in the optic nerve and in the motor pathway to the leg, losing it produces blurred vision and leg weakness by exactly the same failure — which is why one disease shows up in two unrelated places.

Figure Questions

1Long axons are myelinated, but short axons may not be. Why? (Figure 7.3, p. 118)

Because the benefit myelin confers is speed over distance, and over a short axon there is almost no distance for it to buy anything. The sheath works by forcing the impulse to jump from one node of Ranvier to the next instead of travelling continuously, which is up to 100 times faster on an axon of the same diameter. Over a metre-long axon — a motor neuron running from the spinal cord to a toe — that difference is the difference between a usable reflex and an unusable one. Over an interneuron's axon a fraction of a millimetre long, inside the brain or cord, continuous conduction is already fast enough, and the sheath would cost material, space and the metabolic upkeep of the Schwann cells that maintain it without a meaningful return. The book's basketball analogy makes the same point: passing the ball down the court beats dribbling it only when there is a court to cross.

1Choose the incorrect statement:

a) Neurotransmitters diffuse across the myelin sheath. b) In an inhibitory synapse, the neurotransmitter makes it less likely that an action potential will be generated in the postsynaptic (after the synapse) neuron. c) Neurotransmitters are stored in synaptic vesicles. d) Most interneurons are found in the central nervous system.

a

Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synaptic cleft, the gap between two neurons — not the myelin sheath, which is an insulating wrapper along the axon and is not a gap at all. The other three are correct as written: (b) is the definition of an inhibitory synapse, and note it correctly says postsynaptic; (c) is correct — vesicles in the synaptic knob hold the transmitter; (d) is correct — interneurons are found only in the brain and spinal cord.

2In botulism, a type of food poisoning, the poison produced by bacteria in the spoiled food prevents the person's synaptic vesicles from fusing with the neuron's membrane. You would expect that this effect would

a) cause excessive destruction of neurotransmitter in the synaptic cleft. b) destroy myelin. c) prevent the message of the presynaptic cell from reaching the postsynaptic cell. d) cause neurotransmitters to clog the synaptic cleft.

c

Fusion of the vesicle with the presynaptic membrane is the step that releases neurotransmitter into the cleft. Block fusion and no transmitter is released, so nothing crosses and the postsynaptic cell is never signalled. (a) fails because the toxin acts before release, not on breakdown afterward. (b) fails because the toxin acts at the synaptic knob, nowhere near the Schwann cells that make myelin. (d) is backwards — with no release there is less transmitter in the cleft, not a surplus.

3In a resting neuron

a) potassium ions are more concentrated outside the membrane than inside. b) the inside is more negative than the outside. c) sodium ions are more concentrated inside than outside. d) action potentials are being generated.

b

The resting potential is defined by the inner surface being about 70 mV more negative than the outer. (a) and (c) each invert a gradient: at rest potassium is higher inside and sodium is higher outside. (d) contradicts the meaning of "resting" — a resting neuron is one not transmitting an impulse, though its pumps are working hard.

4You are a neurophysiologist trying to identify the function of a particular nerve cell. You notice that this nerve cell fires immediately before a person's pinky finger bends. You correctly conclude that this axon

a) may be part of a sensory neuron carrying information from the finger toward the brain. b) may be part of a motor neuron carrying information from the brain toward the pinky. c) is without a doubt part of an interneuron. d) is dead.

b

Firing immediately before the movement makes this a command going out, not a report coming in — the defining direction of a motor (efferent) neuron, whose effector here is the muscle that bends the finger. (a) reverses the direction and the timing: a sensory neuron from the finger would fire in response to the bend, after it. (c) fails on two counts — interneurons are confined to the brain and spinal cord, and "without a doubt" is unsupportable from a single observation. (d) a dead cell does not fire.

5The synaptic cleft

a) is a chemical that allows two neurons to communicate with one another. b) is a gap between two neurons. c) is a gap between two Schwann cells forming the myelin sheath. d) allows saltatory conduction.

b

The cleft is the small space between the presynaptic and postsynaptic cells. (a) describes a neurotransmitter — a chemical, not a space. (c) and (d) both describe the node of Ranvier, the other gap in this chapter: it is the space between adjacent Schwann cells on a single axon, and it is what makes saltatory conduction possible. The question is built on exactly that swap, so be able to say which gap lies between two cells of the same axon and which lies between two different neurons.

6Choose the correct statement:

a) Neurotransmitters diffuse across the myelin sheath. b) An inhibitory neurotransmitter makes it less likely that an action potential will be generated in the presynaptic (before the synapse) neuron. c) Neurotransmitters are stored in synaptic vesicles. d) Most interneurons are found in the peripheral nervous system.

c

Vesicles in the synaptic knob store the neurotransmitter until calcium entry triggers their fusion and release — the only correct statement in the set. (a) is wrong for the reason given in Q1: transmitters cross the cleft, not the sheath. (b) is wrong by one word — inhibition reduces the chance of an action potential in the postsynaptic neuron, the receiving cell; the presynaptic cell has already fired. (d) inverts the location — interneurons are found only in the central nervous system. This question is Q1 inverted, with options (b) and (d) each altered to be false; comparing the two is the cheapest way to lock both facts down.

7Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which

a) patches of myelin are destroyed. b) neurotransmitters can no longer be produced. c) neurotransmitters can no longer be removed from the synapse. d) synaptic vesicles become clogged.

a

MS progressively destroys myelin sheaths in the brain and spinal cord, leaving hardened scars (scleroses) that slow, block and short-circuit conduction. (b), (c) and (d) are all synaptic faults, and MS is not a disease of the synapse — this question is testing whether you can place the lesion at the right part of the neuron.

8Choose the incorrect statement about ion channels:

a) An ion channel is a small pore (opening) through the plasma membrane. b) An ion channel is usually specific for one type of ion. c) An ion channel uses cellular energy to pump sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and potassium ions (K⁺) into the cell. d) The opening and closing of ion channels is important in the generation of an action potential.

c

That is the sodium-potassium pump, not a channel. The distinction is the whole point: channels let ions move without cellular energy, down their gradients, while the pump spends ATP to move them against their gradients (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in). (a), (b) and (d) are all correct — channels are pores, they are usually ion-specific ("molecular sieves"), and their gated opening and closing is what produces the action potential.

9What is the initial (first) event of an action potential?

a) depolarization caused by potassium ions rushing to the inside. b) repolarization due to rushing of potassium ions to the inside. c) depolarization due to rushing of sodium ions to the inside. d) repolarization due to departure of potassium ions from the axon.

c

Sodium gates open first, sodium enters, and the loss of the charge difference is depolarization. (a) and (b) both have potassium moving inward, which is the wrong direction — potassium's role in the impulse is to leave. (d) names a real event with the right ion and the right direction, but it is the second event, not the first — and repolarization cannot precede the depolarization it reverses.

10Repolarization

a) establishes the threshold voltage. b) is due to movement of potassium ions out of the axon. c) is due to movement of sodium ions into the axon. d) occurs as the sodium-potassium pump moves potassium out of the axon.

b

About halfway through the action potential the potassium gates open, potassium leaves, and the interior returns to being negative relative to the outside. (a) confuses repolarization with threshold, which is the trigger point crossed on the way up. (c) describes depolarization. (d) is the subtlest distractor and the one worth pausing on: the pump is involved in restoring ion distribution, but it acts after the impulse and the book says explicitly that it is too slow to contribute directly to the events of the action potential — and in any case the pump moves potassium in, not out.

11The sodium-potassium pump

a) uses energy to move sodium ions out of the cell and potassium ions into the cell. b) can move sodium ions only from an area where they are highly concentrated to an area where they are less concentrated. c) can move potassium ions (K⁺) only toward a region that is positively charged. d) is part of the myelin sheath.

a

The pump spends ATP to eject 3 Na⁺ and import 2 K⁺ against their concentration gradients. (b) describes movement down a gradient, which is diffusion through a channel and needs no energy — it also contradicts the word "pump." (c) is wrong because the pump moves K⁺ into the cell, whose interior is negative, not positive. (d) misplaces the pump entirely — it is a protein in the neuron's own plasma membrane, not a component of the Schwann-cell wrapping.

12Saltatory conduction

a) occurs only in myelinated nerve fibers. b) is slower than other types of nerve conduction. c) occurs only in the central nervous system. d) is the way an impulse gets from one neuron to another.

a

Saltatory conduction is jumping from node of Ranvier to node of Ranvier, and nodes exist only where there is a myelin sheath to leave gaps in. (b) is backwards — it is up to 100 times faster than conduction on an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter. (c) is wrong because myelinated axons are found in both the peripheral and central nervous systems; the text says most myelinated axons are outside the brain and cord. (d) describes synaptic transmission, which is chemical and happens between neurons, whereas saltatory conduction is electrochemical and happens along a single axon.

13The ______ is an insulating layer formed by Schwann cells that increases the rate of an action potential. (fill in the blank — no options printed)

myelin sheath

"Formed by Schwann cells" and "insulating" together identify the myelin sheath uniquely. L1.3The neuromuscular junction, step by step

Big ideaThe efferent motor neuron branches to many muscle fibers, and where its axon terminal meets one of them, acetylcholine crosses a cleft, opens sodium channels in the sarcolemma, and sodium floods in down its gradient.
  • The arrangement. An efferent motor neuron leaves the CNS in a bundled nerve. It branches, branches, branches, and innervates a variety of different muscle fibers. "Remember, this is a cellular level" — a muscle fiber is a muscle cell.
  • The message reaches the end of the axon — the axon terminal.
  • Where that terminal touches a muscle fiber, the junction is the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) — "you had a chance to see these last week in lab."
  • The neurotransmitter released there is acetylcholine (ACh). He pushed hard on the full word: "ACh is not as specific as I wanted. What does ACh stand for?" — acetylcholine.
  • The sequence, in his order:
    • Acetylcholine is released from the axon terminals into the tiny gap between the neuron and the muscle fiber — the synaptic cleft.
    • It binds acetylcholine receptors on the muscle fiber's plasma membrane.
    • That plasma membrane has its own name: the sarcolemma.
    • Those acetylcholine receptors are coupled to ion channels for sodium. Binding opens them.
    • Sodium floods into the cell.
  • Why sodium moves in, and he made the room say it: concentration gradient. "We've got more sodium outside than we do inside. The door opens, things move downhill. Every time. That's always the answer."
  • The membrane terminology he set up here and reused all lecture:
    • Presynaptic membrane — the neuron's.
    • Postsynaptic membrane — the receiving cell's.
    • Synaptic cleft — the gap between them.

L1.4Twitch, summation, tetanus, fatigue

0 slides
Big ideaOne stimulus gives one twitch; a second stimulus arriving before relaxation builds on the first; enough of them lock the muscle in tetanus; and past that is failure.
  • Muscle twitch — a contraction of the muscle in response to a single stimulus.
    • What counts as the stimulus: he pushed past "calcium" to a nerve impulse — an action potential — "which ultimately leads to the release of calcium from the SR."
    • An electrical shock also works. "If I shock your arm, it's gonna flinch every time." Brief, and typically not part of normal movement.
  • Summation — hitting the muscle with a second stimulus before it is fully relaxed. The second twitch is stronger than the first.
    • His swing analogy: swinging an arm up from the bottom reaches the same height every time. Pushing it again before it comes back down sends it higher. "I built it off of what's already there."

    • "It's like what happens if you've ever been hit with a taser or a stun gun."

  • Fatigue — where the muscle fails. "And when I mean fail, I literally mean fail. They're done."
    • Causes he named: lack of ATP, and ion concentrations — or the lack thereof. "That imbalance has become balanced, and muscles don't really like that. We need that concentration gradient."
  • The graph he drew: single stimulus → single twitch returning to baseline. Then a second stimulus before baseline → a stronger twitch. Repeat → a peak or plateau. Keep going → tetanus, locked. Past that → fatigue.

L1.5Where the ATP comes from, and in what order

Big ideaFour fuel systems hand off in a fixed sequence over the first minute of work — stored ATP, creatine phosphate, glycogen anaerobically, then aerobic metabolism — and only the last one is big.

    • Creatine phosphate — about 10 seconds after that. Creatine phosphate is holding an inorganic phosphate, which gets slapped onto an ADP to make ATP, "only to break that immediately."

    • Creatine phosphate is rebuilt.
    • Oxygen is restored to myoglobin, delivered by hemoglobin in red blood cells.
    • Glycogen reserves are restored.

L1.6Slow-twitch and fast-twitch fibers

Big ideaSlow-twitch fibers are built for endurance on oxygen and fast-twitch for brief power without it, every muscle contains both, and training shifts the balance.

L1.7Aerobic exercise versus hypertrophy

0 slides
Big ideaAerobic training improves delivery and does not make muscles bigger; resistance training above 75% of maximum force makes existing fibers bigger, because the number of fibers is fixed.

    • Promotes development of new blood vessels — "so we get to bring in more oxygen next time."

    • Increases the number of mitochondria. "I have one worker or I have 100 workers… I can get a hell of a lot more done with 100 workers."

L1.9The reflex arc, and what a neuron actually says

0 slides
Big ideaTouching something hot runs receptor → afferent neuron → interneuron in the CNS → efferent neuron → muscle, and the hand moves before you feel the heat — because every neuron says exactly one thing and meaning comes from who is saying it to whom.
  • The worked example: touching something hot.
    • Thermal receptors built into the skin detect the heat difference.
    • The electrochemical signal does not start in the receptor — "but rather starts in the neuron itself. The receptor has the ability to stimulate the neuron to the point that an action potential begins."
    • Once an action potential begins there is no stopping it — an all-or-nothing event.
    • The signal travels the axon to the soma, then out to an interneuron — in the CNS, because that is the only place interneurons exist.
    • The interneuron integrates and interprets, compares it against all the other times that same message has come from the same place, and makes a decision.
    • It passes to an efferent motor neuron leaving the CNS, out to the effector — a skeletal muscle that says "move your hand."
  • The point he was actually making: "You dropped it long before you ever thought hot." That is a reflex arc.
  • When a reflex arc is used: "when damage is being done to my body, where speed is probably the most important thing. Anytime damage is being done to your body, you do not have time to sit there and think about all of the options."
  • The idea worth keeping from this whole section:
    • All neurons say exactly the same thing, and they only say one thing — an action potential, an electrochemical signal.
    • "Depending on who says it, and who they say it to, gives it meaning."
    • The interneuron knows where the signal is coming from, and that is what gives the message meaning.

Key terms

Reflex arc
receptor → afferent neuron → interneuron (CNS) → efferent neuron → effector; fast, protective, no deliberation.
All-or-nothing
once an action potential begins it cannot be stopped or graded.
Exam tip

L1.12Saltatory conduction: what "jumping" actually means

16 slides
Big ideaThe impulse does not leap over the myelin — it travels along the axolemma and is regenerated at each bare node, and the nodes make it faster because that is where the signal gets boosted back up.
  • He corrected the textbook's picture directly, and spent several minutes on it.
    • "A lot of texts will describe a signal as jumping from node to node to node, and a lot of students envision the electricity jumping over the insulating Schwann cell to the next node of Ranvier. That's not how it happens."
    • The conductive part of the neuron is its plasma membrane — the axolemma, exactly as the sarcolemma is for a muscle fiber.
    • His wire analogy: the Schwann cell is "like the plastic coating on the outside of an electrical wire. If it's there, electricity doesn't pass through it. The electricity passes on the… conductor, the copper wire inside."
    • What is true: "the messages travel faster." What is misleading: the picture of a spark arcing over the sheath.
  • What actually happens at a node, and he made the room work it out.
    • The signal decrements as it travels. His rope analogy: whipping a long heavy rope in the gym sends a wave down it, and "by the time it's 10 feet away, that's a little wave." It takes a lot of energy to get a signal to the end of a metre-long axon.
    • At each node — and only at a node, because only there is the axon bare — voltage-gated sodium channels open, sodium floods in, and the signal is boosted back up. "I can slap that rope right here causing the waves to get taller again."
    • "So you can't do that in the myelin sheath? At all?" — No. Only where it can reach the extracellular fluid, because that is where the ions are.
  • Why the nodes make it fast: the impulse is actively regenerated only at the nodes rather than continuously along the whole membrane — up to 100 times faster than on an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter.

Key terms

Saltatory conduction
node-to-node regeneration of the impulse along a myelinated axon; up to 100× faster than unmyelinated conduction of the same diameter.
Voltage-gated ion channel
a channel opened by the arrival of an electrical signal.

L1.13Ion channels: three kinds

0 slides
Big ideaChannels are selective doorways that cost no energy, and they come in three kinds — always open, opened by a chemical, and opened by voltage.
  • Why ions need channels at all. The action potential owes 100% of its existence to the movement of ions across the membrane. They do not pass directly through the plasma membrane because they are charged — he pushed past "they're large" to get charged.
  • Channels are exclusive. "This ion channel only allows certain ions to pass through. I've got ion channels for chloride, for sodium, for potassium."
  • Three kinds, and he named all three:
    • Leak channels — permanently open. He later notes potassium leak channels are always letting potassium out.
    • Ligand-gated ion channels — "that gate might need a key to fit into a lock before it opens." The key is a ligand — a neurotransmitter.
    • Voltage-gated ion channels — opened by the arrival of an electrical signal.
      • His garage-door analogy: pressing the button closes a contact, electricity flows to a motor, the door opens. No power, no door — "no matter how many times you push that button."
  • A gate is a protein that changes shape, opening or closing the channel.
  • Transport through a channel requires no energy. Ions follow their concentration gradient — they always roll downhill. "Why? It's free. The steeper the hill, the faster they move."
  • What does cost energy: the sodium-potassium pump, moving ions against their gradients. That is active transport. "We're trying to paddle our dumbasses upstream."

Leak channel
a permanently open ion channel.
Ligand-gated ion channel
opened when a chemical — a ligand, such as a neurotransmitter — binds it.
Voltage-gated ion channel
opened by the arrival of an electrical signal.
Active transport
movement against a concentration gradient; costs ATP.
Exam tip

Lecture The chapter gives two kinds of channel; he gives three. The textbook says only "some channels are permanently open, others are regulated by a gate." L1.14Resting membrane potential and the sodium-potassium pump

0 slides
Big ideaThe pump spends ATP to push 3 Na⁺ out and bring 2 K⁺ in, building both a concentration gradient and an electrical one, and the result is a resting cell sitting at −70 mV.
  • Resting membrane potential (RMP) = −70 mV.
    • What it means physically: put one oscilloscope probe inside the plasma membrane and one outside, and you read −70 mV. "That is the potential difference between the inside of the cell and the outside of the cell."
    • "Resting" means the neuron is not doing anything — no action potential, no nerve impulse. "It is literally just chilling."
    • It is at RMP about 99% of the time.
  • The sodium-potassium pump maintains it.
    • 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in.
    • It costs ATP to pump the 3 sodium out; "the 2 potassium get to come back in for free" — the pump snaps back into shape as the potassium fits in.
    • This is active transport, because it moves ions against their gradients.
  • Two gradients get built, and he wanted both:
    • Concentration gradient — way more sodium outside than inside, way more potassium inside than outside.
    • Electrical gradient — more positive outside, more negative inside. "That's why we have a resting membrane potential of −70 mV."
  • Why bother: "so that when we open doors, things move as fast as possible." The steeper the gradient, the faster the flux.
  • Potassium leak channels are "kind of just always open, allowing potassium to always leave" — working against the pump, which is part of why the pump has to keep running.

Resting membrane potential (RMP)
−70 mV; the potential difference across the membrane of a neuron that is not transmitting.
Sodium-potassium pump
active transport of 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, against their gradients, at the cost of ATP.
Potassium leak channel
an always-open channel through which potassium continuously leaves.

L1.15The action potential, phase by phase

0 slides
Big ideaA little sodium through ligand-gated channels nudges the cell to −55 mV, and from there it is unstoppable — Na⁺ in to +30, K⁺ out back down, an overshoot below rest, then the pump restores the gradients.
  • The graph he drew, axis by axis: voltage in millivolts against time in milliseconds, with four lines marked — −70 (baseline), −55, 0, and +30.
  • Threshold potential = −55 mV. "Once a cell becomes stimulated enough to reach −55 millivolts, it's like pulling the trigger on a loaded gun. There's no stopping it at this point."
    • His second analogy, which he returned to: stepping off the 10-metre platform at the Clovis West pool. "Once you jump off, there's no going back."
    • This is the all-or-nothing event.
  • What gets the cell from −70 to −55 — the step he ended the lecture on.
    • Some sodium gates open. Not a lot of them, just some. Sodium dribbles in and the membrane potential creeps up.
    • He put it to the room twice: what opens those gates? Not voltage — the voltage is not there yet. A ligand. A neurotransmitter opens a ligand-gated channel, letting in just a little sodium.
    • If enough sodium comes in, the cell reaches −55 mV and threshold is met.
  • Depolarization — −55 up to +30 mV. Caused by sodium influx. Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; sodium is positively charged, so dumping it in raises the overall charge.
  • Repolarization — back down. Sodium channels close and potassium channels open. Potassium leaves, down its gradient, because there is far more potassium inside than outside. Potassium is positively charged, so losing it lowers the charge.
  • Hyperpolarization — the dip below −70. "We let hella potassium out", and the membrane overshoots past RMP.
  • Restoration. During hyperpolarization the sodium-potassium pumps turn on, potassium channels shut, and the pump rebuilds both gradients — 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, at the cost of ATP — back to RMP.
  • A student asked why potassium has to be removed at all: if it is not, "the nerve's not gonna do anything ever again" — the gradient never re-establishes and the cell cannot fire.

Key terms

Threshold potential
−55 mV; the level of depolarization at which the action potential becomes unstoppable.
Depolarization
the rise from −55 to +30 mV, caused by Na⁺ influx.
Repolarization
the fall back down, caused by K⁺ efflux.
Hyperpolarization
the overshoot below −70 mV before restoration.
