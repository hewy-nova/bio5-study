Did you know

  • Skin is the largest organ in the human body.
  • By age 70 the average person has shed about 105 pounds of skin, at a rate of 600,000 particles every hour and about 1.5 pounds a year.
  • People who use indoor tanning salons run a much higher risk of skin cancer than people who have never tanned indoors.

The chapter as one chain

  • Cells specialize. Nothing in the chapter works unless cells first differ from one another; specialization is the entry point.
  • Specialized cells group into four tissue types. Epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous — and the whole rest of the course refers back to these four.
  • Tissues are held together by junctions. Tight, adhesion and gap junctions decide whether a sheet of cells is a seal, a stretchable fabric or an electrically coupled unit — which is why the same four tissue types can build very different organs.
  • Two or more tissues make an organ; organs with a shared job make an organ system. Eleven of them, housed in dorsal and ventral cavities and lined with membranes.
  • The skin is the worked example. It is the one organ system the chapter dissects in full, and it contains all four tissue types at once.
  • The skin is also the effector. Its sweat glands, blood vessels and arrector pili are the machinery the next idea uses.
  • Homeostasis is what all eleven systems are for. Negative feedback — receptor, control center, effector — keeps variables oscillating around a set point.
  • Body temperature is the worked example of homeostasis, and it runs through the skin: thermoreceptors sense, the hypothalamus decides, skin structures act.

4.1From Cells to Organ Systems

pp. 66–73
Big ideaThe body is built in ascending layers — specialized cells → four tissue types → organs → eleven organ systems — and each level exists because the level below it specialized.

The four tissue types

  • Cell specialization. The body carries out many simultaneous functions because its cells are specialized for specific tasks; specialization is only the start, because specialized cells are then organized into tissues, organs and organ systems.
  • Tissue. A group of cells of similar type that work together to serve a common function. Four primary types in humans.
    • Epithelial tissue covers body surfaces, lines body cavities and organs, and forms glands.
    • Connective tissue is a storage site for fat, plays an important role in immunity, and provides protection and support.
    • Muscle tissue is responsible for body movement and for movement of substances through the body.
    • Nervous tissue conducts nerve impulses from one part of the body to another.
    • Lecture The deck adds two functions the section text does not: muscle tissue also stores glycogen and generates heat (slide 6). Both come back in Chapter 6.

Epithelial tissue

  • Two shared characteristics. All epithelia have a free surface and a basement membrane. SEE FIGURE 4.1
    • The free surface may be specialized for protection, secretion or absorption.
    • The basement membrane is a noncellular layer binding the epithelial cells to underlying connective tissue; it helps the epithelium resist stretching.
  • Three cell shapes.
    • Squamous epithelium (skway´-mus) — flattened, scale-like cells. Lines blood vessels and lungs, where flatness lets O2 and CO2 diffuse across easily; in blood vessels the smooth lining also reduces friction.
    • Cuboidal epithelium — cube-shaped cells. Found in many glands and in the lining of kidney tubules; provides some protection and is specialized for secretion and absorption.
    • Columnar epithelium — elongated, column-shaped cells, specialized for absorption and secretion. Lines the small intestine, where many cells carry fingerlike folds that greatly increase surface area for absorption. Goblet cells in that lining produce mucus that eases the passage of food and protects the lining.
  • Two layer counts. Any of the three shapes can be simple (a single layer) or stratified (multiple layers). Stratified epithelium is often protective, because the extra thickness makes it harder for molecules to pass through.

Glands

  • A gland is epithelial tissue that secretes a product.
    • Exocrine glands secrete into ducts leading to body surfaces, cavities or organs — digestive-enzyme glands, milk glands, and the oil and sweat glands of the skin. (Exo: out.)
    • Endocrine glands lack ducts and secrete hormones into the spaces just outside the cells; the hormones then diffuse into the bloodstream and are carried throughout the body. (Endo: inside.) Covered in depth in Chapter 10.

Connective tissue

  • The body's glue. Its most common role is to bind (tendons, ligaments) and support (cartilage, bone), but some types specialize in transport (blood) and energy storage (adipose). It is the most abundant and most widely distributed tissue in the body. SEE FIGURE 4.2
  • Cells in a matrix. All connective tissues are cells embedded in an extracellular matrix of protein fibers plus a noncellular ground substance. Ground substance may be solid (bone), fluid (blood) or gelatinous (cartilage), and is secreted by the connective tissue cells themselves or by nearby cells.
    • Connective tissue is the one tissue made up mostly of matrix rather than cells — the book's image is fruit suspended in a gelatin dessert. The characteristics of any specific connective tissue are set more by its matrix than by its cells.
  • Three fiber types, all produced by fibroblasts, which also repair tears: when skin is cut they move to the wound and lay down collagen that closes it and provides a surface for the outer skin layer to grow back over.
    • Collagen fibers — strong, ropelike, great tensile strength, resist pulling.
    • Elastic fibers — contain random coils; stretch and recoil like a spring. Common where great elasticity is needed — skin, lungs, blood vessels.
    • Reticular fibers — thin, extensively branching strands of collagen forming interconnecting networks that support soft tissues such as liver and spleen.
  • Connective tissue proper — loose and dense, differing in the ratio of cells to extracellular fibers.
    • Loose connective tissue has many cells but fewer, more loosely woven fibers.
      • Areolar connective tissue (ah-ree´-o-lar) — the universal packing material between other tissues; many cells in a soft, easily shaped gelatinous matrix. Found between muscles, letting one muscle move freely over another; also anchors skin to underlying tissues and organs.
      • Adipose tissue (ad´-ë-pos) — cells specialized for fat storage. Most long-term energy stores are fat; fat also insulates and, around certain organs, absorbs shock.
    • Dense connective tissue forms strong bands from large amounts of tightly woven fibers. Found in ligaments (bone to bone), tendons (muscle to bone) and the dermis. Lecture The deck adds that dense connective tissue may be regular or irregular (slide 20) — a distinction the chapter text does not make.
  • Specialized connective tissue — cartilage, bone, blood.
    • Cartilage (kär´-tl-ij) is tough but flexible; cushions between certain bones and maintains the structure of parts such as the ears and nose. Its cells, chondrocytes, sit in spaces in the matrix called lacunae. It has no blood vessels and no nerves, so nutrients reach its cells by diffusion from nearby capillaries; because that is slow, cartilage heals more slowly than bone.
      • Hyaline cartilage — most abundant; support and flexibility; cells in a matrix of collagen fibers and bluish-white gel-like ground substance. Commonly called gristle. At the ends of long bones, where it lets one bone slide easily over another; also part of the nose, ribs, larynx and trachea.
      • Elastic cartilage — more flexible than hyaline because of large amounts of wavy elastic fibers. In the external ear, giving strength and elasticity.
      • Fibrocartilage — fewer cells than hyaline or elastic; matrix contains collagen fibers. Forms a cushioning layer in the knee joint and the outer part of the shock-absorbing disks between the vertebrae. Built to withstand pressure.
    • Bone is living, actively metabolizing tissue with a good blood supply, so it heals promptly. Protects and supports internal structures; produces movement with muscles; stores lipids (in yellow marrow), calcium and phosphorus; produces blood cells (in red marrow). Its matrix is hardened by calcium for rigid support, with collagen fibers adding strength. More in Chapter 5.
    • Blood — a liquid matrix called plasma in which formed elements (cells, and cell fragments called platelets) are suspended. The "fibers" of blood are soluble proteins, visible only when blood clots. Red blood cells transport oxygen and carry some carbon dioxide away; white blood cells help fight infection; platelets assist in clotting. More in Chapter 11.

Muscle tissue

  • Muscle cells — muscle fibers — contract when stimulated. Three types, differing in structure, location and whether they are voluntary or involuntary. All are excitable and contractile. More in Chapters 6 and 12. SEE FIGURE 4.3
    • Skeletal muscle tissue — usually attached to bone, so contraction usually moves a body part. Under conscious control, therefore voluntary. Cells are long cylinders, each with several nuclei, and show striations — alternating light and dark bands caused by the orderly arrangement of the contractile proteins actin and myosin.
    • Cardiac muscle tissue — only in the walls of the heart; its contractions pump blood to the rest of the body. Involuntary. Cells resemble branching cylinders, have striations, and typically only one nucleus. Special junctions at the plasma membranes strengthen the tissue and speed conduction of impulses through the heart.
    • Smooth muscle tissue — involuntary. In the walls of blood vessels and airways, where contraction reduces flow of blood or air; also in the walls of stomach, intestines and bladder, where it mixes and propels food and eliminates wastes. Cells taper at each end, have a single nucleus, and lack striations.

Nervous tissue

  • Makes up the nervous system — brain, spinal cord, nerves. Two general cell types. More in Chapters 7 and 8. SEE FIGURE 4.4
    • Neurons generate and conduct nerve impulses to other neurons, muscle cells or glands. Most have three parts: the cell body (nucleus and most organelles), dendrites (highly branched processes giving a large surface area for receiving signals) and one axon (a long extension that usually conducts impulses away from the cell body).
    • Neuroglia (glial cells) are far more numerous than neurons. They support, insulate and protect neurons, increase the rate at which neurons conduct impulses, and supply neurons with nutrients from nearby blood vessels. Recent studies indicate glial cells communicate with one another and with neurons.

The visual point is how much more of the field the neuroglia occupy than the neuron does.

Cell junctions

  • Three kinds of attachment between cells, each suiting the function of its tissue. Common in many tissues and especially in epithelium.
    • Tight junctions — membranes of neighbouring cells attached so securely they form a leakproof seal. In the linings of the urinary tract and intestines, where the seal prevents urine or digestive juices from passing through the epithelium.
    • Adhesion junctions (also called desmosomes) — less rigid; resemble rivets holding adjacent tissue layers together. The plasma membranes do not actually touch; they are bound by intercellular filaments attached to a thickening in the membrane, so cells stay connected but can still slide slightly. Common in tissues that must withstand stretching — skin, heart muscle, the opening of the uterus.
    • Gap junctions — connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells through small holes, letting certain small molecules and ions flow directly from one cell to the next. In heart and smooth muscle cells they help synchronize electrical activity and therefore contraction.
  • An organ is a structure composed of two or more different tissues that work together to perform a specific function.
  • An organ system is a group of organs with a common function. Example: trachea, bronchi and lungs make up the respiratory system, whose common function is to bring oxygen in and remove carbon dioxide. The human body has 11 major organ systems. SEE FIGURE 4.7
  • Body cavities. Most organs are suspended in internal body cavities, which do two things: protect vital organs from damage when we walk or jump, and let organs slide past one another and change shape — which matters when lungs fill with air, the stomach fills with food, the bladder fills with urine, or the body bends. SEE FIGURE 4.6
    • The ventral cavity ("toward the abdomen") divides into the thoracic (chest) cavity and the abdominal cavity, separated by a muscle sheet, the diaphragm.
      • The thoracic cavity subdivides again into the pleural cavities, housing the lungs, and the pericardial cavity, holding the heart.
      • The abdominal cavity contains the digestive, urinary and reproductive systems.
    • The dorsal cavity ("toward the back") subdivides into the cranial cavity, enclosing the brain, and the spinal cavity, housing the spinal cord.
  • Membranes are sheets of epithelium supported by connective tissue. They cover body cavities and organ surfaces and form physical barriers protecting underlying tissues. Four types.
    • Mucous membranes line passageways that open to the exterior — respiratory, digestive, reproductive, urinary. Some, like that of the small intestine, are specialized for absorption; others, as in the respiratory system, secrete mucus that traps bacteria and viruses.
    • Serous membranes line the thoracic and abdominal cavities and the organs within them, secreting a fluid that lubricates those organs.
    • Synovial membranes line the cavities of freely movable joints such as the knee, secreting a fluid that lubricates the joint and eases movement.
    • Cutaneous membrane — the skin — covers the outside of the body. Unlike the others it is thick, relatively waterproof and comparatively dry.

4.2Skin: An Organ System

pp. 74–79
Big ideaThe skin is the chapter's worked example of an organ system — a two-layered barrier plus its derivatives that protects, waterproofs, regulates temperature, makes vitamin D and senses the outside world.

The integumentary system and what skin does

  • Integumentary system. The skin and its derivatives — hair, nails, sweat glands, oil glands, wax glands. (An integument is an outer covering.) It counts as an organ system because skin and derivatives function together to provide many services.
  • Protection — a physical barrier shielding body contents from invasion by foreign bacteria and other harmful particles, from ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and from physical and chemical insult. Beyond the passive barrier, the skin contains macrophages, which fight infection actively (Chapter 13).
  • Preventing water loss — the outermost layer of cells contains the water-resistant protein keratin, which prevents excessive water loss from underlying tissues.
  • Temperature regulation — we perspire imperceptibly almost constantly; during strenuous exercise or in high environmental temperature the sweat glands become active and greatly increase output. Evaporation from the skin surface sheds excess heat. Changes in blood flow to the skin also regulate temperature.
  • Vitamin D production — modified cholesterol molecules in the skin's outer layer are converted to vitamin D by UV radiation; it travels in the bloodstream to liver and kidneys, where it is chemically modified to take up its role in stimulating absorption of calcium and phosphorus from food.
  • Sensation — the skin contains structures for detecting temperature, touch, pressure and pain. These receptors are components of the nervous system and keep us informed about the external environment.

The three layers

  • On most of the body the skin is less than 5 mm thick (less than a quarter inch), yet it is one of the largest organs: about one-twelfth of body weight, surface area 1.5 to 2 m2 (1.8 to 2.4 yd2). SEE FIGURE 4.8
    • Epidermis — the thin outer layer; a protective barrier against environmental hazards.
    • Dermis — the thicker inner layer; contains blood vessels, nerves, sweat and oil glands, and hair follicles.
    • Hypodermis (subcutaneous layer) — loose connective tissue beneath the skin that anchors it to the tissues of other organ systems below. Not usually considered part of the skin.
  • The epidermis is itself several layers of epithelial cells.
    • The outer surface — the part you can touch — is made of dead skin cells. Most of what you see on another person's surface is dead. They are shed at about 30,000 to 40,000 each minute; much of the dust in a room is dead skin cells.
    • Swimming or a long bath makes the dead surface cells absorb water and swell, wrinkling the skin — most noticeable where the dead layer is thickest, on palms and soles.
    • Skin does not thin as cells are shed, because they are continuously replaced from below. The deepest layer of epidermis contains rapidly dividing cells; new cells push older ones toward the surface, and on the way those cells flatten and die because they no longer receive nourishment from the dermis. Along the way keratin, a tough fibrous protein, gradually replaces the cells' cytoplasmic contents. About two weeks to a month passes from a cell's formation to its shedding.
    • Transdermal drug administration. Although the epidermis is a water-resistant barrier, lipid-soluble materials can cross the lipid cell membranes of epidermal cells; a drug dissolved in a lipid solvent can cross the epidermis, diffuse into the underlying connective tissue and be absorbed into the blood. Common uses: hormones for birth control or menopausal symptoms, nicotine for smoking cessation, antiemetics for motion sickness. SEE FIGURE 4.9
  • The dermis is much thicker than the epidermis over most of the body; it consists primarily of connective tissue, and also contains blood vessels, hair follicles, oil glands, ducts of sweat glands, sensory structures and nerve endings.
    • Unlike the epidermis, the dermis does not wear away — which is why tattoos, made by injecting tiny droplets of ink into the dermal layer, are permanent. Because the dermis is laced with nerves and sensory receptors, getting a tattoo hurts. SEE FIGURE 4.10
    • Blood vessels are present in the dermis but not in the epidermis. Nutrients reach the epidermis by passing out of dermal vessels and diffusing through tissue fluid into the layer above. Where skin is traumatized — a burn, or an ill-fitting shoe rubbing a heel — this fluid accumulates between epidermis and dermis, separating the layers and forming blisters.
    • The lower dermis is dense connective tissue containing collagen and elastic fibers, a combination that lets skin stretch and return to shape.
    • Aging. Resilience decreases with age; the most pronounced effects begin in the late forties, when collagen fibers stiffen and decrease in number and elastic fibers thicken and lose elasticity. With reduced moisture and less fat in the hypodermis, this produces wrinkles and sagging.
    • Botox. Some wrinkles, such as frown lines, are caused by contraction of facial muscles. Injecting Botox — the toxin from the bacterium that causes botulism — temporarily paralyzes those muscles, so the contractions that form the wrinkles cannot occur. The muscles regain the ability to contract over the next several months, so injections must be repeated. SEE FIGURE 4.11
  • The hypodermis shares some skin functions: it cushions blows and helps prevent extreme changes in body temperature, because it holds about half of the body's fat stores.
    • In infants and toddlers this fat layer covers the entire body; with maturity some stores are redistributed. In women, subcutaneous fat tends to accumulate in breasts, hips and thighs; in both sexes it accumulates in the abdominal hypodermis and in the sides of the lower back.
    • Liposuction vacuums fat from the hypodermis to reshape the body. It is not a way to lose much weight — not more than a few pounds can be removed — but it sculpts and removes bulges, and because it removes the storing cells, fat does not usually return to those areas. Generally safe but not risk free; in some patients it has produced blood clots that traveled to the lungs, causing death.
    • Newer noninvasive FDA-approved procedures use freezing, laser, ultrasound or radio frequencies to kill fat cells; the body removes the dead cells over days to months. They remove less than liposuction but can take half an inch off a waistline in a single half-hour treatment.
  • Two interacting factors: the quantity and distribution of pigment, and blood flow.
  • Melanin is produced by melanocytes at the base of the epidermis. These spiderlike cells produce two kinds — a yellow-to-red form (pheomelanin) and the more common black-to-brown form (eumelanin). The melanin is taken up by surrounding epidermal cells, colouring the entire epidermis.
    • All people have about the same number of melanocytes. Differences in skin colour come from differences in the form of melanin produced and the size and number of pigment granules; genetic makeup determines which combination is produced.
  • Circulation. Well-oxygenated blood in dermal vessels gives a pinkish or reddish tint. Intense embarrassment increases blood flow, heightening the colour in face and neck — blushing, which is impossible to stop. Other intense emotions can drain colour: a sudden fright causes a rapid drop in blood supply and pallor.
  • Oxygenation. Well-oxygenated blood is ruby red; poorly oxygenated blood is a much deeper red that reads as bluish through the skin. In extreme cold the body shunts blood away from the skin to the core, which reduces oxygen supply to small surface vessels, and oxygen-poor blood seen through the thin skin of the lips makes them look blue. Too little sleep can slightly lower blood oxygen, darkening the blood; in some people that darker blood is visible through the thin skin under the eyes as dark circles.
  • Tanning is a change in skin colour from sun exposure: melanocytes respond to UV by increasing melanin production. This is protective, because melanin absorbs some UV and stops it reaching the lower epidermis and dermis. The skin needs some UV for vitamin D production, but too much is harmful. Lecture The deck adds the point the essay makes in passing and the exam is likely to want: the protective buildup of melanin is not instantaneous (slide 60) — that gap is when the damage happens.

Hair, nails and glands

  • The epidermis gives rise to hair, nails, oil glands, sweat glands, wax glands and teeth. (Teeth are in Chapter 15.)
  • Hair grows almost everywhere except a few areas such as lips, palms and soles.
    • Function is largely protection: scalp hair shields the head from UV; nostril and external ear canal hair keeps particles and bugs out; eyebrows and eyelashes keep unwanted particles, glare, perspiration and rain out of the eyes.
    • Hair also has a sensory role — receptors associated with hair follicles are sensitive to touch.
    • A hair consists of a shaft (projecting above the skin surface) and a root (extending below the surface into the dermis or hypodermis, embedded in a hair follicle). Nerve endings surround the follicle and are so sensitive that we notice even slight movements of the shaft.
    • Each hair is supplied with an oil gland that opens onto the follicle and supplies an oily secretion making the hair soft and pliant.
    • In the dermis a tiny smooth muscle, the arrector pili, attaches to the hair follicle. Its contraction pulls on the follicle and makes the hair stand up; it is associated with fear and with cold. The tiny mound of flesh at the base of the erect hair is a goose bump.
  • Nails protect the sensitive tips of fingers and toes. Like hair, they are modified skin tissue hardened by keratin. The nail itself is dead and lacks sensory receptors, but it is embedded in tissue so sensitive that we detect the slightest pressure of an object on the nail — so nails act as sensory "antennas". They also help us manipulate objects.
  • Three glands in the skin — oil, sweat and wax. All three develop from epidermal cells but differ in location, structure and function.
    • Oil (sebaceous) glands occur virtually all over the body except palms and soles. They secrete sebum, an oily substance of fats, cholesterol, proteins and salts; the secretory part sits in the dermis. Sebum lubricates hair and skin and contains substances that inhibit growth of certain bacteria. If the duct becomes blocked, sebum accumulates, bacteria can invade the gland and hair follicle, and the result is acne.
    • Sweat glands produce sweat — largely water plus some salts, lactic acid, vitamin C and metabolic wastes such as urea. Some wastes are eliminated in sweating, but the principal function is temperature regulation by evaporation from the skin surface.
    • Wax glands are modified sweat glands found in the external ear canal. They produce wax, which protects the ear by trapping small particles.

4.3Homeostasis

pp. 80–82
Big ideaHomeostasis is the dynamic maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment, achieved mainly by negative feedback loops built from a receptor, a control center and an effector.

What homeostasis is

  • Why it is needed. To stay healthy, organ systems must constantly adjust their functioning in response to changes in the internal and external environments. They are interdependent, working together to provide every cell's basic needs — water, nutrients, oxygen and a normal body temperature.
    • The book's analogy: just as city dwellers breathe the same air and drink the same city water, all body cells are surrounded by the same extracellular fluid, so changes in that fluid's makeup affect every cell.
  • Homeostasis — the ability to maintain a relatively stable internal environment despite changes in the surroundings. The word means "to stay the same".
    • But conditions inside never actually stay the same. Homeostatic mechanisms do not maintain absolute constancy; they dampen fluctuations around a set point to keep conditions within a range. Homeostasis is dynamic, not static.
  • When it fails — diabetes. Normally, as a meal is digested and nutrients absorbed, rising blood glucose stimulates the pancreas to release insulin, whose general effect is to lower blood glucose back toward a more desirable value. In diabetes either the pancreas does not produce enough insulin or body cells cannot use it. Without insulin, blood glucose can rise to a point that damages eyes, kidneys, nerves and blood vessels.
  • Communication. Homeostasis depends on communication within the body, by two primary means: the nervous system brings about quick responses to change, and the endocrine system produces hormones, which bring about slower and longer-lasting responses.

Negative feedback and its three components

  • Negative feedback mechanisms are corrective measures that slow or reverse a variation from the normal value of a factor — blood glucose, body temperature — and return it to normal. When the normal value is reached the corrective measures cease: the normal value is the feedback that turns off the response.
  • Positive feedback mechanisms cause a change that promotes continued change in the same direction. Positive feedback (described in Chapter 10) does not promote homeostasis.
  • The three components. SEE FIGURE 4.12
    • A receptor detects change in the internal or external environment — a sensor that monitors the environment and, on detecting a change in some variable, sends that information to the control center.
    • A control center determines the factor's set point — the level or range that is normal for that factor. It integrates information from all pertinent receptors and selects an appropriate response. In most of the body's homeostatic systems the control center is in the brain.
    • An effector, often a muscle or gland, carries out the selected response.
  • The thermostat analogy. In a house in winter a thermostat is both the temperature-sensing receptor and the control center. If it senses the temperature has fallen below a programmed set point it turns on the heating (the effector); when the temperature returns to the set point it turns the heating off. Temperature fluctuates around the set point but stays within a range.
  • The hypothalamus is the body's temperature control center — a region of the brain whose set point is approximately 37 °C (98.6 °F), differing slightly from person to person. Temperature must not vary far from this, because even small changes have dramatic effects on metabolism. SEE FIGURE 4.13
    • Temperature is sensed at the outer surface by skin receptors and deep inside by receptors that sense the temperature of the blood. The hypothalamus receives input from both.
  • Too hot (example environment 38 °C / 100.4 °F): thermoreceptors in the skin detect heat and message the hypothalamus, which sends nerve impulses to the sweat glands to increase secretions. As perspiration evaporates and temperature drops below 37 °C, the signals stop. It is negative feedback because the effect it produces — cooling of the skin — is relayed back and shuts the corrective mechanism off.
    • Other heat-losing mechanisms: dilation of blood vessels in the dermis (releases more heat to the surrounding air; explains the flushed look during strenuous exercise) and relaxation of the arrector pili muscles (keeps damp, cooling hair lying close to the skin). The brain may also initiate behavioural responses — seeking shade, removing a sweatshirt.
  • Too cold: subtle drops are detected largely by skin thermoreceptors, which message the hypothalamus. The brain sends impulses to sweat glands ordering decreased activity, and to dermal vessels telling them to constrict — which reduces blood flow to the extremities, conserving heat for the internal organs ("cold hands, warm heart").
    • Also: contraction of the arrector pili muscles, making hairs stand on end and trapping an insulating layer of air — piloerection, less effective in humans than in more heavily furred animals. The body also increases metabolic activity to generate heat, and uses repeated contraction of skeletal muscles — shivering. Finally, behavioural responses such as folding one's arms across the chest.
  • Hyperthermia — abnormally elevated body temperature, commonly called heat stroke, marked by confusion and dizziness, when the mechanisms for lowering temperature fail. If core temperature reaches about 42 °C (107 °F) the heartbeat becomes irregular, blood oxygen drops, the liver ceases to function, and unconsciousness and death soon follow. Few people survive 43 °C (110 °F).
  • Hypothermia — abnormally low body temperature, 35 °C (95 °F) or below, disrupting nervous system function and the temperature-regulating mechanisms themselves. Sufferers usually become giddy and confused at first; at 33 °C (91.4 °F) they lose consciousness; at 30 °C (86 °F) blood vessels are completely constricted and temperature regulation is fully shut down, and death soon follows. It can be treated if detected early; in severe cases dialysis machines artificially warm the blood and pump it back into the body.

Stop And Think

1When you exercise, your breathing rate, blood pressure and heart rate all rise. Is that a violation of homeostasis? (p. 80)

No — it is homeostasis working. Exercising muscle consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and heat faster than at rest, so leaving breathing and circulation unchanged is what would let the internal environment drift. The raised rates are the corrective response that holds blood oxygen, blood carbon dioxide, pH and temperature near their set points. The chapter's own definition settles it: homeostasis is a dynamic state that dampens fluctuation around a set point, not a state in which nothing changes. What is held constant is the composition of the extracellular fluid, not the rate of the machinery that maintains it.

2As people age, the activity of their sweat glands declines. Why does that explain why public authorities worry more about the elderly than about children developing a heat-related illness during a heat wave? (p. 81)

Sweating is the body's main effector for shedding heat when the environment is above the set point — evaporation from the skin surface is what removes the excess. If sweat gland activity has declined, that effector is weakened, so the negative feedback loop cannot bring body temperature back down even though the receptors and the hypothalamus are working normally. The loop fails at the effector stage, temperature keeps climbing, and hyperthermia becomes likely. Children still have fully active sweat glands, so their cooling loop still closes.

3Frostbite is damage to tissues exposed to cold. Given what you know about the body's response to cold, why are fingers and toes especially susceptible? (p. 82)

One of the body's responses to falling temperature is constriction of the blood vessels in the dermis, which shunts blood away from the extremities to conserve heat for the internal organs. That is protective for the core but it is precisely what leaves fingers and toes with the least blood flow, so they lose heat and receive the fewest nutrients and least warmth exactly when the cold is worst. The body is trading the extremities for the core, and fingers and toes are the tissue that pays.

Figure Questions

1Calcitonin, from the thyroid gland, lowers blood calcium levels. Parathyroid hormone, from the parathyroid glands, raises them. Describe a negative feedback relationship involving these hormones as effectors that would keep blood calcium stable. (Figure 4.12, p. 81)

Blood calcium is the variable, and a set point for it sits in the control center. Receptors sensitive to blood calcium monitor its level. When blood calcium rises above the set point, the receptors report this, the control center directs the thyroid to release calcitonin, and calcitonin lowers blood calcium; as the level falls back to the set point, calcitonin release is shut off — the return to normal is itself the feedback that stops the response. When blood calcium falls below the set point, the same receptors report the drop, the control center directs the parathyroid glands to release parathyroid hormone, and PTH raises blood calcium until the set point is reached, at which point PTH release is shut off. The two hormones are opposing effectors on one variable, exactly like the sweating and shivering branches of Figure 4.13, so blood calcium oscillates in a narrow range around the set point rather than sitting exactly on it.

Health Issue

Fun in the Sun? (pp. 77-78)

  • Skin cancers are increasing at an alarming rate, largely from sun exposure.
  • Mechanism. UV radiation makes melanocytes increase melanin production, and melanin absorbs UV before it can damage the genetic information of deeper cells. But the protective melanin buildup is not instantaneous, which is the gap in which damage happens.
  • In skin cancer, UV alters the genetic material of skin cells so they grow and divide uncontrollably, forming a tumor.
  • Some experts fear rates will rise dramatically if the ozone layer, which blocks some UV before it reaches Earth, keeps thinning (Chapter 24).
  • Basal cell carcinoma — the most common; arises in the rapidly dividing cells of the deepest layer of epidermis.
  • Squamous cell carcinoma — second most common; arises in newly formed skin cells as they flatten and move toward the surface.
  • Melanoma — least common and most dangerous; arises in melanocytes. Unlike the two carcinomas, untreated melanomas often metastasize, first into lymph nodes and later into vital organs.
  • Survival: about 90% if the melanoma is found before it has metastasized; about 14% once cancerous cells have spread throughout the body.
  • Detection — the ABCD mnemonic. A = asymmetry (most melanomas are irregular in shape); B = border (often diffuse and unclear); C = colour (mottled — brown, black, red, white and blue); D = diameter (growths more than 5 mm, about 0.2 in., are threatening).
  • Prevention. Avoid prolonged sun exposure; wear a hat, long sleeves and sunglasses; use sunscreen of at least SPF 15, applied about 45 minutes before going out so the skin absorbs it and it is less likely to wash away with perspiration; use it even when overcast, because UV penetrates clouds; reapply after swimming.
  • The argument against relying on sunscreen. Most block the higher-energy UV-B while giving only limited protection against lower-energy UV-A. UV-B causes burning; recent research suggests UV-A exposure weakens the immune system, possibly impairing its ability to fight melanoma. Ironically, by preventing sunburn, sunscreens let people stay in the sun longer — possibly increasing melanoma risk.
  • Against tanning salons. For years they claimed to use "safe" wavelengths because they avoided skin-reddening UV-B — but those "safe" wavelengths are UV-A, and given the apparent UV-A/melanoma link the danger is now obvious.
  • Questions to consider: should tanning salons be required to explain the dangers of UV-A? How would you balance skin cancer risk against the vitamin D deficiency risk that excessive sunscreen use can create?
Health Issue

Acne: The Miseries and Myths (p. 79)

  • About four out of five teenagers have acne, which will probably annoy or distress them into their twenties and possibly beyond.
  • Simple acne is a condition of hair follicles associated with oil glands.
  • Cause. During the teenage years oil glands increase in size and produce larger amounts of oily sebum. The change is prompted, in both males and females, by rising levels of "male" hormones called androgens, secreted by the testes, ovaries and adrenal glands.
  • Consequently acne occurs most where oil glands are largest and most numerous — face, chest, upper back and shoulders.
  • Acne is the inflammation that follows when sebum and dead cells clog the duct where the oil gland opens onto the hair follicle.
  • A follicle obstructed by sebum and cells is a whitehead.
  • When sebum in a plugged follicle mixes with melanin, the result is a blackhead — so melanin, not dirt and not bacteria, is what makes these blemishes dark.
  • A pimple is a red raised bump, often with a white dot of pus, formed when obstructed follicles rupture and spew their contents into the surrounding epidermis. Rupture may happen naturally or be induced by squeezing. Usually heals within a week or two without scarring.
  • Myths refuted. Eating nuts, chocolate, pizza or potato chips does not cause acne. Acne is not caused by poor hygiene either — follicles plug from below, so dirt or oil on the skin surface is not responsible. (Most doctors still recommend washing the face two or three times a day with hot water to help open plugged follicles.)

Hand sanitizers (p. 85)

  • Source excerpted in the book: R. Babeluk et al., "Hand hygiene — evaluation of three disinfectant hand sanitizers in a community setting," PLOS ONE.
  • Design. Sixty undergraduates with no prior hand-hygiene training, as representatives of the general (non-healthcare) population. 15Applying the Concepts — Thor, a college ski champion, tore the cartilage in his knee and asked whether he would be ready to compete in a month. Why would you expect the answer to be no? (p. 84)

16Applying the Concepts — Hannah has the flu. As her fever rises she gets the chills, shivers and piles on blankets. She takes acetaminophen, her fever breaks, and as her temperature returns to normal she throws off the blankets, looks flushed and perspires. Explain using the mechanisms of body temperature control. (p. 84)

A fever raises the hypothalamic set point; only the set point moves, and the same loop runs in both directions

While the fever is climbing, Hannah's actual temperature is below the new, higher set point, so as far as the control center is concerned she is too cold. The hypothalamus therefore runs the entire cold response — skeletal muscles contract repeatedly (shivering), dermal vessels constrict, metabolic activity rises — and she adds behavioural responses by covering up. The subjective chill comes from the set point being above her temperature, not from the room being cold. When acetaminophen lowers the set point back toward normal, the relationship inverts: her temperature is now above the set point, so the hypothalamus runs the heat-loss response — sweat glands increase secretion, dermal vessels dilate (the flush), and she throws off the blankets.

17Applying the Concepts — Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is caused by defects in genes that disrupt collagen production. Explain why its symptoms include joints that extend beyond their normal range and stretchy, saggy skin. (p. 84)

Collagen is the fiber that resists pulling, so defective collagen removes the restraint from both ligaments and dermis

Collagen fibers are the strong, ropelike fibers with great tensile strength, and they are what makes dense connective tissue into strong bands. Ligaments — dense connective tissue joining bone to bone — depend on collagen to limit how far a joint can travel, so defective collagen makes those bands weak and overly extensible. In the skin, the lower dermis is dense connective tissue containing collagen alongside elastic fibers, and it is that combination that lets skin stretch and then return to shape. With collagen defective the restraint half is lost: the skin stretches too far and does not recover. The chapter makes the same connection when explaining aging skin.

18Consider This Case — why would Eunho feel more pain in her right leg, with second-degree burns, than in her right arm, where the third-degree damage was greater? (p. 82)

The pain receptors are in the dermis; the arm's receptors were destroyed, the leg's were injured but alive

The sensory receptors and nerve endings that report pain sit in the dermis, not in the dead outer epidermis. The leg's second-degree burn reached only the upper dermis, so the pain receptors there were injured and irritated but still alive and still connected — exactly the condition for intense pain. The arm's third-degree burn destroyed epidermis and dermis outright, taking the pain receptors with them. The greater injury reports less pain because the reporting apparatus is gone.

19Consider This Case — what would you predict to be the immediate medical concerns for a patient with third-degree burns? (p. 82)

Infection, fluid and electrolyte loss, failed thermoregulation, lost sensation, and no capacity to regenerate

Every function the section attributes to intact skin is lost over the burned area at once. The barrier against bacteria is gone, so infection is first. The keratinized water-resistant layer is gone, so massive fluid and electrolyte loss follows, with the risk of shock. Temperature regulation over that area fails, because the sweat glands, dermal blood vessels and hair follicles that serve as thermoregulatory effectors have been destroyed. Sensation is lost. And because the dermis is destroyed rather than merely damaged, the skin cannot regenerate from below, so grafting will be required rather than natural healing.

5 · Epithelial Tissue

Draw

The lab's 3×3 grid — rows squamous, cuboidal, columnar; columns simple, stratified, plus a pseudostratified cell showing columnar only. In each cell draw the cells sitting on a basement membrane with a free surface above.

  • basement membrane

  • stratified squamous

  • stratified columnar
  • pseudostratified ciliated columnar

  • cilia

Write

The defining characteristics

  • Lines the outer surfaces of the body (epidermis) and the inner surfaces of the respiratory, gastrointestinal, urinary and reproductive tracts, and forms glands.
  • Tightly packed cells attached to a basement membrane. Always has a free surface — no cells attached to its outermost layer.
  • Avascular — no blood vessels. All nutrition diffuses out of blood vessels in the connective tissue deep to the basement membrane. That single fact explains why the outer layers of stratified epithelium die.
  • Glands: secretions released into the interstitial space and picked up by blood = endocrine; released into ducts and carried to a site = exocrine.

How they are named

  • By layering and by cell shape. Three shapes — squamous, cuboidal, columnar. Simple = one layer; stratified = more than one.
  • A stratified epithelium is named for the shape of its apical layer, not its basal layer.
  • Pseudostratified only appears layered — one layer, packed so tightly the nuclei sit at different levels.

Type, location, function

  • Simple squamous — single layer of flat cells, flattened elliptical nucleus. Ideal for diffusion; minimises distance. Lines blood vessels and alveoli.
  • Simple cuboidal — height ≈ width, round nuclei. Secretion and/or absorption. Kidney tubules, bronchioles, many glands.
  • Simple columnar — height > width, oval nuclei elongated top to bottom. Secretion and/or absorption. Bronchi and digestive tract.
  • Stratified squamous — the basal layer sits on the basement membrane and retains the ability to divide; cells flatten as they move away from nutrition. Ideal for protection from trauma. Mucous membranes and skin.
  • Pseudostratified ciliated columnar — single layer, nuclei appear layered, has cilia that move mucus and trapped particles out of the trachea. Trachea and Fallopian tubes.
  • Stratified cuboidal — 2–3 layers, rare; mammary and salivary glands.
  • Stratified columnar — 2–3 layers, rare; rectal tissues, conjunctiva, pharynx.
  • Transitional — bladder, urethra, ureters. Allows the urinary organs to expand and stretch.

{{N}} Avascular + free surface + basement membrane is the three-part definition. The lab returns to avascularity to explain both nutrition by diffusion and why stratified squamous cells die as they rise.

The defining characteristic

  • Cells scattered in a matrix. The matrix is extracellular material — fibres embedded in a ground substance. This is the opposite of epithelium, which is tightly packed cells with very little matrix.
  • The matrix is unique to each type, by the relative numbers and types of fibres and the character of the ground substance.

Three fibre types

  • Reticular — thin, branching, dark on histology. Support and attachment.
  • Elastic — stretch and return to original shape; thin purple or dark coiled fibres. Elasticity.
  • Collagen — thick, strong, appear thick and pink. Strength and resistance to stretching.

The ground substance varies

  • Fluid (plasma of blood) → gel-like (hyaline cartilage) → mineralised (bone).

The other cells

  • Fibroblasts produce the fibres. Adipocytes are fat cells. Macrophages phagocytose damaged cells and debris. Plus cells for repair and immunity.

Classification

  • Connective tissue proper — loose (areolar, adipose, reticular) and dense (dense regular, elastic, dense irregular).
  • Supportive — cartilage (hyaline, fibrocartilage, elastic) and bone.
  • Fluid — blood and lymph.

The four this lab actually examines

  • Areolar — contains all three fibre types and all the cell types, loosely arranged criss-cross. Support and attachment in multiple directions.
  • Adipose — fat cells, very little matrix or fibres. Fat in one large droplet (white fat) or multiple small droplets (brown fat); the fat pushes the nucleus to the periphery. Cushions, stores energy, insulates.
  • Dense regular — collagen packed in one direction. Tendons and ligaments, where tensile strength is needed in a single direction.
  • Dense irregular — collagen in multiple directions, to resist tearing from multiple directions. Fascia, joint capsules, the dermis of skin.
  • Hyaline cartilage — the most common cartilage; matrix rich in proteoglycans with short collagen fibres, giving a smooth surface and a glassy appearance. Forms the embryonic skeleton later replaced by bone; found at the ends of long bones (reduces friction, protects from compression), in the rib cage and the nose.

The blood-supply exception, and why it matters

  • Connective tissues typically have very good blood supply — except dense connective tissue and cartilage. Those two therefore have long healing times.

The other two cartilages, for completeness

  • Fibrocartilage — collagen in thick bundles, added strength. Menisci of the knee and the intervertebral discs.
  • Elastic cartilage — significant collagen and elastic fibres; strength plus elasticity. The ear.

Write

The epidermis

  • Outermost layer of the skin, made of stratified squamous epithelial tissue. 4 layers — 5 in thick skin.
  • Stratum basale — deepest, a single layer of keratinocytes, and the only layer that divides. When they divide, the cell on the basement membrane stays and the new cell is pushed outward. On UV exposure these produce an inactive form of vitamin D essential for calcium regulation. Interspersed here are melanocytes and Merkel cells.
  • Melanocytes produce melanin, distributed in granules to the keratinocytes above, protecting them from UV damage. Melanin production increases in response to UV.
  • Merkel cells are modified receptors for sensory neurons and detect discriminative touch.
  • Stratum spinosum — multiple layers of keratinocytes.
  • Stratum granulosum — 3–5 layers, granular in appearance from keratin granules accumulating as the cells begin to die from lack of nutrition. Cells start to flatten.
  • Stratum lucidum — only in thick skin. Contains the remnants of dead keratinocytes (keratin and lipid) and appears translucent.
  • Stratum corneum — most superficial, named for the flattened, angular dead keratinocytes.

Why the outer cells die — the one mechanism behind the whole layer

  • Epithelial tissue has no blood vessels. Nutrients diffuse from the dermis into the epidermis and wastes diffuse back out to the dermal vessels. The layers furthest from the basement membrane receive very little nutrition, so they die. The keratin, granules and lipid membranes they leave behind form the barrier against pathogens and chemicals, the waterproof barrier against water loss, and the protection against physical trauma and abrasion.

The dermis

  • Lies deep to the epidermis. A significant amount of loose and dense irregular connective tissue surrounding nerves, blood and lymph vessels, glands and follicles. Two layers.
  • Papillary layer — the superficial one, from the basement membrane to the base of the papillae. Areolar connective tissue and blood vessels for nutrient and waste exchange. Contains the sensory neurons connecting to Merkel cells, and Meissner corpuscles for touch.
  • Reticular layer — dense irregular connective tissue, blood vessels and glands. Much thicker than the papillary layer and more dense with collagen and elastic fibres. Contains Pacinian corpuscles (deep pressure and vibration) and Ruffini corpuscles (stretch and body position).
  • Dermal papillae are foldings that increase the surface area connecting to the epidermis above.

The hypodermis

  • Also called the subcutaneous layer. Below the dermis and not actually part of the skin — it connects the skin to deeper tissues.
  • Areolar connective tissue plus larger blood and lymph vessels that supply the dermal vessels.
  • A significant amount of adipose — insulation, cushioning, energy storage.
  • How you know you have reached it on a slide: moving away from the epidermis, when you see significant amounts of adipocytes, you are in the hypodermis.
Watch out

Exam tip

{{N}} Learn the strata from the bottom up — basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum, corneum — because that is the order a cell actually travels, and the order explains why it dies.

Accessory structures

L1.3Epithelium: basement membrane, free surface, shape × layers

7 slides
Big ideaEvery epithelium sits on a basement membrane with a free apical surface opposite; classify it by the number of layers and by the shape of the cells nearest that free surface.
  • Two structural characteristics all epithelia share.
    • A basement membrane underneath — "under this carpet there's concrete". Whatever cell type is on top, the basement membrane is always there. It is not the bottom: under it is the underlying connective tissue, the way there is ground under the concrete. It gives the epithelium structural integrity and resists stretching and tearing — "but if you twist and pull hard enough, it's gonna tear."
    • An apical surface — the free edge opposite the basement membrane (apex). It may be specialised for protection, secretion or absorption.
  • Layers. One layer of cells on the basement membrane is simple; two or more is stratified — "stratified means stacked."

    • Cuboidal — as wide as tall as deep, box-like. Reserved for secretion and absorption.
    • Columnar — the cube stretched much taller than it is wide. Also secretion and absorption, and the height adds protection even in a single layer — "they're thick."
  • Where the shapes are used. Simple squamous is the thinnest possible barrier, so it makes the walls of capillaries ("exchange vessels") and the alveoli of the lungs: gas diffuses across only two cells of the thinnest shape there is. Squamous cells can also give a slick surface that reduces friction between two articulating surfaces. Columnar cells line the small intestine, where he wants secretion, absorption and protection, because "there's only one tube that travels all the way through my body."

L1.4Glands: coiled sheets, with or without a duct

2 slides
Big ideaA gland is a sheet of epithelium rolled into a tube; exocrine glands empty into ducts, endocrine glands empty into the interstitial fluid and let the blood carry the product everywhere.

L1.5Connective tissue: cells that don't touch, in a matrix that can be anything

11 slides
Big ideaConnective tissue is cells suspended in an extracellular matrix whose ground substance can be liquid, gel or rock and whose fibres are all made by the fibroblast — so blood and bone are the same kind of tissue.
  • The most abundant, most widely distributed tissue in the body. "This one's pretty tough."
  • Two characteristics.
    • The cells are not usually in direct contact with one another — "look to your left, look to your right; you're right next to someone, but you're not touching them."
    • The cells sit in an extracellular matrix (ECM). The matrix has a ground substance that sets its physical properties, and protein fibres — collagen, elastic and reticular — that add more. It might be tough, stretchy, snap back like a hair tie, be rigid and hard, or be liquid. "Ain't gonna be tough like concrete or liquid and be the same kind of tissue?   • Ground substance is non-cellular and varies widely: solid in bone, liquid in blood, gelatinous — "Jell-O-like" — in cartilage, depending on hydration.

    • Loose (areolar) — he read the slide's name as loose irregular fibrous connective tissue, also areolar. Few fibres around the cells.

Elastic stretches and snaps back — your ears.

L1.8Junctions: duct tape, hand-holding, and a pipe through the wall

4 slides
Big ideaTight junctions seal, adhesion junctions anchor cells to each other and to the basement membrane, gap junctions connect cytoplasms — and the gap junctions in cardiac intercalated discs are why the heart beats as one.
  • "The book says there's three types. I know of a few more, and some of these have multiple examples."
  • Tight junctions — duct tape. They eliminate leakage between cells. If something is on the surface and blood vessels are underneath, he does not want it slipping between the cells into the blood — because once in the blood it has access to everywhere. To get through, it must go through a cell, which means the cell needs a channel in its membrane for it. Found between cells lining the urinary tract and the intestine.
  • Adhesion junctions — anchoring. They attach cells to one another and to the underlying basement membrane, "so that cells don't wander away from where they're supposed to be."

    • Metastasis is the failure case. His analogy: he would be a bad librarian; he talks too loud. A student asked "what's stopping the transfer?" — he deferred it.

  • Gap junctions — a pipe through the wall. Drill a hole between two rooms and put a pipe in it: you can see through, talk through, pass a note through. Gap junctions connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells for cell-to-cell communication.

3 slides
Big ideaTwo or more tissues make an organ, two or more organs a system; and the body's cavities and openings are lined by four kinds of membrane — mucous, serous, synovial, cutaneous.

L1.10The eleven organ systems, with what he added to each

1 slides
Big ideaHe walked Figure 4.7's eleven systems as a preview of the semester, and added a physiological detail to almost every one.
  • Integumentary — protects underlying tissues, provides sensation, regulates temperature by sweating, synthesises vitamin D under UV.
  • Skeletal — muscle attachment, organ protection, stores calcium and phosphorus, and produces all the blood cells.
  • Muscular — with the skeleton, moves the body and holds posture; moves fluids internally; generates a lot of heat.
  • Nervous — regulates and integrates body functions via neurons. "None of that would be possible without it."
  • Endocrine — works hand in hand with the nervous system. "If my nervous system is wired, point-to-point communication, my endocrine system is wireless" — hormones hijack the cardiovascular system for transport.
  • Cardiovascular — transports nutrients, respiratory gases, waste and heat; immune cells, antibodies, hormones; regulates tissue pH.
    • Blood pH is 7.35–7.45. Outside that range things start happening. Metabolic acidosis / alkalosis can be countered by changing rate and depth of breathing — deliberately producing a respiratory alkalosis to undo a metabolic acidosis. "You'll learn so much of that in Bio 22."
  • Lymphatic — wherever there is a capillary bed there is a lymphatic capillary. It also protects against infection through lymphocytes, two of the five leukocytes.
  • Respiratory — exchanges gases with the environment. Why breathe at all? Not for the blood — for the mitochondria: oxygen is the final electron acceptor of the electron transport chain. No oxygen, the chain stops, ATP production stops, "I can't pay my workers, they stop working, I die." (The part of Ch. 3 skipped for now, returning with digestion.)

L2.4The epidermis: alive at the bottom, dead at the top, because diffusion runs out

2 slides
Big ideaThe epidermis has no blood vessels, so only the cells close enough to the dermis for diffusion stay alive — everything above about the fourth cell dies, and that wall of dead keratinocytes is the protection.
  • No blood vessels in the epidermis. He asked and answered it himself, emphatically. Nourishment comes from the dermis, by simple diffusion.
  • The three-cell rule. "Diffusion can only occur across approximately 3 cells. By the time we get to the 4th cell, that cell's not doing too good." No nutrients in, no waste out — so around the fourth cell, cells begin to die.
  • What dying looks like. Organelles degenerate, plasma membranes thicken, the cells flatten and spread out.
  • Stratum corneum — the uppermost layer: 15 to 30 layers of dead, flattened, interlocking keratinocytes. "Your bodies are literally covered in dead cells. Anyone that has ever touched you has touched dead." They protect you in death — that is the point of them.
  • Stratum basale — the deepest layer, against the dermis. Rapidly dividing cells that give rise to keratinocytes, the most numerous cell type in the epidermis, and to melanocytes.
    • He offered stratum germinativum as the other name for it, and said he does not use it — "it's just hard to say."
  • Keratin. Keratinocytes make the protein keratin, package it into vesicles and release it. It maintains structural stability — "so the skin doesn't fall apart" — and it is the same protein your hair and nails are made of.

The gradient, in one line

The cell at the bottom is alive and dividing; the cell at the top is a flat keratin-filled envelope. Same cell, ~15–30 layers of travel apart, and nothing in between changed except its distance from a blood vessel.

L2.10Hair, and why thick skin has no hair

2 slides
Big ideaHair protects and it senses, it grows everywhere except the palms and soles — where the skin is thick and has a fifth layer instead — and every hair you have ever seen is dead.
  • Primary function: protection, against abrasion.
  • It grows over most of the body. "You can touch anywhere on your body, you will touch hair, except for two places" — the palms of the hands and the soles of the feet.
  • Those two places grow thick skin rather than thin skin: the epidermis there has five layers rather than four, and the fifth is the stratum lucidum.
    • So the two facts are the same fact: no hair and an extra layer, in the same two places.
  • Hair is a sense organ. Brushing the hairs on your arm without touching the skin is still felt — the shaft vibrates, the follicle vibrates, and a free nerve ending wrapped around the follicle detects it. That is the hair root plexus.
  • The hair you can see is dead. "The hair's alive deep in your skin." Dead, interlocking keratinocytes, with melanocytes around as well.
  • Down in the follicle: the hair root, sitting in the dermis or even the hypodermis, embedded in a follicle, with a papilla and a matrix of cells dividing like crazy, and melanocytes producing eumelanin, pheomelanin and carotene in the ratio that is your natural hair colour.
  • Same mechanism as the epidermis. As keratinocytes are pushed away from the matrix, they are too far from any blood vessel, simple diffusion is not enough, and they die — before the hair ever breaches the skin surface.
  • Where root becomes shaft: at the duct of the sebaceous gland emptying into the follicle. "Your hair is dead like right here."
  • Two consequences he drew out:
    • Shaving does not hurt because the shaft is dead. "If your hair were alive, none of you would ever shave, ever, anywhere."
    • Hair products cannot make hair healthy. "You're putting makeup on a corpse." They slick the dead keratinocytes down so they lie flat — which is why it looks good — but "be wary of claims that say keep your hair healthy and shiny."
  • The proof you can do yourself: pluck a hair and look at the bottom — it is whitish and a different colour. That whitish part is the living root that was below the surface.

L3.6Back to Chapter 3: metabolism is catabolic and anabolic

L3.8Glycolysis: what goes in, what comes out

L3.9NAD+ as an Uber driver, and OIL RIG

L3.10The transition reaction: one carbon lighter

L3.12The electron transport chain: hot potato, and why you breathe

Big ideaFour complexes pass high-energy electrons down a line, skimming energy off each hand-off to pump protons uphill, and oxygen catches the electron at the end — which is the only reason the whole thing keeps running.
  • Where: the inner membrane of the mitochondrion. He asked for it twice and wanted the specific answer, not "the mitochondrion."
  • The hot potato. Complex I, complex II, Q, complex III, complex IV pass the electron along. Each hand-off skims off some of its energy.
  • That energy pumps protons uphill. H+ is moved from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, "where there's already more of them." Moving against a gradient costs energy — that is what the skimmed energy buys.
  • Oxygen is the terminal (final) electron acceptor. "Oxygen is who is supposed to grab that hot potato when the music stops."
  • No oxygen, no chain. Complex IV holds the electron, nobody takes it, the line stops — and the citric acid cycle backs up behind it.
  • Oxygen plus two protons makes water. ½ O2 + 2 H+ → H2O. The spent electron "goes out to be water."
  • The yield: 32 ATP per glucose, against 2 from glycolysis and 2 from the citric acid cycle — 36 in total. "I could have 36, or I could have four. That's the difference oxygen makes in your life."
  • Where the carriers unload. NADH drops its electrons at complex I; FADH2 does not unload until complex II. Because NADH's electrons pass through more complexes, each NADH yields 3 ATP and each FADH2 yields 2.

This is the only reason you are breathing oxygen right now. For this one step. For its job, to grab the hot potato when the music stops. That is the only reason you're breathing right now.

Chapter 4, in one idea

The body is built in levels, and homeostasis is why. Cells specialise; the specialised cells group into four tissue types; junctions decide what a sheet of those cells can do; two or more tissues make an organ; organs with a shared job make an organ system; there are eleven. Then the chapter picks one system — the skin — and dissects it in full, which works because skin contains all four tissue types at once. And then it explains what all eleven are for: holding the internal environment steady by negative feedback. The worked example of feedback is body temperature, and body temperature runs through the skin. So the chapter's last third uses the machinery its middle third built.

  • Four tissue types, and only four. Epithelial (covers, lines, forms glands), connective (binds, supports, stores, transports), muscle (movement), nervous (conducts). Every organ in the rest of the course is a combination of these.
  • Epithelium is a 3 × 2 grid, not six names — three shapes (squamous, cuboidal, columnar) crossed with two layer counts (simple, stratified).
  • Connective tissue is the odd one out. It is the only tissue defined by its matrix rather than its cells, and it is the only category that contains things as unalike as fat, bone and blood.
  • Three junctions, three verbs. Tight = seal. Adhesion = rivet. Gap = channel. Match each to a tissue that needs it and the whole topic is three facts.
  • Two great cavities. Ventral (thoracic + abdominal, split by the diaphragm; thoracic subdivides into pleural and pericardial) and dorsal (cranial + spinal). Four membranes line them: mucous, serous, synovial, cutaneous.
  • Skin is two layers plus a basement. Epidermis — thin, dead on top, keratinised, no blood vessels. Dermis — thick, vascular, holds the nerves, glands and follicles, does not wear away. Hypodermis — fat, anchoring, and not part of the skin.
  • Feedback is three boxes. Receptor → control center → effector, closing back on the variable. For temperature: thermoreceptors → hypothalamus → sweat glands, dermal vessels and skeletal muscles.

Cram list

  1. Four tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous. Not six, not three.
  2. Epithelium = shape × layers. Squamous/cuboidal/columnar crossed with simple/stratified.
  3. Every epithelium has a free surface and a basement membrane.
  4. A gland is epithelial tissue that secretes. Exocrine = through ducts. Endocrine = no ducts, hormones into tissue fluid.
  5. Connective tissue is defined by its matrix, not its cells. It is the only one that is.
  6. Matrix = protein fibers + ground substance. Ground substance is solid (bone), fluid (blood) or gelatinous (cartilage).
  7. Three fibers, all made by fibroblasts: collagen (resists pulling), elastic (recoils), reticular (branching networks).
  8. Blood is connective tissue. So is bone. So is fat.
  9. Ligament = bone to bone. Tendon = muscle to bone. Both dense connective tissue.
  10. Cartilage is avascular and heals slowly; bone is vascular and heals promptly. Chondrocytes sit in lacunae.
  11. Three cartilages: hyaline (most abundant, gristle, joint ends), elastic (external ear), fibrocartilage (knee, vertebral disks — built for pressure).
  12. Skeletal = striated, many nuclei, voluntary. Cardiac = striated, branching, one nucleus, involuntary, heart only. Smooth = tapered, one nucleus, no striations, involuntary.
  13. Junctions: tight = seal (gut, urinary tract), adhesion = rivet (skin, stretchy tissue), gap = channel (heart, smooth muscle).
  14. Organ = 2+ tissues. Organ system = organs with a shared job. Eleven of them.
  15. Ventral cavity = thoracic + abdominal, split by the diaphragm; thoracic holds the pleural cavities (lungs) and the pericardial cavity (heart). Dorsal = cranial + spinal.
  16. Four membranes: mucous (opens to exterior), serous (closed cavities), synovial (joints), cutaneous (skin).
  17. The epidermis has no blood vessels. The dermis does. Tattoos, blisters and burn pain all follow from that one fact.
  18. Keratin waterproofs the epidermis. New cells come from the deepest epidermal layer and die on the way up. Two weeks to a month, formation to shedding.
  19. The hypodermis is not part of the skin. It holds about half the body's fat.
  20. Everyone has about the same number of melanocytes. The form of melanin and the size and number of granules differ.
  21. Oil glands open onto the hair follicle, not the skin surface. That is why acne works the way it does — and a blackhead is dark from melanin, not dirt.
  22. Skin cancers: basal cell (most common, deepest epidermal layer), squamous cell (second, flattening cells), melanoma (least common, most dangerous, melanocytes). ABCD: asymmetry, border, colour, diameter.
  23. Homeostasis is dynamic — fluctuation dampened around a set point, not constancy.
  24. Receptor → control center → effector. Learn the template, then map it: thermoreceptors → hypothalamus → sweat glands, dermal vessels, skeletal muscles.
  25. "Negative" means opposing, not harmful. Positive feedback drives change further in the same direction and does not promote homeostasis.
  26. Set point **37 °C**. Hyperthermia crisis 42 °C. Hypothermia at or below 35 °C, unconscious at 33 °C, shutdown at 30 °C.
