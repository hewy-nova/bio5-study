1Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

The book refuses to give a one-sentence definition of life. Which pair of counterexamples is its reason?

Answer: b

Both are the book's own: a wet ink spot can fall on another page and reproduce itself almost exactly, and crystals grow, yet neither is alive. Every one-line definition fails against something non-living that meets it, so life is characterised by a list of seven traits rather than defined. The exam trap is the framing: an item asking for "the definition of life" is checking that you know there is none and can give the list instead. The other pairs are plausible-sounding but are not what the chapter argues from.

2Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

Which pair of things named below are both characteristics of life on the book's list of seven?

Answer: b

The seven: made of cells; contain nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates and lipids; grow and reproduce; use energy and raw materials (metabolism); respond to the environment; maintain homeostasis; populations evolve and have adaptive traits. Movement, breathing and consciousness are things some organisms do, not defining traits — plants do none of the three and are alive. Whalen's study guide asks for all seven with examples, so know the list well enough to spot an intruder, not just to recite it.

3Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

A person shivers in the cold and sweats in the heat. Which characteristic of life is this, and what is its defining feature?

Answer: c

Homeostasis is a relatively constant, self-correcting internal environment: the body detects a departure and acts to undo it. Shivering and sweating are the book's own examples. It overlaps with "responding to the environment", which is why option a tempts — but response is the general ability to detect and react to any stimulus (a chameleon striking at prey), while homeostasis is specifically about keeping the inside within limits. Adaptation is a population-level, inherited trait, not a moment-to-moment adjustment. Ch. 4 turns this into receptor → control centre → effector.

4Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

For an organism to respond to its environment, the book says it needs two things. Which pair is it?

Answer: b

Detection plus a means of reaction — in humans, sense organs detect, the nervous system processes, and the skeletal and muscular systems execute. Options a and c describe the human implementation, not the requirement: a single-celled organism with no nerves, brain or skeleton still detects and reacts. Reading the general requirement rather than our particular hardware is what the objective "what is required to do so" is asking for.

5Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

In the Amazon, tall trees grow buttress-like supports and vines climb over other plants to reach light. The book uses these as examples of…

Answer: b

They are adaptive traits — features that help an organism survive and reproduce in its natural environment — produced by natural selection: individuals with beneficial genetic traits leave more offspring, so the trait spreads through the population. Buttresses answer thin topsoil and shallow roots; climbing answers the fight for light. Option c is the tempting near-miss, because a vine growing toward light is a response — but the exam-level point is that the capacity to climb was inherited and selected, which is the evolution characteristic. Note the wording: populations evolve; individuals do not.

6Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

An epiphyte is a plant that…

Answer: c

Epiphytes root on other plants rather than in the ground, living high in the canopy for sunlight — the orchid in Figure 1.1's seventh panel. The book uses it as its adaptive-trait example, so it is a likely matching item. Option b is a vine, which the book mentions in the same paragraph as a different strategy; option a is the soil condition the adaptations answer, not the plant.

7Ch. 11.1
  • textbook

Which statement about evolution matches how the book words the seventh characteristic of life?

Answer: c

The trait is stated at the population level on purpose: "populations of living things have adaptive traits and evolve." An individual is born with its genes and dies with them; what changes is the frequency of traits in the population as those who reproduce more pass theirs on. Option d smuggles the right mechanism onto the wrong unit — the commonest error. Option b adds intention, which selection never has.

8Ch. 11.2
  • textbook

Which grouping of the three domains is correct?

Answer: a

Bacteria and Archaea are the two prokaryotic domains — small single cells with no nucleus and no internal compartments; Eukarya holds everything with a nucleus and organelles, from protists to humans. Option c fails because most protists are single-celled eukaryotes, so cell number does not separate the domains. Domain is the highest level, above kingdom, and only Eukarya is subdivided into the four kingdoms — protists, fungi, plants, animals.

9Ch. 11.2
  • textbook

A biologist finds a single-celled organism with no nucleus living in a boiling hot spring. In which domain does it most likely belong?

Answer: b

No nucleus rules out both Eukarya options immediately. Between the two prokaryotic domains, Figure 1.2's own description separates them: "most [Archaea] live in extreme environments." A hot spring is exactly that cue. Bacteria is the tempting answer because it is the prokaryote everyone knows; the distinguishing detail the figure gives is habitat.

10Ch. 11.2
  • textbook

Which trait puts an animal among the mammals, and which puts it among the primates?

Answer: c

The book gives two mammal criteria — hair, and milk from mammary glands — and a separate primate suite: forward-looking eyes and a particularly well-developed brain. A backbone is the vertebrate criterion, one level up. Opposable thumbs are listed for humans, monkeys and apes, not for all primates. End-of-chapter question 2 tests exactly the eyes/mammal confusion, and the Stop and Think asks which trait you would check on a new rain-forest animal to call it a mammal: hair.

11Ch. 11.2
  • textbook

Put these in order from least inclusive to most inclusive.

Answer: a

Humans sit inside primates, inside mammals, inside vertebrates, inside the animal kingdom — each group nested in the next. Classification shows evolutionary relatedness by successively closer groupings, so the smaller the group, the more recent the shared ancestor. The study guide's "inclusiveness" objective is about exactly this kind of nesting, in both the classification and the organisational ladders.

12Ch. 11.3
  • textbook

All the yellow-bellied marmots living in one alpine meadow are a…

Answer: b

One species, one place: a population. Add the other species that can interact there — the grasses, the hawks — and it is a community; add the meadow's physical environment (soil, water, climate) and it is an ecosystem. Each level is the previous one plus exactly one ingredient, which is how end-of-chapter question 3 is built. "Species" is the tempting distractor because the marmots are one species, but the term for the group in a place is population.

13Ch. 11.3
  • textbook

Which of the following is the smallest level that includes non-living components?

Answer: d

Up to community, the levels are entirely organisms — one species, then many. The ecosystem is the first level defined to include the physical environment, and the biosphere is all ecosystems together. The book notes an ecosystem's size is set by whoever studies it — a rotting log or the whole Earth — but whatever its size it is viewed as relatively self-contained.

14Ch. 11.3
  • textbook

Figure 1.3's ladder has twelve levels; the section text lists only five within the individual. Which three levels does the figure add below and around "cell"?

Answer: a

The text runs molecules → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems; the figure starts lower, at atom and molecule, inserts organelle between molecule and cell, and adds "individual" above organ system. The chapter tab records this as an unresolved conflict. If asked to order the levels, give the twelve-level ladder — the five-level list is a subset of it, and the study guide asks you to order them.

15Ch. 11.3
  • textbook

If Earth were the size of a basketball, the biosphere would be about…

Answer: b

The book's own image. The biosphere extends only about 11 km (7 miles) above sea level and the same distance below, to the deepest trenches — a thin film on a planet 12,700 km across. The point is how narrow the zone is where light, minerals, water and gases combine to make life possible.

16Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

According to the book, which statement about the scientific method is accurate?

Answer: c

The book says there is no single "the scientific method" — it is a way of learning about the natural world by applying rules of logic to how information is gathered and conclusions are drawn. It usually begins with an observation, not a hypothesis; the numbered steps are a convenience; and no experiment proves a hypothesis true, only supports it. Three of the four options are the folk version of the method.

17Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

"The full moon makes people behave irrationally" is not a good scientific question, according to the book, because it…

Answer: b

The book's requirement is that questions be reasonable and consistent with existing knowledge; the full-moon claim rests on folk belief with no known mechanism. It could be measured (count emergency-room visits by moon phase), so option a fails — measurability is not the problem. The other three questions in that end-of-chapter item (obesity and life expectancy, dietary fat and breast cancer, temperature and enzyme rate) each propose a testable relationship between two defined variables.

18Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

Which statement about a hypothesis does the book make outright?

Answer: b

Data can support a hypothesis but never prove it, because a future observation could contradict it; one clean disconfirming result, however, can show it false. That asymmetry is why science works by ruling out alternative hypotheses rather than by confirming a favourite. It is stated flatly in §1.4 and is the classic exam item on this section.

19Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

A drug trial randomly splits patients into two groups; one gets the drug, one a placebo, and blood pressure is measured after six weeks. Identify the independent and the dependent variable.

Answer: b

The independent variable is the one factor the researcher deliberately changes between groups — drug or no drug; the dependent variable is what is measured because it depends on that change — blood pressure. The book pins them to the axes of a graph: independent on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis. Option a reverses them, the single most common error; the placebo is the control treatment, not a variable in its own right, and six weeks is a fixed condition.

20Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

In the oatmeal study the control group ate farina, a wheat cereal with no β-glucan. What is the purpose of choosing farina rather than nothing at all?

Answer: b

A controlled experiment treats both groups identically except for the one independent variable. If the control group ate nothing, the groups would differ in eating a daily bowl of cereal at all — calories, routine, attention — and any LDL difference could be due to that. Farina matches everything except the β-glucan. Option d describes blinding, a separate device; option a would ruin the control.

21Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

Volunteers for the oatmeal study were assigned to groups at random rather than choosing. The reason is to avoid…

Answer: c

People who choose oatmeal probably differ from those who choose farina in ways that also affect cholesterol — diet, exercise, motivation. Those uncontrolled differences are confounding variables, and with them present you cannot say which factor caused the result. Random assignment spreads such differences evenly, leaving the cereal as the only systematic difference. This is the Stop and Think on p. 7, and the study guide has an objective on confounders specifically.

22Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

An experiment's result is reported as p < 0.05. This means…

Answer: b

Statistical significance measures the possibility that the results arose by chance; a probability under 5% is generally accepted, and the lower the number, the more confidence. It says nothing about the size of the effect (a tiny difference can be significant with enough subjects) and never amounts to proof. The oatmeal study's end-of-treatment differences were significant from the starting values — which is what let the book draw a conclusion.

23Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

In the oatmeal experiment the results were shown as a bar graph, but the follow-up study used a line graph. Why the change?

Answer: c

The book chooses the graph by the data: bars for discrete categories (control vs experimental at one time point), a line for change over time (LDL at the start, at 6 weeks on cereal, at 12 weeks after stopping). Reading a graph starts with the axes — x is the independent variable, y the dependent — and the follow-up's x-axis is weeks. Expect to be handed a graph and asked what it shows.

24Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

The follow-up oatmeal study found LDL fell more with 2 or 3 oz of oatmeal than with 1 oz, and crept back up after the cereal stopped. What does the second finding add?

Answer: b

The reversal shows the cholesterol-lowering is a consequence of ongoing oatmeal consumption, not a permanent change — stop the cause and the effect fades. The dose finding (more oatmeal, more effect) and the reversal together make the causal case far stronger than a single before-and-after difference could. Farina's line stayed flat throughout, which is what a proper control looks like on the graph.

25Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

The cell theory ("all cells come from pre-existing cells") is called a theory rather than a hypothesis because it…

Answer: c

A theory is a well-supported, wide-ranging explanation formed when many related hypotheses have been confirmed repeatedly and fit together; because of its breadth no single experiment could test it, which is why option b fails. It still leads to new predictions and remains open to revision — option d describes dogma, not science. The everyday sense of "just a theory" is the reverse of the scientific one, and end-of-chapter questions 5 and 8 are built as mirror images to catch the swap with "hypothesis".

26Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

A student notices that every dog she has ever met has had a wet nose, and concludes that dogs have wet noses. Later she reasons: "if dogs have wet noses, then the next dog I meet will too." Name the two kinds of reasoning, in order.

Answer: b

Accumulating specific observations until a general statement seems justified is induction — how hypotheses are generated. Starting from the general statement and reasoning to a specific if–then expectation is deduction — how predictions are made from a hypothesis. The oatmeal prediction ("if oatmeal lowers cholesterol, then a person eating it for 6 weeks will…") is the book's deduction example. The study guide asks for an example of each, so be able to produce one in each direction.

27Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

A new asthma drug has passed animal testing. In which clinical-trial phase is it first given to people, and to whom?

Answer: b

Phase I asks only whether the drug is safe in humans and at what dose — so it uses a small group of healthy people, fewer than 100. Phase II asks whether it works, so it moves to a few hundred people with the disease. Phase III asks how it compares with existing treatments, in thousands. Table 1.1 states the three questions in that order, and the FDA approves only drugs that pass all three. The Consider This Case (Maribel) asks you to design all three phases.

28Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

Two patients in a trial receive identical-looking pills; one contains the drug, one contains sugar. Neither patient knows which. The purpose of the sugar pill is to…

Answer: b

People often improve because they believe they are being treated. A placebo — an innocuous look-alike — gives the control group that belief too, so any extra improvement in the drug group is the drug's. Option d is what double-blinding adds: keeping the researchers unaware as well, so their expectations cannot colour how they record results. The book gives those two reasons separately, one per side.

29Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

A trial is single-blind if the participants do not know who received the drug. Making it double-blind additionally prevents…

Answer: b

Double-blind means neither participants nor researchers know the assignment. The extra blind exists because a researcher who wants a result may, without meaning to, record or interpret ambiguous outcomes in its favour. Option c is handled by the placebo itself; option d by random assignment. The study guide lists placebo and double-blind as separate objectives — keep their two jobs separate.

30Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

Which document must a participant sign before a clinical trial, and what must it contain?

Answer: b

Informed consent is legally required: the document lists every possible harmful effect and must be signed before participation, and the participant must be mentally capable of understanding the treatment and its risks. It exists because the first human phases are precisely where safety is not yet known. It is on the study guide's term list.

31Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

Researchers compare asthma hospital admissions with daily air-pollution readings across a city over ten years. What kind of study is this, and what can it show?

Answer: b

Nobody assigned anyone to breathe polluted air — the researchers looked for a pattern in a large population as it is, which is an epidemiological study. Its value is that it can examine exposures you could never ethically impose; its limit is that with no controlled variables it can show only correlation, never cause. The book's cell-phone example makes the point: early studies hinted at a link in heavy users, a larger one found no dose–response, and none can settle the matter alone.

32Ch. 11.4
  • textbook

A 350,000-person study found brain-tumour rates did not rise with years of cell-phone use and were no higher in the most-exposed brain regions. The book calls this a lack of…

Answer: c

If a factor causes a disease, more exposure should generally mean more disease — a dose–response relationship, like more oatmeal giving a bigger LDL drop. Its absence in the newer study is the strongest reason the book gives for doubting a link, while still noting that cancers take years to develop, so research continues. Recognising dose–response as evidence for causation, and its absence as evidence against, is the deeper skill behind the "correlation vs cause" objective.

33Ch. 11.5
  • textbook

A headline reads "Fish Oil Increases Longevity." The study behind it fed rats a high-fish-oil diet. The book identifies two separate flaws in taking a fish-oil capsule on the strength of it. Which pair?

Answer: a

Species: rat physiology does not all generalise — rats are more resistant to heart disease than we are. 34Ch. 11.5

People who eat fish three times a week live longer than those who do not. The book says this alone does not show fish causes longevity because…

35Ch. 11.5

Two websites make opposite claims about a supplement. One ends in .gov and reports a controlled study; the other ends in .com and quotes customer testimonials. Using Table 1.2 and the book's checklist, which is the stronger source, and for which two reasons?

1Ch. 1short answer
  • textbook

List the seven characteristics of life and give one example of each. Then explain why the book lists characteristics instead of defining life.

Model answer

(1) Contain nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates and lipids — DNA in every cell. (2) Composed of cells — a bacterium is one cell, a human trillions. (3) Grow and reproduce — an embryo becomes an adult; bacteria copy themselves. (4) Use energy and raw materials, i.e. metabolism — extracting energy from food. (5) Respond to the environment — a chameleon striking at prey. (6) Maintain homeostasis — shivering when cold, sweating when hot. (7) Populations evolve and have adaptive traits — buttress roots and epiphytes in the rain forest. The book lists characteristics because every one-sentence definition fails against a non-living counterexample: an ink spot "reproduces", a crystal "grows". No single trait is unique to life; the full set is.

Seven, each with a two-word example, then the ink-spot/crystal reason. The examples show you understand the trait rather than the label, and the last sentence is what separates a list from an answer.

2Ch. 1short answer
  • textbook

Describe the design of the oatmeal experiment from question to conclusion, naming the hypothesis, the prediction, the independent and dependent variables, the control, and what the result showed. Then say what the follow-up added.

Model answer

Question: does eating oatmeal lower blood cholesterol? Hypothesis: β-glucan in oatmeal lowers blood cholesterol. Prediction: if so, then a person eating a bowl of oatmeal a day for 6 weeks will lower their LDL. Adults with LDL between 4.5 and 5.5 mmol/L were randomly assigned: the experimental group ate 1 oz of oatmeal daily, the control group 1 oz of farina, a cereal identical except for lacking β-glucan. Independent variable: the cereal (oatmeal vs farina). Dependent variable: blood LDL, measured at the start and at 6 weeks. Result: LDL fell more in the oatmeal group, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05), supporting — not proving — the hypothesis. The follow-up used four groups (farina, 1, 2, 3 oz) and measured again 6 weeks after stopping: the effect increased with dose and reversed when the cereal stopped, which strengthens the causal case and shows the benefit depends on continuing to eat it.

Every named piece is a mark: hypothesis vs prediction as separate statements, both variables correctly assigned, why farina and why random assignment, "supports not proves", and the dose–response plus reversal from the follow-up.

3Ch. 1short answer
  • textbook

Explain what a confounding variable is, give an example from the chapter, and describe the design feature that guards against it.

Model answer

A confounding variable is an additional factor, not controlled for, that differs between the groups and could have caused the observed effect — so that you cannot tell which variable was responsible. The chapter's example is self-selection: if volunteers chose the oatmeal or farina group themselves, those who chose oatmeal might also eat better, exercise more or be more health-conscious, and any LDL difference could be due to those habits rather than to β-glucan. The guard is random assignment to groups, which distributes such differences evenly, together with treating both groups identically in every respect but the independent variable (the matched farina control). Correlation studies cannot do this, which is why they stop short of cause.

Define it as an uncontrolled difference between groups, give the self-selection example, then random assignment and the matched control. The last sentence links it to the epidemiology objective.

4Ch. 1short answer
  • textbook

A new drug has shown promise in animals. Describe the three phases of clinical trials it must pass, stating for each who takes part and what question is being answered. Then explain the roles of the placebo, double-blinding and informed consent.

Model answer

Phase I: fewer than 100 healthy volunteers — is the drug safe for humans, at what dose range, with what side effects? Phase II: a few hundred people with the target disease — does it work for its intended purpose? Phase III: thousands of participants — how does it compare with existing treatments? The FDA approves only drugs that pass all three, a process that usually takes more than 8 years. A placebo is an inactive substance made to look like the drug, given to the randomly assigned control group so that improvement caused merely by expecting treatment can be subtracted from the drug's real effect. Double-blinding keeps both participants and researchers unaware of who received what, because participants' expectations can affect their response and researchers' expectations can affect how they interpret data. Informed consent is a legally required document listing every possible harmful effect, signed before participation by someone capable of understanding the risks.

Three phases with participants and question each, then placebo (subtracts expectation), double-blind (both sides, two reasons), consent (signed, lists harms). All five are separate objectives on the study guide.

5Ch. 1short answer
  • textbook

Explain the difference between correlation and cause using the fish-and-longevity example, and say why epidemiological studies are nonetheless valuable.

Model answer

A correlation is a relationship in which two factors change together; cause means one actually produces the other. People who eat fish three times a week live longer — a correlation — but that does not show fish causes longevity, because a third factor could produce both: fish-eaters may exercise more, hold less stressful jobs, or live where there is less pollution. Only a controlled experiment, which holds other factors constant and assigns the treatment, can establish cause. Epidemiological studies — looking for patterns of disease across large populations — are still valuable because they can examine exposures no one could ethically assign (air pollution, smoking, cell-phone use) and can point to links worth testing; and a dose–response relationship in such data, or its absence, is real evidence either way.

Definitions, the example with a named third factor, the controlled-experiment contrast, then two reasons epidemiology matters. The third-factor sentence is the one graders look for.

1Ch. 22.1
  • textbook

An astronaut whose mass is 68 kg on Earth stands on the Moon, where gravity is weaker. Which statement uses the book's terms correctly?

Answer: b

Mass is how much matter an object contains, measured in grams or kilograms, and the amount of matter in the astronaut did not change on the trip. Weight is the pull of gravity on that mass, so it is weight that drops on the Moon. Option a is the everyday swap — people say "weigh" when a scale is really reporting mass on Earth, where the two track each other. The same matter can be solid, liquid or gas; the book's three traditional states are a change of arrangement, not of mass.

2Ch. 22.1
  • textbook

Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass. Which of the following is therefore not matter?

Answer: a

Light is energy moving through space — the book's own definition of radiation — and has no mass, so it fails the test. The other three are the three states of matter: steam is water as a gas, helium is a gas, ice is a solid; a gas is still matter even though you cannot see it, which is why option c tempts. All matter is made of atoms, the units that ordinary chemical means cannot break down further. This is the first sentence of §2.1 and the study guide's first objective, so expect a definition item on it.

3Ch. 22.1
  • textbook

A neutral atom has 8 protons. How many electrons does it have, and how does Figure 2.1 arrange them?

Answer: c

A neutral atom has as many electrons (negative, outside the nucleus) as protons (positive, in the nucleus), so eight. The innermost shell holds at most 2, the next at most 8, so the eight fill as 2 then 6 — the flat oxygen in Figure 2.1. Option d is the "split evenly" instinct; the shells are not equal in capacity. Shells are energy levels: the electrons with the most energy are the farthest out, and the flat circles are a convenience for a three-dimensional electron cloud.

4Ch. 22.1
  • textbook

Helium (2 electrons) is inert; sodium (11 electrons) reacts violently with water. Which single feature of the atom explains the difference?

Answer: c

Helium's two electrons exactly fill its first shell, so it has no reason to interact; sodium's eleventh electron sits alone in a third shell that could hold eight, so it reacts to get rid of it. The book calls this the sentence everything else depends on: the number of electrons in the outermost shell determines an atom's chemical properties, and atoms whose outer shells are not full tend to interact. Options a and d change with every element without predicting reactivity; neutrons affect mass only. Bonding in §2.2 and water's polarity in §2.3 are both consequences of this one fact.

  • textbook

Fluorine has an atomic number of 9 and an atomic mass of 19. Which count is right?

Answer: c

Atomic number is the proton count, so 9. Atomic mass is protons plus neutrons (each about 1 amu; electrons count as zero), so neutrons = 19 − 9 = 10. A neutral atom matches electrons to protons: 9, arranged 2 and 7. Option a swaps the neutron and electron arithmetic; option d forgets to subtract the protons. This is the p. 23 Stop and Think, and end-of-chapter question 1's wrong statement is "protons plus electrons equals the atomic mass".

12C, 13C and 14C are all carbon. What differs between them, and what stays the same?

Answer: a

Isotopes are atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons: all three carbons have 6 protons, with 6, 7 or 8 neutrons giving masses 12, 13 and 14. Changing the proton count (option b) would make a different element, not an isotope. Some isotopes are unstable and give off radiation as they break down — the radioisotopes, about 60 of which occur naturally — which is how the chapter gets from atomic structure to medicine.

A patient is given radioactive iodine (131I). Why does it act on the thyroid rather than the whole body, and what decides whether it images the gland or destroys tissue?

Answer: d

The thyroid takes up iodine to regulate growth and metabolism, so any iodine — radioactive or not — concentrates there. Fluorine has 7 electrons in its outer shell. Which element would you expect to react most like it, and why?

Answer: b

The periodic table's columns (groups) collect elements with the same number of outermost-shell electrons, and because that count governs reactivity, members of a group behave alike — so fluorine's column-mates chlorine, bromine and iodine all need exactly one electron. Option d is the clever near-miss: sodium is one electron from a full shell too, but one too many, so it gives an electron away rather than taking one — opposite behaviour. Rows do not predict reactivity. Read the table by column for behaviour and by row for shell count.

Which pair is classified correctly by the book's definitions of molecule and compound?

Answer: c

A molecule is any structure held together by covalent bonds; a compound is a substance formed from two or more elements. Two oxygen atoms bonded together are a molecule, but one element cannot make a compound. Water has two elements and covalent bonds, so it is both. Carbon has 4 outer electrons and room for 8; hydrogen has 1 and room for 2. Predict how many hydrogens one carbon bonds to in methane, and what kind of bond forms.

Answer: b

Carbon needs four more electrons and each hydrogen can share one, so four hydrogens fill carbon's shell while carbon's shared electrons fill each hydrogen's — every one of the five atoms ends full, and nothing has been given away. That is a covalent bond, and CH4 is Table 2.2's example of the strongest bond type. Option c has the right count with the wrong mechanism: transfer makes ions, and neither carbon nor hydrogen gains a full shell by losing or taking four electrons. Compounds form because unfilled outer shells drive atoms to share or transfer until they are full.

Rank the three bond types from strongest to weakest, as Table 2.2 does, and name the reason the middle one is weaker than the top.

Answer: d

Covalent (sharing) is strongest, ionic (transfer, then attraction of opposite charges) is strong, hydrogen (attraction between partial charges) is weak — which is why hydrogen bonds are drawn dotted. A label reads 3C12H22O11. What does the notation say, and what would a structural formula add that this cannot?

Figure 2.8 draws methane, carbon dioxide and nitrogen gas. Which matches each to its bond type?

Answer: c

The count is the number of shared pairs per bond. Methane's carbon shares one pair with each of four hydrogens — four single bonds. Sodium's electrons are arranged 2, 8, 1. After it becomes Na+, how does the book describe its shells?

Which sequence correctly orders the events of ionic bonding in sodium chloride (Figure 2.9)?

In a water molecule the shared electrons spend more time near the oxygen. What follows for the molecule as a whole?

Answer: a

Unequal sharing in a covalent bond makes the bond polar, and a molecule with unevenly distributed charge is a polar molecule: the electron-rich oxygen end is δ−, the hydrogen ends δ+, and the molecule is still neutral overall. Nonpolar molecules such as lipids share evenly and have no such regions, which is why oil and water do not mix. A student asked Whalen whether one base "takes more of the hydrogen" when a C–G pair's three hydrogen bonds break. His answer, and the reason?

Answer: c

A hydrogen bond is not shared electrons (covalent) and not transferred electrons (ionic); it is the attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen and a slightly negative atom nearby, usually oxygen or nitrogen. Table salt dissolves in water. Which description matches Figure 2.11 and uses the terms correctly?

Answer: b

The solvent does the dissolving (water), the solute is what is dissolved (the salt), and the result is a solution. Water can pull NaCl apart into independent ions because it is polar: sodium ions are attracted to the negative (oxygen) regions of water molecules and chloride ions to the positive (hydrogen) regions, so in Figure 2.11 the oxygens face Na+ and the hydrogens face Cl- — orientation is the whole point of the figure.   • textbook

About 67% of the human body is water — roughly 45 kg of a 68 kg person. The book uses this figure to explain why we…

Answer: d

Water has a high heat capacity: raising its temperature takes a great deal of heat because the hydrogen bonds holding its molecules together must be broken first. A body that is two-thirds water is therefore slow to warm or cool, and blood redistributes that heat — fingers do not usually freeze on a frigid day because blood carries heat to them from the muscles. Option a is a real property of water but the wrong one (heat of vaporisation), and pH is a separate mechanism. Note the number: 67% for the whole body; the 78% (brain) and 25% (bone) figures in the Did You Know panel are tissue values, and end-of-chapter question 6 uses "about 25%" as a whole-body distractor.

  • textbook

Carter, arriving from a northern state, collapses at a hot, humid first football practice with confusion and slurred speech. Why does the humidity in particular make heat stroke likely?

Answer: a

The body's main defence against overheating is water's high heat of vaporisation: evaporating sweat carries away a great deal of heat because hydrogen bonds must break for each molecule to leave the liquid. Humidity does not change that property (option c) — it blocks the evaporation itself, so the heat stays. Confusion and slurred speech are the brain signs of heat stroke, a rise usually to 40 °C (104 °F) or higher; the very young, the very old, and anyone doing strenuous work in heat they are unaccustomed to are at risk, and the book names heat plus humidity as particularly dangerous. This is the Consider This Case, so it is a likely scenario item.

Beer is about pH 3 (Figure 2.12). Compared with a solution at pH 5, beer has…

Answer: c

pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen-ion concentration, so each unit is a tenfold change: at pH 5 the concentration is 1 × 10-5 moles per litre, at pH 3 it is 1 × 10-3 — two steps, 10 × 10 = 100 times more H+, and lower pH is more acidic. What would hydrolysis of a triglyceride yield, and how many water molecules are used?

Answer: b

A triglyceride is one glycerol joined to three fatty acids, each attached by dehydration synthesis, which released one water per bond. Hydrolysis is the exact reverse: a water is added across each covalent bond, the H going to one side and the OH to the other, so three bonds take three waters and give back one glycerol and three free fatty acids. Option d is a phospholipid, not a fat. Carbohydrates, fats and proteins all contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Which class always contains a fourth element, and from which part of its monomer does it come?

Answer: d

Every amino acid carries an amino group, NH2, on its central carbon, so every protein contains nitrogen. Which line puts glucose, sucrose and glycogen in the right carbohydrate classes?

Carbohydrates are classified by size. Which describes a fatty acid, and through which part does it attach to glycerol?

Answer: d

A fatty acid is a chain of carbons bonded to hydrogens with the acidic group COOH at one end, and it bonds to glycerol by dehydration synthesis at that carboxyl end (Figure 2.17). The book says one count separates a storage molecule from a membrane molecule. Which is it?

Answer: b

A triglyceride is glycerol plus three fatty acids; a phospholipid is glycerol plus two fatty acids and a negatively charged phosphate group carrying a small polar variable group (R). Swapping one tail for a phosphate gives the molecule a polar, hydrophilic head and a nonpolar, hydrophobic tail — two regions on one molecule — and that is what lets phospholipids form a bilayer while triglycerides just pool as fat. A fever or a pH shift "unravels" a protein. Which statement matches the book on denaturation?

Answer: a

Denaturation is loss of shape: heat or a pH change breaks the weak bonds — hydrogen, ionic, side-chain interactions — that hold the secondary, tertiary and quaternary levels, the molecule unravels, and because function depends on shape even a minor change can mean loss of function. Compare covalent, ionic and hydrogen bonds: what holds each together, how strong each is, and one biological example of each. Then explain why the weakest one still matters.

Model answer

Covalent: two or more atoms share electrons in their outer shells (equally or unequally); the strongest bond; example, the C–H bonds of methane or the peptide bond between amino acids — which Whalen said is simply a covalent bond. Ionic: one atom transfers electrons to another, creating oppositely charged ions whose mutual attraction is the bond; strong, but weaker than covalent because nothing is shared; example, Na+ and Cl- in table salt. Define acid and base with the book's examples, explain the pH scale, and describe how the blood's buffer holds pH at 7.4. Say why that matters.

Model answer

Sometimes water dissociates: H–O–H ⇌ H+ + OH-. An acid is anything that releases H+ in water (HCl → H+ + Cl-) and so raises H+ concentration; a base is anything that releases OH- (NaOH → Na+ + OH-), and the OH- combines with H+ to form water, lowering H+ concentration. pH is the negative logarithm of H+ concentration on a scale of 0 to 14: 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic, and each unit is a tenfold change, so pH 4 has 10 times the H+ of pH 5 and 100 times that of pH 6. A buffer keeps pH from changing dramatically by removing excess H+ when it rises and adding H+ when it falls. Blood uses the carbonic acid–bicarbonate system, CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-: extra H+ combines with bicarbonate and leaves solution; a shortage makes carbonic acid dissociate to supply more. It matters because almost every reaction in the body runs near pH 7, enzymes are proteins that denature outside their range, and a drop from 7.4 to 7.0 or a rise to 7.8 can cause death in minutes. The stomach is the exception at pH 1–3, from HCl — an acid, not a buffer.

Acid and base each with the equation and the effect on H+, "logarithmic — tenfold per unit", the buffer written as a reversible equation that runs both ways, the 7.4 / 7.0 / 7.8 numbers, and the reason (enzymes). Saying HCl is the buffer loses the mark end-of-chapter question 11 is built on.

3Ch. 2short answer
  • textbook

Describe the four levels of protein structure, naming what holds each and one consequence the book gives of altering it. Then explain what denaturation does and does not do.

Model answer

Primary structure is the sequence of amino acids, joined by peptide bonds and determined by the genes; it dictates all the higher levels, and substituting a single amino acid in haemoglobin causes sickle-cell anaemia, whose misshapen protein deforms red blood cells that then clog small vessels. Secondary structure is the helices and pleated sheets formed by bends and coils in the chain, held by hydrogen bonds; altering the secondary structure of a nerve-cell surface protein can turn it into an infectious prion (Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, mad cow disease). Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional shape, produced by hydrogen, ionic and covalent bonds between the side chains (R groups); it is the level that gives an enzyme's active site its shape. Quaternary structure exists only in proteins with two or more polypeptide subunits and is the assembled subunits, held largely by attractions between oppositely charged variable groups — haemoglobin has four. Denaturation, from heat or a pH change, breaks the weak bonds holding levels two to four, so the molecule unravels, loses its shape and therefore its function; it does not break the peptide bonds of the primary sequence — that is hydrolysis, which is digestion, not denaturation.

Four levels each with what holds it and the book's own example (sickle cell, prion, active site, haemoglobin's four subunits), then denaturation as loss of shape and function at levels 2–4 with the primary sequence intact. The "does not" half is the distinction most answers miss.

4Ch. 2short answer
  • textbook

Using E + S → ES → E + P, explain how an enzyme works and why it is specific, and describe the roles of cofactors and coenzymes with an example of each.

Model answer

An enzyme (E) — almost always a protein — speeds a chemical reaction without being consumed; without enzymes most reactions in cells would be far too slow to sustain life, and with them they run 10,000 to a million times faster. The substrate (S) binds a specific location on the enzyme, the active site, forming the enzyme–substrate complex (ES); binding orients the substrate so it can react; the product (P) is released and the enzyme, unchanged, binds another substrate — about 1000 per second for a typical enzyme. Maltase splitting maltose into glucose is the book's example, and enzymes also run synthesis reactions, joining two substrates into one product. Specificity comes from shape: each enzyme binds only one or a few substrates because its active site and the substrate fit like jigsaw pieces. Cofactors are non-protein substances that help the enzyme convert substrate to product — some permanently at the active site, some binding with the substrate; inorganic cofactors include zinc and iron. Coenzymes are the organic, carbon-containing cofactors — the vitamins. Because the enzyme is a protein, heat or the wrong pH denatures it, the active site changes shape, and the substrate no longer fits — which is why blood pH is buffered so tightly.

Each letter of the equation named, "not consumed", the two speed numbers, active site and fit as the basis of specificity, cofactor (zinc, iron) versus coenzyme (vitamin) with the subset relation right, and the link to denaturation. The maltase example shows you can read the equation, not just recite it.

Name the four classes of macromolecule, the monomer or building block of each, one example, and the main functions the book gives each. Then explain how all of them are built and broken down.

Model answer

Carbohydrates — monosaccharides such as glucose; examples sucrose (a disaccharide), starch, glycogen and cellulose (polysaccharides); fuel for the body, with glycogen as the animal store in muscle and liver, starch the plant store, cellulose the plant structural polysaccharide and our dietary fibre. Lipids — not a true polymer of one repeating monomer, but built from glycerol and fatty acids (triglycerides: one glycerol plus three fatty acids; phospholipids: glycerol, two fatty acids and a phosphate) or on the four-ring steroid skeleton (cholesterol); long-term energy storage at twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate, cushioning and insulation, the plasma membrane bilayer, and steroid hormones. 6Ch. 2short answer
  • textbook

Explain why water is polar and how that leads to hydrogen bonding, then use those ideas to account for three properties of water the body depends on. Finish with what went wrong for Carter at football practice.

Model answer

In water's covalent bonds the shared electrons spend more time near the oxygen, so the oxygen carries a slight negative charge and each hydrogen a slight positive one — the molecule is polar, with positive and negative regions but no net charge. The slightly positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen of another: a hydrogen bond, weak individually but numerous. Three consequences: (1) water is an excellent solvent — its charged regions surround and separate ions and other polar molecules (Figure 2.11, oxygens facing Na+, hydrogens facing Cl-), which with its cohesion makes it the body's transport medium, the liquid part of blood and urine; (2) high heat capacity — a great deal of heat is needed to raise its temperature because hydrogen bonds must be broken, so a body that is about 67% water resists temperature change and blood redistributes heat; (3) high heat of vaporisation — evaporation also requires breaking hydrogen bonds, so evaporating sweat carries away a great deal of heat and cools us. Carter, unaccustomed to heat, exercised hard on a hot and humid day; humidity — water vapour already in the air — inhibits the evaporation of sweat, so the cooling mechanism failed, his body temperature rose toward 40 °C or higher, and the confusion and slurred speech were signs of heat stroke damaging the brain.

Unequal sharing → partial charges → hydrogen bonds, stated in that order; three properties each traced back to hydrogen bonds and each given its consequence for the body; then the case, with humidity blocking evaporation as the specific failure. "Polar, so hydrogen bonds" is the sentence every mark hangs on.

Surface area scales with the square of the side and volume with the cube, so doubling the side multiplies surface by four but volume by eight — the ratio halves. A red blood cell is placed in (a) distilled water, (b) a 10% salt solution, (c) 0.9% saline. Predict what happens in each case and explain why, using the terms hypotonic, hypertonic and isotonic.

Model answer

(a) Distilled water is hypotonic to the cell — it has a lower solute concentration than the cytoplasm — so water moves in by osmosis, toward the higher solute concentration inside; the cell swells and bursts (haemolysis). (b) 10% salt is hypertonic — a higher solute concentration than the cytoplasm — so water moves out of the cell toward the solute; the cell shrinks and its membrane wrinkles (crenation). (c) 0.9% saline is isotonic — the same solute concentration as the cytoplasm — so water crosses the membrane at equal rates in both directions and the cell keeps its shape, which is why isotonic saline is used for intravenous fluids. In every case only water moves; the membrane is selectively permeable and the salt stays where it is.

Full credit names the tonicity of each solution, states the direction water moves and why (toward the higher solute concentration), and gives the result with its term — haemolysis, crenation, no net change. The sentence that shows understanding rather than memorised outcomes is "water moves toward the higher solute concentration"; the sentence that loses marks is any version of "salt moves into the cell".

Trace the path of a protein that will be secreted from the cell, from the gene to the outside, naming each structure it passes through.

The gene is transcribed into mRNA in the nucleus; the mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore in the nuclear envelope and is read by a ribosome that docks on the rough ER; the growing polypeptide is threaded into the ER, where it folds and receives its first modifications; a transport vesicle buds from the ER and carries it to the Golgi complex, which modifies the protein further, sorts it by destination, and packages it into a secretory vesicle; the vesicle travels along the cytoskeleton to the plasma membrane, fuses with it, and releases the protein into the extracellular fluid by exocytosis, the vesicle membrane becoming part of the plasma membrane.

3Ch. 3short answer
  • textbook
  • lab manual

Compare passive and active transport. Give two examples of each, and say where endocytosis and exocytosis belong and why.

Model answer

Passive transport moves substances down their concentration gradient using no cellular energy: simple diffusion (oxygen and carbon dioxide straight through the lipid bilayer), facilitated diffusion (glucose through a carrier protein, ions through channel proteins) and osmosis (water across a selectively permeable membrane toward the higher solute concentration). Active transport moves substances against their gradient and requires ATP through a carrier protein: the sodium–potassium pump, which keeps sodium low and potassium high inside every cell, and the uptake of nutrients by intestinal cells already rich in them. Endocytosis (phagocytosis of a bacterium by a white blood cell, pinocytosis of fluid droplets) and exocytosis (release of hormones, digestive enzymes or neurotransmitters) move bulk material in vesicles rather than molecule by molecule; they belong on the active side because forming and moving a vesicle costs ATP, even though nothing is being moved against a gradient.

Two contrasts carry the marks — direction (down versus against the gradient) and energy (none versus ATP) — followed by named examples with the molecule and the route for each. The vesicle processes must be placed with active transport with the reason given: they cost energy. A common error is to call facilitated diffusion active because it uses a protein; the protein is the help, the gradient is the power.

Describe the four steps of aerobic respiration — where each occurs, what goes in and comes out, and how much ATP each yields. Then explain the role of oxygen and what happens to a cell that runs out of it.

This is the study guide's objective almost word for word: four steps with location, inputs and outputs, and ATP at each; then oxygen's role stated as final electron acceptor (Whalen's "why do you breathe? for the mitochondria"), then fermentation as glycolysis plus lactic acid with its 2-ATP yield. 5Ch. 3short answer
  • textbook

Compare the rough and smooth ER, and explain how a lysosomal storage disease such as Tay-Sachs arises.

Model answer

Both are networks of membranous channels continuous with the nuclear envelope. The rough ER is studded with ribosomes and makes and begins processing the proteins destined for export, for membranes and for lysosomes; the smooth ER has no ribosomes and makes lipids including steroid hormones, stores calcium in muscle cells, and detoxifies drugs and alcohol in liver cells. Lysosomes are vesicles of digestive enzymes made by the Golgi; they fuse with engulfed material and with worn-out organelles and break them down, and their membrane keeps the enzymes from digesting the cell itself. In a lysosomal storage disease one of those enzymes is missing or inactive because of an inherited mutation, so the one substance it should break down cannot be broken down and accumulates inside the lysosomes until the cell is disabled or destroyed. In Tay-Sachs the missing enzyme degrades a lipid in nerve cells; the lipid builds up in the brain, the neurons are destroyed, and the child loses sight, movement and mental function, usually dying by about age five. It is autosomal recessive, so two unaffected carrier parents can have an affected child.

Ribosomes-or-not and protein-versus-lipid are the ER contrast, with the smooth ER's detox and calcium jobs as the extra marks. For the disease, the chain "missing enzyme → substrate not broken down → accumulates in lysosomes → cells destroyed" must be explicit — accumulation, not leakage — followed by the Tay-Sachs specifics: a lipid, nerve cells, early childhood death, recessive inheritance. Saying the Golgi makes lysosomes links the two halves of the answer.

Whalen's own prompt: compare and contrast mitosis and meiosis — what each consists of, what each makes, where each happens, why each is done, and whether genetic material is exchanged between homologues.

Model answer

Mitosis is one nuclear division producing two genetically identical diploid (2n, 46) daughter cells; it happens in somatic cells throughout the body; its purpose is growth and repair; homologues move independently and there is no crossing over. Meiosis is two nuclear divisions producing four genetically unique haploid (n, 23) cells — gametes; it happens only in the gonads (ovaries and testes); its purposes are to keep the chromosome number constant across generations (a zygote returns to 46 at fertilisation) and to increase genetic variability; homologues pair in synapsis and exchange corresponding segments by crossing over in prophase I. Within meiosis, meiosis I is the reduction division (homologues separate, 2n to n) and meiosis II is the mitosis-like division (sister chromatids separate, n to n). Both start from a cell with replicated chromosomes and use the same four phases; the difference is that in meiosis I the homologues come to the plate together, as 23 pairs, while in mitosis 46 line up individually.

Full credit needs all five contrasts (divisions, product with ploidy and number, location, purpose, exchange), plus the reduction-division sentence and the metaphase-arrangement difference. Explain the two sources of genetic variability in meiosis, naming the phase in which each occurs and what each one actually shuffles.

Model answer

Crossing over, in prophase I: while homologues are paired gene for gene in synapsis, forming a tetrad, non-sister chromatids (one maternal, one paternal) cross and exchange corresponding segments — the same genes at the same loci, but possibly different alleles — so each chromatid ends up a new combination of maternal and paternal alleles; two of the four resulting chromosomes are recombinant (Figure 19.13). This is why the sister chromatids separated at anaphase II are no longer identical. Independent assortment, at metaphase I: each homologous pair orients on the plate at random, independently of every other pair, so which member of each pair a gamete receives is a coin flip — with 23 pairs, 223 combinations before crossing over is even counted (Figure 19.14). Crossing over shuffles alleles within a chromosome; independent assortment shuffles whole chromosomes between gametes. Random fertilisation adds a third source, but it is not part of meiosis.

Two mechanisms, two phases, correctly paired — Whalen asked which was which more than once. "Corresponding segments between non-sister chromatids" and "each pair independently of every other" are the phrases that show understanding; the within-versus-between sentence is the deeper point. Ch. 20's laws of segregation and independent assortment are these two events renamed.

White blood cells are cultured in nourishing medium, then treated with a drug that destroys the mitotic spindle, so division cannot proceed past metaphase — the chromosomes cannot attach at the plate or be pulled apart. Every chromosome shows as two sister chromatids for two reasons together: the cell was arrested after S phase, so each chromosome had already been replicated, and it was arrested at metaphase, before anaphase separated the sisters — and at metaphase the chromosomes are fully condensed, which is what makes them visible under a light microscope in the first place. The karyotype is read for defects of number (trisomy 21, XO, XXY, XXX, XYY) and of structure (deletions, duplications, translocations such as the chromosome 5 change behind cri du chat). Whalen reads the sex chromosomes first — XY or XX — before looking for anything atypical, and the point of the exercise is to connect an atypical chromosome to what is seen phenotypically. Fetal cells from amniotic fluid can be karyotyped the same way before birth.

Culture, spindle-destroying drug, metaphase arrest, the pairing criteria; both reasons for "two chromatids" (after S, before anaphase); number and structure with an example of each. The spindle-drug mechanism is the same one behind the chemotherapy and "spindle fails to form" questions — say so if asked for a link.

Give the two different reasons an early-source stem cell (embryonic or cord blood) is more useful than an adult one, and name the four potency categories in order with a source for each.

Model answer

Potency and rejection risk — separate reasons that happen to run in the same direction. Potency: cells from earlier in development can become more cell types. Totipotent — all cell types, from the fertilised egg; pluripotent — nearly every cell type, from embryonic stem cells in the blastocyst's inner cell mass at about a week; multipotent — several cell types, from cord blood, the placenta and some adult stem cells; unipotent — one cell type, from some adult stem cells (fat stem cells make only fat, bone and cartilage). Rejection: every cell carries self (MHC) markers the immune system reads, and cells not recognised as self are attacked; the markers develop through prenatal life and early childhood, so cells from an earlier source have immature markers and are less likely to be rejected — the reason cord blood can treat leukaemia when no bone-marrow match exists, and sickle-cell anaemia and thalassaemia have already been treated with it. Two routes get pluripotent cells matched to the patient without the rejection problem at all: therapeutic cloning (somatic cell nuclear transfer, then harvesting the inner cell mass) and induced pluripotent stem cells (an adult skin cell reprogrammed, with no embryo destroyed).

Both axes named and explained separately, the four-rung ladder in order with sources, and the self-marker mechanism stated as a mechanism (markers immature, so not read as foreign). "Why use cord blood" wants the rejection answer; "why use embryonic" wants the potency answer. The therapeutic-cloning sentence covers the study guide's last term.

Describe the three phases of interphase, and explain how a chromosome's structure differs at the start and the end of it — in terms of chromatin, chromatids and count.

Model answer

G1 (first gap): the new daughter cell grows to full size, makes proteins and carries out its ordinary job — where most of your cells are right now; some specialised cells (most neurons) leave the cycle here into G0 and never divide again, while others such as liver cells stop but can resume. S (synthesis): every chromosome's DNA is replicated, semiconservatively, as in Lab 3. G2 (second gap): further growth, duplication of organelles and centrosomes, and proofreading of the copied DNA before division is allowed. Throughout interphase the genetic material is chromatin — long, thin, uncondensed threads of DNA wound on histone spools — because only in that loose form can DNA be replicated and genes read. At the start of interphase each chromosome is one unreplicated DNA molecule with its histones. By the end it is two identical sister chromatids joined at a centromere: still one chromosome, still uncondensed, but with twice the DNA. The chromosome count is unchanged at 46; the chromatid count has gone from 46 to 92. Condensation into visible rods happens only in prophase, when replication and gene activity shut down.

Three phases with one job each in order, the chromatin explanation, then the one-molecule / two-chromatids contrast with "count unchanged, DNA doubled". This is the Figure 19.3 question on p. 397 and Whalen's "interphase is G1 + S + G2" rule. G0 is book- and lab-side only.

Whalen asked for the specific phase of meiosis where the law of segregation actually happens and would not accept "meiosis". Which is it?

Answer: b

Explain why a son cannot inherit an X-linked recessive allele from his father, and why such conditions are more common in males than in females. Name two examples.

A father passes either an X or a Y to each child, never both. To be male, a son must have received his father's Y — so his single X can only have come from his mother, and any X-linked allele he carries is hers. The Y is tiny and carries no counterpart for most X genes, so whatever allele sits on a male's one X is expressed, recessive or not; there is no second copy to mask it. A female has two X's and is affected only if the recessive allele is on both, so with one copy she is an unaffected carrier instead. Examples: red-green colour blindness (Whalen's own, from his mother), haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

Apoptosis is programmed cell death for cells with damaged DNA; mutant p53 no longer triggers it, so damaged cells survive. Contact inhibition stops normal cells dividing once they touch neighbours in a single layer; cancer cells ignore it and pile up. Senescence is the limit of about 50–60 divisions set by telomeres shortening at each replication; cancer cells switch telomerase back on and rebuild the tips — telomerase is present in nearly 90% of tumour biopsies. Cellular adhesion molecules keep cells anchored, and unanchored normal cells self-destruct; cancer cells degrade CAMs and their oncogenes falsely signal that they are still attached. Blood supply: at about a million cells a tumour starves unless it attracts capillaries; it secretes growth factors that make vessels grow in, overriding the normal p53-linked inhibitor, and those vessels become the escape route for metastasis. Immune system: natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells kill cancer cells daily; some cancer cells release factors that inhibit them, others multiply faster than they can be destroyed. Because so many independent safeguards must fail, cancer takes mutations in at least two genes, usually more than six, accumulated over years.

A red blood cell whose cytoplasm is equivalent to 0.9% NaCl is dropped into 3% NaCl. Predict what happens to the cell.

DNA ligase comes last, sealing the Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand into one piece, and RNA polymerase belongs to Activity 3, not to replication. The textbook's §21.2 names only "an enzyme" for the unzipping and credits polymerase with the building; the lab requires all three names, so on the lab exam answer at the lab's level of detail — the Week 3 conflicts table records exactly this gap.

3Lab 3short answer

Explain why one strand at the replication fork is built continuously and the other in fragments. Name every enzyme and structure the lab requires, and say what each does.

Model answer

Helicase unwinds the helix from the origin of replication and breaks the hydrogen bonds, opening a replication fork. The lab's last objective is to explain how a DNA mutation can affect protein structure and function. A single base is substituted in one copy of a gene; a single base is inserted in another. Model answer

Codons are read three bases at a time, in order, from AUG, with no punctuation. A substitution changes one base and therefore at most one codon, so the frame is kept: because the code is degenerate, the new codon may specify the same amino acid (silent — GGC to GGA is still glycine), or a different one, which may or may not change how the protein folds and works; if it creates a stop codon the protein is cut short. An insertion (or deletion) shifts the frame: every codon downstream is regrouped, so every amino acid from that point is different, and a stop codon usually appears early or the true stop is lost — the protein is truncated or garbled and normally non-functional. The difference is frame kept versus frame shifted, and the earlier the shift falls, the more of the protein is wrong. A substitution does no harm when it is silent, or when it lands in an intron that is spliced out before translation.

Full credit: the three-at-a-time reading frame as the mechanism; substitution = one codon, frame kept, with the silent case explained by degeneracy; insertion or deletion = frameshift, everything downstream wrong, worse the earlier it is; and the note that a shifted frame usually produces an early stop. Two genes sit close together on chromosome 7. A student's dihybrid square predicts 25% of each gamete type. What actually happens, and what can rescue the prediction?

Answer: d

Genes on the same chromosome are linked: the whole chromosome moves into a gamete as a unit, so the two alleles that arrived together leave together and the parental combinations dominate. That is the one exception to the law of independent assortment, and it is why the board sheet says a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 dihybrid result is valid only if the two genes are on different chromosomes. The rescue is the bead swap: a crossover falling between the two genes recombines them, and the closer they sit the less often that happens. From your stop-motion photographs, describe the three metaphases you modelled — mitotic metaphase, metaphase I and metaphase II — giving for a human cell how many chromosomes are at the plate, how they are arranged, and what separates in the anaphase that follows each.

Model answer

Mitotic metaphase: 46 replicated chromosomes (92 chromatids) lined up individually in a single row at the equator, spindle fibres attached at each centromere. Anaphase separates sister chromatids — 46 to each pole, and each chromatid is called a chromosome from the moment it is separated — giving two diploid daughters with identical DNA. Metaphase I: 46 chromosomes as 23 homologous pairs side by side, a doubled row, after synapsis and crossing over in prophase I; which member of each pair faces which pole is random (independent assortment). Metaphase II: 23 replicated chromosomes in a single row in each of the two cells, with no replication in the interkinesis before it. In the model the numbers are 2 chromosomes in a row, then 1 pair, then 1 chromosome per cell.

A three-generation pedigree for cystic fibrosis: in generation I an unaffected man and woman have three children — an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. Model answer

Conventions: squares male, circles female, filled = affected, generations on separate lines, a horizontal line joining mates. Start from the homozygous recessive: the affected daughter is cc, so each generation I parent supplied a c — both are Cc carriers, phenotypically normal. Her unaffected siblings are CC or Cc; the chart cannot distinguish them, and "not enough information" is the correct entry. In generation III the unaffected grandson has a cc mother, so he must have received a c from her and a C from his father: he is Cc, a carrier. His father is CC or Cc — again undetermined. Autosomal recessive is shown by an affected child born to two unaffected parents: the trait skipped generation I, which a dominant allele cannot do, because with dominance every affected person has an affected parent and the trait appears in every generation (Marfan syndrome). It is not X-linked recessive because an affected daughter would need an affected X from each parent, which would make her father affected, and he is not; the pattern here fits an autosomal gene carried silently by heterozygotes — about 1 in 22 US Caucasians carries the CF allele.
